# Anticipation

kind: principle

A move needs a move before it. These rules cover when Kei adds an anticipation, how big and how long it is, how it is
built across the body, and how takes and game moves change it.

### ANT1. Plan each action as anticipation, action and reaction
stages: plan, blocking

Split every self-powered action into three parts and time each one on its own. The anticipation comes first, then the
action, then the reaction that finishes it. Give each part just the frames it needs to read, with nothing spare. The
anticipation shows the audience what is about to happen and where to look, so the action never seems to come from
nowhere. It is usually slower than the action it serves.

### ANT2. A move that simply starts looks pulled
stages: blocking, passes, checks

A body powers its own moves, so it gathers and releases energy before each one. Without that gathering, the motion
pops, or it reads as if a string yanked the character from outside, and a fast action may not read at all. Never let
the first changed frame after a hold be the start of the action itself. Scrub the frames just before every start and
confirm that a anticipation is there by choice, not left over from the interpolation.

### ANT4. The anticipation names the action
stages: plan, blocking, self_review

Watch only the frames before an action. A viewer should be able to say what comes next and where it will land. The
anticipation ranges from a shift of attitude in the head up to a full-body wind-up, chosen by the action and the style.
Put it on the part that will act, and keep that part clear of the body so its shape reads.

### ANT5. Freeze each anticipation pose and test it
stages: blocking, checks, self_review

Pause on every anticipation pose alone. If that frame does not point clearly at the action that follows, rebuild the
anticipation; the action is not the fault. A anticipation can also stand without its action: a threat, a feint or a
change of mind is a full wind-up that releases back to rest or into a different move. A frozen, interrupted wind-up
needs a visible cause and a hold long enough for the missing action to be felt.

### ANT6. The eye arrives late, so the anticipation leads it
stages: plan, blocking, passes

A viewer's gaze needs time to travel to a part that starts moving in a still frame, so the anticipation is what brings
it there. Begin the anticipation on the acting part no later than about 5 frames (6 at 30 fps) before any action the
audience must catch. The stronger the anticipation, the faster the action after it may be; if the anticipation is
weak, slow the action. With a strong anticipation and a full follow-through, 1 to 3 frames of action can carry the
move, and a dash from a held anticipation may exit in 1 or 2 frames. Any trail such a fast move leaves takes at least
12 frames to fade out.

### ANT7. Size the anticipation to the action
stages: plan, blocking, self_review

The depth and length of a anticipation tell the audience how fast and how big the action will be. When the two
disagree, belief breaks: a tiny gathering ahead of a burst of force, or a giant one ahead of a feeble result. Such a
mismatch is allowed only as a joke. A body saves energy: a small task gets a small anticipation, and only an effort
near the body's limit earns a full wind-up. Use the smallest anticipation that still makes the action read. A light one
is felt more than seen.

### ANT8. Style and character set the dial
stages: understanding, plan

Broad cartoon work uses the most anticipation and stacks it. Naturalistic feature work sits in the middle. Realistic
work uses the least, but never none: before a forward move, a slight dip and a small drop back are enough. Stylised
work may freeze the whole figure and move a single part; in realistic work the body is never fully frozen. The
character changes the dial too. A confident, in-control character prepares small and slow but still prepares, and
never makes a big hurried wind-up that breaks its calm. Before blocking, record in the plan where the style sits on
this dial and what state the character is in.

### ANT9. Build the anticipation channel by channel
stages: blocking, passes

Never prepare by reversing every translation and rotation away from the target at once and joining it to the action
with a plain in-between. That reads mechanical. Let some channels already head toward the target while others still
pull away: the lower body moves toward the goal while the upper body turns away, or the body translates back while it
rotates forward. A anticipation need not go opposite to the move at all: a step back, a dip, a rise, a small creep
forward, a glance or a lift can each start a lunge. Try several versions and keep the one that looks organic.

### ANT10. Anticipation is contrast in time
stages: plan, blocking

Squash and stretch contrasts shapes; anticipation contrasts moments, a gathering against a release. So a anticipation
does not have to be a squash. When it is one, the squash tips away from the coming jump, and the stretch that follows
lies along the line of flight. Because the body's own muscles start it, the anticipation eases out of the hold before
it; it does not pop.

### ANT11. Store tension with parts that oppose each other
stages: blocking, passes

A strong anticipation can coil parts in opposite directions: the body and head sink to push up while the arms rise to
strike down. A strike reads in four story poses: the anticipation, the swing, the hit and the settle. The wind-up of a
strike is a curl that feeds the swing, never a back-and-forth along one straight line.

### ANT12. Wind up, hold the energy, release fast
stages: plan, blocking

A wind-up builds energy, keeps it for a beat, then lets it go in a snap. Broad comic work can spend a second to a
second and a half on the wind-up and only 1 to 3 frames on the hit it releases; use that pair as a proportion to scale
to the shot. When the same hit comes again, its wind-up shrinks to about a quarter of the first one.

### ANT13. A violent release goes out, over, then back in
stages: blocking, passes

For a sudden, forceful action, go from the anticipation straight to a pose past the final one, then pull back to the
final. A very large force reaches the body on the very frame it acts; the frames after it make the shock readable. For
more shock, push the breakdown the other way instead of using the plain middle, and let small parts such as fingers
reverse on the way back. The timing can run twos, then ones, then twos: a slow build, a sudden change that can drop
frames of motion, and a contained finish.

### ANT14. Leave the anticipation slowly and arrive late
stages: blocking, passes

Spacing out of a anticipation favours the anticipation pose; spacing into the next key favours that key. The favouring
can be extreme. Across the breakdowns the head alone travels and the body and hands barely shift; then the whole
figure changes at once. When a part must turn over, for example a hand flipping, do it near the start of the move. The
fastest frames hide the turn, and the remaining path is free to trace a clean arc.

### ANT15. After a hold, lead with one part on the side of the move
stages: blocking, passes

When a character leaves a still pose, start with one part on the side the move goes to: an arm, a shoulder, a thumb.
It signals the move and draws the eye there. For a reach, the shoulder and collarbone rise a key before the hand. For
an action aimed at a target, the eyes and head settle on the target first and the trunk leans toward it.

### ANT16. The thought is the first layer of the anticipation
stages: plan, blocking

A deliberate action starts in the mind: the eyes search and settle, the attitude changes, the character holds a beat
to read it, then the body winds up, then it acts. The head turns to the target and stays there long enough to read
while the body pauses or shifts slightly toward it, and an action that needs aim may get more than one look. The look
and the gesture it leads never land on the same frame. Shown this way, the thought lets the audience know what the
character will do a moment before it does it. A searching character heads for the next spot before it has finished
with this one. A character that has to think before it moves prepares more than one that reacts. On a character with
no face controls, the look and tilt of the head carry that beat. A reaction never starts before its cause.

### ANT17. Break a symmetric pose unevenly
stages: blocking, passes

When a character leaves a pose where both sides mirror each other, give the two arms different timing and different
directions. The head drags behind during the anticipation and then leads the release.

### ANT18. A pre-anticipation is felt, not seen
stages: blocking, passes

A pre-anticipation is a tiny move against the main anticipation just before it starts. It lasts 1 frame, sometimes 2,
never more than 3. It sits on one part, the part where the eye should go. Use it before a big or fast anticipation, or
when a still or background character starts to move, to add snap. Keep it subtle in realistic work. Stepped through, a
one-frame pop can look like a glitch; at speed it reads as texture. Record it in the plan as a deliberate exception to
smooth motion so a check does not remove it.

### ANT19. Stack one layer at most
stages: blocking, passes, checks

A anticipation may carry one small anticipation of its own: forward, back, then forward. Never stack three layers on a
realistic move; the result is a wiggle with no life in it. Realistic work allows a single slight counter-move and
nothing more. Even when the first move of a shot is itself a anticipation, a small gathering a few frames ahead, about
4, gets the character going. A gentle drift backwards ahead of a launch counts as a moving hold. It is not a
pre-anticipation.

### ANT21. Sell an unexpected weight with a failed try
stages: plan, blocking

When a weight has to look like more than the character bargained for, or heavier than the audience expects, let the
first attempt fail. Key a confident set-up and grab, an anticipation, and an effort in which the load does not move.
Hold while the thought changes. Then give a clearly deeper second anticipation, a bigger jerk, a strained lift that
the plan declares, and an aftermath such as a stagger or a collapse. The second anticipation sits visibly lower and
more folded than the first. A long struggle changes its approach on every attempt, rests or collapses between
attempts, and links its strong extremes with quick, uneven moves.

### ANT22. A surprise breaks an expected action
stages: plan, blocking

A gag works when the audience expects one action and gets another. Prepare the expected action clearly, then deliver a
different one. An object in a gag can give a warning before its event, such as a stack of crates creaking and leaning
before it topples; that warning is a staging choice, not the object's own anticipation.

### ANT24. In a game, anticipation length is a design value
stages: plan, checks

Size each anticipation from its role in play and record it in the plan. An enemy's telegraph lasts longer and uses
larger poses, which gives the player time to defend or step aside: at least about 6 frames at 30 fps for a move the
player must read. On both sides, a move that deals more damage winds up longer, as a trade of risk and reward. A
telegraph in competitive play between players stays small. Across a move set, check that the heavier hits wind up
longer.

### ANT25. Key the extreme of every anticipation
stages: blocking, passes

Lock each anticipation extreme and each overshoot with a key of its own. On a single rigid object, draw back about 14
to 16 percent of the distance the move will cover, eased over about 6 frames; on an object that moves itself, an
overshoot of about 2 to 3 percent is enough. A anticipation plus an overshoot reads as life. A passive object, moved
only by something else, has no anticipation and no overshoot.

### ANT26. Anticipation is its own pass
stages: blocking, passes

Treat the anticipation as a key pose in its own right and give it a pass of its own after the main poses. It sets the
scale of the move that follows. The rig knows nothing about the action to come: name each anticipation and key it on
purpose.

### ANT27. Learn anticipations from a working scene, then audit your own
stages: study, references, self_review

Before blocking, take a finished scene of the same kind and list every anticipation in it: its length, what it points
at, and the smaller ones nested in the bigger ones. Make the same list for the blocking and compare. For a anticipation
in doubt, study a person doing that action at that effort, then try it bigger and smaller. Build several versions and
compare them at speed, and reach a complete playable version early before polishing any part.

### ANT28. Read balance, anticipation and follow-through first
stages: checks, self_review

These three break belief faster than anything else, so every review reads them before any acting detail. The test is
whether a fault drags the eye off the character and onto the motion. Too much anticipation reads as slow motion,
underwater, silly or over-smoothed. Too little reads mechanical and jerky, or as pulled from outside.

### ANT29. A release builds pressure first
stages: blocking, passes

Before a pop, a flick or a throw, the part that lets go swells and draws back as if pressed against something: a thumb
about to flick a coin presses into the fingertip until its pad flattens, then springs. Place the breakdown into that
pressed pose so the viewer sees it for a moment before the release.

### ANT30. A crouch is effort let go
stages: blocking, passes

Going down into a push or a jump, the legs stop holding the body up and it sinks between them. The hips stay part of
the body; never just slide the root straight down. Gravity keeps the body low until the push grows larger than its
weight: a build, then the release. The feet stay locked through the push, and the toes are the last to leave the
ground.
