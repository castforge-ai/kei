# Creatures, effects, cloth and hair

kind: subject

These rules cover animals and invented creatures, wings and flight, the soft parts that trail a body (flesh, cloth,
hair, tails), and the effects a character shot carries, such as splashes, flames, smoke and impact bursts.

### FXC4. A game creature gets its attitude before its move set
stages: brief, plan

Before any moves are listed for an enemy or a boss, write one line on how it threatens and one on how much dignity
it keeps. When a design repels, name exactly what repels and change only that. A creature with no limbs needs other
ways to act, through its body, head or tail, so its emotes, taunts and reactions still read at gameplay distance.
Check the markings on a snake or a long tail for strobing at 30 fps.

### FXC3. An invented creature borrows its timing from a real one
stages: understanding, plan

Pick a real animal whose movement has the quality the invented creature needs, and lend its timing to the design: a
dragon no bigger than a cat can take the quick, darting starts and freezes of a lizard, and a walking hill of stone can
take the slow, deliberate placement of an elephant's feet. The design may correct what looks wrong, yet the true gait
stays underneath. Add one small physical detail that viewers know from their own bodies, such as nostrils flaring on
a breath in or a ribcage that swells and settles, and the creature reads as alive.

### FXC2. Learn the skeleton, then write the order of the parts
stages: understanding, study, plan

Before timing a creature, look at its bones and its joint ranges: where the legs sit under the body, how the pelvis
tilts, how upright the stance is. A hare at rest keeps its long hind feet folded flat beneath it, ready to bolt, while
a bear stands on legs set under it like columns. Then take the timing from film, because still photographs miss the
extremes and how the parts are timed against each other.

For each species, write down which part moves first. A cow rising from the ground pushes its hind end up while it
still kneels on its front knees, then straightens the front legs; a horse does the reverse, front legs first, then a
heave of the hindquarters. However far a design is pushed, the true bones and the true order of movement stay beneath
it; without them every animal moves alike.

### FXC1. Study the species before the first key
stages: references, study, understanding

An animal shot starts with research on that animal. Collect film of it, plus photos shot against a grid, and read
from them how high and how low the hips and the withers travel, how far apart in time the feet land, and how the
muscles change shape. Most four-legged animals share one basic walk. Learn it well on one medium build (horse, dog or
large cat), then adapt it to the new animal by size, weight, design and footfall intervals. Act the move on all
fours. Use the reference to understand the motion; never copy it frame by frame.

Check the result against the reference with a fixed grid or a fixed point: the bob heights and the footfall gaps
should match.

### FXC10. Decide whether a tail is passive or acted
stages: plan, passes

A living creature can move its tail on purpose. For each shot, decide in the plan whether the tail only follows the
body or whether the character drives it. A passive tail takes its motion from the body; an acted tail is keyed from
its root and still carries the trailing delay down to its tip.

### FXC12. Down a chain of hair, tail or cloth, each section lags and swings wider
stages: weight, plan, blocking, passes, checks

In a hanging chain, the top section peaks with its parent or one frame after. Over three sections the tip trails the
top by about 1 to 3 frames. Each lower section swings a little further than the one above it, typically 1.2 to 1.3
times as far, so the tip travels about 1.5 times the top's range. On every frame the chain forms one C, with the end
furthest back. Long ears on a heavy body are such a chain hung from the head: they stretch a little at the lowest
point of the move, drop through that low and rise back to rest, all of it starting 2 or 3 frames after the head.

When the driver stops, each lower section keeps going the old way for one frame more than its parent, then catches up
and passes beyond rest before it settles.

### FXC11. Soft masses run against the body's rise and fall
stages: passes, checks

Every soft or loose mass on a body (flesh, loose cloth, hair) travels the opposite way to the body's bob: down while
the body rises, up while it sinks. Keep the counter-motion small and restrained, and keep it even when the main move
heads another way; in a sneak, for instance, the head presses forward while the hands draw back. On a slow walk it
lags by about half a cycle; on a fast one, by a few frames. The side sway of the hips peaks one or two frames after
the passing position. A garment hanging from the hips swings against them first, then follows them, and hair moves
last of all.

To check, stop on the body's high point and confirm the soft mass is at its low point, then stop on the passing and
confirm the hip mass is still travelling.

### FXC5. A turning four-legged animal leads with the inside foot
stages: blocking, checks

On a curve at a canter or a gallop, make the inner front foot the leading one, with the weight tipped toward it. Use
an outer lead only as a declared choice, for a clumsy or panicking animal. To check, find the last front foot to land
in each stride; it should be the one nearer the centre of the curve.

### FXC7. Wing-beat rate follows size
stages: plan, blocking

Set the beat period from the wingspan. A creature with a span of about 20 cm takes about 2 frames per beat at 24 fps
(about 2.5 at 30 fps); a creature with a span near 1.8 m takes about 12 frames per beat (about 15 at 30 fps); place
other sizes between these two. Down and up strokes take roughly equal time, with a big creature's downstroke a little
longer. A big creature never flutters, and the beat rate alone should tell the viewer its size.

### FXC6. A wing pushes down open and comes back up folded
stages: understanding, blocking, passes

The downstroke delivers the power: the wing spreads as wide as it can, with the feathers closed into one surface. On
the upstroke the wing bends at the wrist and the tip feathers part like slats. Key the wrist first. The body rides
against the wings: it lifts while they press down, peaks near the end of the downstroke, and sinks on the upstroke.
A body that bobs in step with its wings is wrong.

In level flight the beat tilts a little forward going down and back going up. That tilt grows when rising, landing
and hovering. A hover stands the body almost upright while the wing tips trace circles. The tips fan out from the
wrist and trail behind wherever the wrist just came from, so they flip direction one or two frames after each turn
of the wrist.

### FXC9. The outer wing trails the inner wing
stages: blocking, passes, checks

On a large wing, the hand section moves about 2 frames after the arm section, and it folds on the upstroke. A
membrane is pulled tight on the downstroke and goes slack on the upstroke. When it opens, it opens from the elbow
outward, and the tips straighten last. Strong climbing beats overshoot at the tips. Near the ground, solve the wing
tip clearance in the keys so no beat touches or crosses the floor.

### FXC8. A large winged creature flies in a fixed order
stages: plan, blocking

For a large four-legged creature with wings, plan the flight as a sequence. Take-off: a crouch over the front legs,
then a vault where all four limbs push and the front legs lead (about 0.2 s), then a rise like a thrown body while the
wings open. The first downstroke comes near the top of that rise, and about three heavy beats bring it to a steady
climbing angle. Then cruise, glide down, and land.

To land, the body pitches upright, the wings swing forward to brake, and the legs reach. Two or three braking beats
follow. The hind feet touch first. Next the torso rolls forward over its feet; meanwhile the wings travel forward and
downward, rise once more, and are the last thing to fold. A cruise beat of about one per second (24 frames), with roughly 57 % of the beat spent on the
downstroke, has read well at this size.

### FXC13. Effects serve the acting and follow the style
stages: plan, passes, self_review

A character shot carries only the effects and debris it needs; in a game most effects are built in the engine, so
the animation marks where and when they fire. On a story frame, the face, the eyes and the main pose of the acting
must stay readable through any effect; play the effect down where it would hide them. Its look follows the style chosen for the project. The same fire can
be drawn in very different designs.

### FXC19. An impact effect fires on the contact and fades slowly
stages: blocking, passes, checks

The first frame of an impact or burst effect is the frame of the hit or the accent. Full size comes within roughly 3
frames (4 frames at 30 fps). The fading tail then lasts longer and moves slower than the burst did. Small effects (a
gunshot, a splat, impact lines, dust from a slam) finish in roughly 5 frames, or 6 at 30 fps. Compose a large blast
from these units instead of new timings: its main burst fills the screen within the usual burst time; a flicker of
about two burst lengths follows it; a flash about one small effect long comes before it as a warning; and smoke closes
the event over several seconds. A bigger event grows by adding further bursts; it never slows its first burst down.

The eye should arrive at the spot just before the burst, and the burst should be gone almost before it is seen. Note
the effect's first frame and length in the plan, measured from the contact frame.

### FXC22. A speed trail stays where it formed and dies fast
stages: plan, passes, checks

Speed and impact come first from spacing and anticipation; a well spaced, well prepared fast move reads with no trail
at all. When a move is too fast for two clean frames, one stretched frame along the path does the job. A trail, a
smear streak or a speed line is an engine effect, such as a world-space trail or ribbon emitter or the engine's motion
blur, declared in the plan and never keyed on the rig. It stays behind in world space and never travels with the
object, which would make it read as threads tied to it. It shrinks from its old end, may drift slightly against the
travel, and fades in about 3 frames (4 at 30 fps); a spectacular exit may stretch it to about 16 frames (20 at 30).
Motion blur works only over short distances, since a long blur loses the volume. A zip-off still prepares the other
way, speeds up and then exits.

To check, hide the effect: the move must still read from its anticipation and spacing, or the spacing is fixed first.
With the effect shown, track the trail's world position each frame (still, or drifting slightly backward) and its
size (falling to zero), and count its life in frames.

### FXC20. Parts of an effect leave a few at a time
stages: passes, checks

Nothing disappears all at once. Drops at the edge of a splash, sparks, debris and particles drop out a few per frame
over several frames. Each particle follows its own path under gravity. A count of elements removed per frame should
show a spread, never a spike.

### FXC16. A splash goes from mass to sheets to drops
stages: blocking, passes, checks

Water hitting something breaks up in stages: a mass first, then sheets, then single drops. Each drop flies on its own
parabola. Each drop is drawn long in the direction it moves, and the drops on their way down are the longest. A deep
entry adds a second event, a jet that rises from the centre. Time the water against its size: slowed down it reads
thick and viscous, and hurried it loses its mass. Fit each drop's path to a parabola and compare its stretch with its
speed.

### FXC18. Build a flash from alternating frames, within safe limits
stages: passes, checks

Lightning or a strong flash is made of single frames that alternate light and dark. In a game, keep any flashing
inside the platform's photosensitivity limits, since rapid alternation can harm some viewers.

### FXC14. A rising effect slows as it climbs and never sinks
stages: passes, checks

Flames and smoke move fastest at their base and slow as they rise. A tongue of flame or a puff that stalls, or seems
to move downward, ruins the effect. The time to climb grows with size: a few frames in a small flame, several
seconds in a large fire. A surge comes in fast and dies away slowly. Fire and gas may change volume between frames;
this is a declared exception to keeping volume constant.

Check by tracking a few marks on the effect and reading their height each frame: they must only climb, and their gaps
shrink toward the top.

### FXC17. Small flames sway; large flames break apart
stages: passes

A small flame keeps its base still and moves gently back and forth with a slight bob. A large flame makes a busy
pattern of shapes and curls that change violently, and pieces break off, rise and fade. In both, the base holds and
the top carries the motion.

### FXC15. A looping effect hides its repeat
stages: passes, self_review

Smoke and other repeating effects use long cycles, 32 frames or more, and the gaps between tracked marks close up to
about half toward the top. A repeated puff needs at least two different shapes so the eye cannot find the loop.
Layered effects show depth through speed: the near layer crosses the screen faster than the far one.

### FXC21. Play only rigid motion in reverse
stages: passes

A rigid object can run backwards and still look right. Anything loose cannot: a load riding a moving part separates
on the frame after its carrier turns, and trailing parts and effects lag in one direction of time only. Reverse a
section only when nothing loose or trailing is in it.
