# The passes and the working method

kind: principle

How Kei moves a shot from the brief to delivery: the order of the stages and passes, what the plan and the X-sheet
hold, how a stepped blocking becomes splined motion, how polish is ordered, and the standing rules for judging work.

### WFL1. Every shot runs the whole pipeline, and every stage leaves proof
stages: all

The stages run in a fixed order on every shot, whatever its size or format: brief, rig, weight, understanding,
references, study, plan, blocking, passes, checks, self-review, delivery. Each stage writes something a person can
open in the shot folder: a character sheet, reference notes, the plan with its pose sheet and arc drawing, blocking
sheets, gate notes. A stage is either done or not started; it is never left waiting for later. The pose sheet exists
before blocking starts, and blocking exists before any later pass.

However good the motion looks, a shot with a missing stage is not ready. A shot whose planning was rebuilt after the
animation says so on every report.

### WFL42. Work from the rule book, and grow it
stages: brief, plan, self_review

Open the rule book before any animation task and build the plan against it. Wherever a choice depends on a rule, the
plan and the notes cite that rule by its ID. Record corrections locally first. Promote a lesson into a shared rule book only after checking its source, your right to publish it, and its confidentiality.

### WFL41. Never write over accepted work
stages: plan, passes, deliver

Start every shot from a saved start scene that holds the character, the delivery and review cameras, the rest pose
and the export settings. Each saved version carries a one-line note of what changed. Save a new version before any
large change; old versions are the undo, never leftover layers or hidden keys. New work always goes into a new
version, so an accepted result or an earlier round stays exactly as it was.

### WFL11. The passes run in one order, and none is skipped
stages: plan, blocking, passes, checks

The order: reference and timing chart; thumbnail poses; blocking, stepped, root first then head and limbs; the
breakdown pass, still stepped; spline; polish; the performance pass; the review through the delivery camera; export.
Within the passes, keys come first, then the extremes, then breakdowns, then one run per part in order of importance
for the shot, with hair, cloth and other loose parts last. Each layer adds arcs: a small flourish waits for the
refining passes and stays out of the first blocking.

A pass shown for notes is called close to final, never final. No spline and no polish start before the blocking is
approved.

A cinematic starts from a reel that is a rough 3D previz, and nothing is locked while the performance can still change.
Once the performance exists, four gated steps follow in this order: the final cameras are placed; a pass sets, beat by
beat, what each look is aimed at; polish sets every contact on the final models; and only then is the timing locked.
Rank the scenes by importance and give the most polish to those ranked highest.

### WFL12. Gate every pass, and fix a fault in the pass that found it
stages: blocking, passes, checks, self_review

Each pass ends with a gate, and the next pass waits until it passes. Working in passes exists so that problems show
early, when they are cheap to change; the blocking gate matters most. Whatever a gate finds is repaired inside that pass,
not carried on: a wrong bounce left in stepped keys only gets harder to see and fix after the spline.

Polish cannot rescue weak blocking. It starts only when the mechanics work, the weight sits right and the poses are
good; on a solidly blocked part it only tidies the curves. Any change bigger than a tidy is a blocking fault: save the
current version first, send it back through its gate, and log it as missed there if it was visible at that gate.
Before a final version, the blocking and breakdown gates run again.

### WFL13. Show a lean first pass early
stages: blocking, checks

Keep the first stepped pass down to the keys that define the action, so it can be shown when about a tenth to a fifth
of the shot's time budget is spent. A wrong direction found then costs little; found after days of breakdowns, it
costs the shot. The person who signs off the shot sees the rough pass before detail piles up.

### WFL2. Collect the inputs before the first pass
stages: brief, understanding, plan

Before anything is posed, the brief must give: the action and why the character does it; the character's build,
weight, age, mood and energy; the style (realistic or broad, realistic when unstated) and how far it is pushed; how
the motion is used (standalone, player, or non-player character); the frame rate; the length, or leave to choose it;
and for a cycle or any travel, the travel speed. When one is missing, ask. Never invent a want or a mood to fill the
gap.

Write the weight as a load set against the character's strength: a tired or weak body reads heavier at the same mass.
Note the body facts that change a pose, such as proportions, footwear and stance width. These numbers sit on the plan
and blocking reads them; it never guesses them.

### WFL3. A text request is animated from principles, not traced
stages: brief, plan

When the request is words only, there is nothing to fit frame by frame: the motion is built from the principles, the
plan and studied references. Fitting a body to a video frame by frame belongs only to a separate, reference-driven
workflow. In prompt work, review the key-pose silhouettes before any motion is built, and judge the result with the
same overlays and checks as any other shot.

### WFL4. Study a reference, then animate a version of your own
stages: study, plan

Go through the reference clip one frame at a time and describe it in fresh terms: the timing, the contacts, the key
silhouettes, the arcs, where the weight goes, what reads as attitude. Animate from that description and the brief,
never from the clip itself. Then compare renders with the clip at matching moments (contact, passing, peak) and
name each gap as a principle.

Split what the study gives into two lists. Take the principles. Leave behind what belongs to the clip alone: its
choreography, its performer and build, its props, its exact poses and positions. Approved shots and their review notes join the references for the next
shot.

When the clip shows action on a part the rig does not have, move that action into the nearest parts it does have.
Pick a take by what it adds and by what it will cost to animate. For a mechanics shot, keep the acting plain so the
mechanics stay visible.

### WFL5. Plan the shot before touching the rig
stages: plan, blocking

Act the shot out against a stopwatch and write down the frame of every event: each step, look and grab. Draw many
quick, simple thumbnails before choosing, then fix the keys and extremes with their frame numbers, and the
breakdowns with theirs too. A line across the thumbnails at hip height helps read the weight. Blocking then only
transfers the plan into the rig. Thumbnails, key silhouettes and the plan sheet are reviewed before any motion is
made.

Treating a brief as a build job, with keys typed straight in at their final interpolation, skips every place a weak
pose could be caught. When work comes out muddy, generic, mechanically weak or evenly timed, suspect the plan before
the keys. Starting at the computer with no plan is working blind.

### WFL9. Choose the technique per section, on the plan
stages: plan, blocking

Pose to pose suits acted moments. Layering suits physics and travel: animate the driving part alone first, stepped on
twos with its spacing chart, then add each further layer (height, travel, rotation, squash) and show each stepped.
Straight ahead suits loose parts after the body is done, and a whole-body section only when it follows a close,
fitted reference. Write the chosen technique and its reason on the X-sheet, and also how each section will later be
polished. The pass order stays the same whatever technique a section uses.

A straight-ahead section is declared on the plan. Each key in it is a whole-body pose, copied forward a frame or two
and then changed, never one part at a time; its timing comes later, in a stepped pass of its own. Fix its contacts and
layout marks first: pose each planted contact ahead of time, then build the keys that lead into it.

### WFL7. The X-sheet gives every key and breakdown a frame
stages: plan, blocking

Before the rig is touched, the timing chart names the frame of each key and each breakdown, never only the keys, and
the key each breakdown favours. Place the hits first, on whole frames, and time the wind-up, the main move and the
response as three separate spans. Before blocking, check the plan's numbers for faults: a hold too short to read, a gap
too long for its move, a step labelled long that the numbers make shorter than the short step. A pushed moment is
planned as a phrase with three parts: the approach, the hold, the exit. Number the sheet on odd frames on twos, place
the story poses on those numbers, and mark a continuous action as one span.

Choose the step by section speed: about fours for a gentle ease, threes for slow parts and the ease into a final
pose, twos for medium moves, ones for the fastest frames. As a guide, a normal walking step takes about 12 frames
and a tired one about 24. Typical pose density is about four poses for a broad move of ten frames, one every five
frames for a slow move, and a pose on every frame for a scramble. Read the finished sheet for rhythm, texture and
readability before blocking.

Test alternative durations between the planned poses. For each version, record the intended action and ask an independent viewer what they understood. Choose the version that communicates the intended action, and keep the test and its measurements as the basis for this shot's timing.

### WFL8. A spacing chart per move also sets the order of work
stages: plan, blocking, passes

For each key-to-key move, draw a chart: the keys circled, the breakdown marked differently, the ticks spread wide where
the move is fast and packed where it cushions, the sections on ones and on twos noted. The chart is also the work
order: keys first, then breakdowns, then the in-between that matters most, never straight from left to right. Every
frame's place comes from the planned timing, never from a guess made while posing.

### WFL10. A shot of several parts is timed as a reel first
stages: plan, blocking, checks

When a piece spans two or more shots played in sequence, cut a reel of one stepped story pose or thumbnail per beat of
each shot, at the planned lengths, and review it before any shot is blocked. The reel gate is passed when each of these
written checks holds, with the reel played at full speed:
- beside every beat, one line says what that beat adds that no earlier beat has shown, and no line is empty;
- every beat names the beat it grows out of and why it follows from it;
- every beat carries an energy mark, and the marks never sag or run flat before the climax;
- each beat's played length and content are compared with its line in the plan, and any difference is written down;
- every point a viewer new to the piece would have to ask about is listed, and the list ends empty.

When the idea changes, edit the reel, or whichever plan layer owns that idea, and run the gates again; it is never
patched into splined motion. Each shot then begins from the length the reel gives it and from its linking poses: one
shot ends on the pose the next one opens with, and the direction of travel and of gaze carries over the cut. Later
passes replace their stepped poses in the reel and are judged beside their neighbours. A walking beat that ends at a
cut or at the end of a clip ends on the down after its last contact plus one to three frames of rise, never on the
frame the foot first touches.

The strongest moments get built before anything else, and the stretches that lead into them are fitted afterwards. A
beat whose only content is getting the character somewhere either jumps straight to the arrival or shrinks to one
strong image, and a beat that has made its point hands over to the next one at once. Mark each beat as action or calm
and mix the two through the piece so that it breathes. Before the reel is cut, the piece's subject fits in two
sentences at most. Across a cut, an identical world speed may seem quicker or slower once the character fills more or
less of the frame, so try a doubtful section at two or three speeds and pick by eye. A cinematic adds its own passes
and gates, listed in WFL11.

### WFL32. One line of intent, and one ranked notes list, run through every pass
stages: plan, passes, self_review

Before blocking, list the shot's emotional beats, a line apiece: what the character feels, what sets it off, what it
realises, how the feeling changes, and what it does. Before polish, read the one-line intent again; each note is
fixed in whatever way best supports that intent; polish brings no new ideas, and each note is traced to the fundamental it lacks. A new acting note may still be
layered on in polish only when it is declared, built on single axes, and its wobble traced and fixed.

Keep one dated, ranked notes list for the shot from blocking to polish, so the most important fixes survive a deadline.
A weak spot found late is fixed at its own pass level when time allows; otherwise it is logged as a known issue.

### WFL35. Mechanics and performance are two judgements
stages: plan, passes, self_review

A shot can be mechanically sound and still read flat and generic. Rate mechanics and performance separately and let
neither excuse the other. After the mechanics work, run a performance pass with its own X-sheet: settle who the
character is and add moments no other character would have, such as a reason for each action, a held
decision before committing, looks that lead, accents and holds. Push the key poses toward that attitude: extend
fully where the move permits, open the arcs of the arms, make the line of action clear. Change poses and amplitudes
but not timing that works, and keep every hard check. This is a layer of character, never noise on top of the mechanics.

Push the key poses once already before the breakdowns, and again after notes. Correct and planned is not finished.

### WFL21. Look for the extra position
stages: blocking, passes

Ahead of a downward antic, try going up a little first while the body's angle shifts. After an accent, try one
momentary pose past it in the opposite shape, such as a squash after a stretch. After a big key, put the overshoot and
any left and right offsets in the breakdown that follows. Late breakdowns sit between the main masses; only the parts
that settle travel beyond their end pose. An extra position stays when it brings change and goes when it only
decorates. A showy arrival may wind its antic through one continuous curl that still cushions into the end pose while
the body leans against it.

### WFL22. Breakdowns by eye first, and re-read the shot after them
stages: passes, self_review

Pose the breakdowns by looking at renders; the curve editor comes later. Overshoots wait until the transitions work. At
that point, write down whatever reads badly (a pose, the length of a hold) and fix it ahead of the first spline
pass. Keep
one look for the whole shot, cartoony or realistic, and use contrast inside it, a slow action placed beside a fast
one.

### WFL27. Leave to the curve only true halves
stages: blocking, passes

Let interpolation fill only plain halves. Everything else gets a key: thirds, favours, eccentric joint actions
(the peel of a foot, the turn of a knuckle, a wrist that leads), every contact, every lead and follow, and every frame
of a very fast action, while the curves stay smooth. Before splining, settle the route between each pair of keys with a breakdown:
straight interpolation gives tent shapes at constant speed, and flat tangents everywhere make the motion stop at each
key.

### WFL23. Leave stepped only when the blocking is locked tight
stages: blocking, passes

Splines spread every loose gap of the blocking across the in-betweens, so tighten the blocking before the switch.
Key every control that moves the character on every pose frame and at both ends of every hold, and leave no gap longer
than about three or four frames. Give every arc enough keys or breakdowns that straight lines joining them already
trace its curve. As a guide to the density that takes: points a quarter or an eighth of a turn apart still read as
corners, while points every 20 to 25 deg or so, about sixteen around a full circle, already pass for round. A
tightening pass stays stepped and changes timing only by adding or removing whole frames between keys.

Save the last stepped version and its render. Write its fault list (rig faults, arc faults, planned detail layers)
as the first notes of the spline pass. Timing, holds and graphic devices such as smears are decided in blocking; the
spline only softens them, turning stepped holds into slowly moving holds and easing the arrivals.

### WFL25. Convert everything at once, then read every curve
stages: passes

Convert every control in one go, save a new version, and treat the result as a preview. Go through the first render
one section at a time, noting every fault and its cure before any edit; listing every pop comes first.
The conversion does about half the work. Some stepped timing may be let go where it fights the motion, but story
poses, beats and contacts change only after a return to their gate.

### WFL26. Own every tangent
stages: passes, deliver

Left to itself, the software fills every gap with smooth, slow, floaty in-betweens. Every key's tangents are chosen:
flat at an extreme, tilted to keep flow, broken at a hard contact, linear only into a corner chosen on purpose,
stepped only during blocking. A smoothing mode that moves handles by itself when a key moves is not trusted, and
nothing overshoots unless it was put there. A key added during polish starts smooth so it cannot pop as a step.
Per-frame values are produced only when exporting, as a read-out of the curves; sampling is never a way of animating.

### WFL34. Keep the shot editable at every pass
stages: passes, checks

Each control holds few, clean keys, so any pose or beat can still change late by moving a handful of them. Clean a
curve against a faded copy of the previous pass behind it, as a guide to intent rather than a target: as few keys as
keep the shape, frequently a single key at the extreme, tangents flattened then unified, the arc shaped by the handles.
Remove chatter by removing keys, never by adding them or running a filter. A curve that needs many keys points to a
planning fault upstream. Any pass that ends with dense keys has failed.

A flat shelf that no pose asked for is usually a stray key, left behind when some other control was keyed: delete
it and tilt the neighbouring handle so the curve flows. To rebuild a messy stretch, key every channel on a trusted
frame at each end, pose one breakdown between them, delete every key in between, and only then shape the curves.

### WFL28. Refine in a fixed order: blocking, breakdowns, spline, polish
stages: passes

After blocking, Kei refines the shot in three passes, always in this order: breakdowns, then spline, then polish. Each
pass writes a new folder and never overwrites an earlier one, so every pass can be compared with the one before.
Breakdowns add the poses between the keys, each one a whole pose, while the shot stays stepped. Spline turns the
stepped poses into curves; every tangent is chosen (WFL26), and the curves are read before the motion is judged.
Polish fixes what still reads wrong, working down the shot's ranked notes. Each pass ends with its own gate, and the
next pass starts only after that gate passes. If a pass shows that the plan was wrong, update the plan sheet and write
down what changed and why.

### WFL30. Every part moves on its own before polish, and polish goes in chunks
stages: passes, checks

Before polish, check every control for motion of its own: hips, feet, knees, shoulders, head. If some part has never been
animated, the shot returns to the previous pass. A short shot is polished over its whole length; a long one in chunks of about 30 to 40
frames, each finished before the next. In each chunk check timing, arcs, pops, how connected parts follow each other,
any missing axis, then details. Budget polish time per part.

### WFL29. Polish from the root outward
stages: passes

Start with the root, because every part inherits it: translation first (height, then side to side, then forward),
then rotation. Work on the root with the limbs hidden, delete in-betweens the blocking left on the height curve, keep
about one key per extreme and flatten tops and bottoms into a plateau. The hip curves are the cleanest in the shot:
each stretch flows into the next and none goes fully flat, since a stalled hip leaves the trunk and the legs lifeless
as well. Then the second hip control, each foot, the torso from spine to chest, then the head, then shoulders and arms,
then the settle, and the knee pops last.

Before playing the root, read each of its curves and list the suspects by frame: a rotation that zig-zags, a
translation with a stair or a cluster of keys, an arc missing its top. Then confirm each one in the viewport.
Parents come before children. After any change to the body, check the legs and feet again. To change a tangent's
weight, drag it without changing its angle.

### WFL33. Find a fault by stepping every frame; fix it at the nearest key
stages: blocking, passes, checks, self_review

Step through every frame, never only from key to key, because a fault can hide in an in-between the software made.
Decide the result first, then touch only the controls that result needs. Fix it by moving or reshaping the closest
existing key or breakdown and its tangents, by a small amount, or by adding a single breakdown. Never fix it with a
solved key on the failing frame or a run of keys on consecutive frames. If a fix needs two or more added breakdowns in
a single step, the pose itself is wrong: go back to the blocking gate. After every fix, check the curves for
smoothness again. Keys on many frames are acceptable only while the curve stays smooth.

Allow at most three rounds of looking and fixing per gate or pass. A hard failure still open after that is reported,
not buried: stop and hand it back with the frames and images concerned, a record of the attempts, and one clear
question.

### WFL31. Final polish is a short ranked list of fixes on a working shot
stages: passes, self_review

Final polish starts only when timing is close and the weight is right; a shot needing large changes to timing or poses
is not in polish. Every polish session opens with fresh notes from a looping render, written before any change, and
a re-read of the intent line. Fix weight and mechanics first, then overlap and squash, then final touches. The closing
stretch of polish keeps to a fixed list, in this order: how each foot rolls; the pelvis rocking as the support leg
straightens; knee pops; which way knees and elbows point; parts passing through each other at contacts; and squash and
stretch. Nothing outside that list is reopened unless it is broken.

Spend detail where the audience looks, centre of interest first, resting parts last and least, and only once the
blocking reads at a glance. Before investing in a detail, watch the shot at full speed in the delivery camera; if
the detail cannot be seen there, drop it.

### WFL36. Look at every frame after every pass, before reading any number
stages: passes, checks, self_review

After each pass, render every frame for each camera as contact sheets, with onion trails, one dot per frame and a
looping preview; from the breakdown pass on, show the previous pass in grey behind. Look in this order: the poses
frame by frame in each camera, the arcs and trails, the spacing from the dots, any cycle across its loop point,
playback at speed, then the comparison. Write one note per problem, giving the camera, the frame range, what is seen
and what will change, with at least one note per camera. Fix, and only then read the measurements. Polish must never
add a pop.

### WFL39. Review the twelve principles at the end of every pass
stages: passes, checks, self_review

Every pass, still pose and plan ends with a principles table, one row per principle: timing, squash and
stretch, anticipation, staging, arcs, easing in and out, overlap with follow-through, secondary action, the choice
between straight ahead and pose to pose, exaggeration, solid drawing, appeal. Each row says how the principle shows in
this shot, what was looked at, and whether it was fixed; a row marked not applicable gives its reason. For a still
pose, timing means the pose's place in its action. Close with four written judgements: whether the movement itself is
good, whether the weight reads, whether the timing is right, and whether a viewer would care what happens to the
character.

### WFL40. Each pass is delivered with its evidence
stages: passes, deliver

Draw the path and the spacing ticks before animating. With each pass, deliver a motion-trail image, a table of each
key with its frame, position and gap to the next, and a short self-review against the brief's criteria. A 3D version
comes only after the 2D passes hold.
