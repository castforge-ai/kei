# The blocking method

kind: principle

Blocking turns a written plan into stepped poses on the rig. These rules cover what must exist before the first key,
the kinds of pose and the order they are made in, the breakdown rounds, the retime of blocking plus, and the two gates
that a shot passes before any spline.

### BLK1. Know what the object is before planning its motion
stages: brief, understanding, plan, self_review

Before choosing, planning or reviewing a motion, read from the rig what the thing is. A passive prop and a living
creature move for different reasons. A prop is physical and obeys forces: it bounces passively, each bounce smaller
than the last. A creature acts with intent, even when it happens to be round: each hop is a choice, each landing soaks
up the weight and becomes the wind-up for the next push, its heights and timings are chosen, and its stop is a
decision. A creature animated with a prop recipe fails, even when the recipe passed on a prop: decaying bounce heights
and a slide to rest do not make a living thing.

Before reusing work that passed, confirm that the new thing belongs to the same family as the old one. Code, keys or
a motion approved for one kind of object are never reused for another kind; only the method transfers. Read the
limits of the brief (length, camera, style, what is forbidden) and stay inside them.

### BLK3. Building on approved work keeps what was approved
stages: plan, blocking, checks

An approved base is approved as a whole. Before changing it, list the properties it passed. After each declared
change, test every property on that list again, not only the one the change was aimed at.

### BLK4. Plan in beats, then give each beat its poses
stages: plan

Split the action into beats before thinking about poses: usually a start, an anticipation, the action, a recovery or
reaction and a final state. A beat is not a pose and may need several: give each beat one pose first and add another
only when the idea fails to read. On the plan sheet, give every beat a kind (hold, action, anticipation, cycle) and a
length in frames; together the lengths fill the clip exactly, and a beat can only get longer if a neighbour gives up
the same number of frames. Mark every frame an event must hit, such as a contact, an impact or a gameplay frame.
Before the first key, the whole sequence of beats can be told aloud, in order. An action described as a change between
two states (calm to panic, for example) is easier to block than one described as a list of moves.

### BLK5. Four ranks of drawing
stages: plan, blocking

A key is a pose that tells the story of this shot. Keys are as few as can carry the story, marked apart on the plan,
and they hold the screen longest. Build and time them before any other rank, and give them the closest attention.
Play the keys alone and stepped: if the story does not read from them, nothing later will fix it. An extreme is a pose the motion needs that tells no story by itself: a reversal of direction, a touch or contact,
an antic. A breakdown lies between two keys or extremes and chooses the route from one to the other. All remaining
frames are in-betweens. Nothing in a walk cycle is a story beat, so its contacts and passings take the key rank; once a
walk sits inside a shot, the shot's story beats take that rank and the walk's poses become extremes and breakdowns.

### BLK6. Give every key a function before posing it
stages: plan, blocking

Each key on the plan carries a name from a fixed set: moving hold, antic or crouch, push-off, passing, up, apex,
contact, down or absorb, extreme, hit, settle, hold. Acting adds start, think, decide, held expression, inhale, look
away, sink and touch. Each key also names what it reacts to. Every action, a mishap included, finishes on a settle
pose that was designed rather than left over. The function list fixes the order of the poses and shows a missing one
at a glance.

### BLK7. Thumbnail more than one version
stages: plan, study

Act and time the action until the same version repeats, then sketch the first idea and at least two others, avoiding
the obvious one. Use simple shapes drawn in the proportions of the rig rather than those of the performer, numbered on a ground line with arrows
and a word for the accent. On each sketch draw the line of action first, then the hip line and centre line, and write
the order in which the parts move. For a travelling mass draw its whole path as one line with the contacts and
spacing marked. Keep a list of the rejected ideas; one may come back.

A planning sketch shows only the line of force, the masses and where they touch. For an effort such as a push, a pull
or a lift, draw several force versions and then choose. Line weight is never decoration: thicken the stroke where a
mass is squeezed against something, and on the edge that leads the move, switching that edge on the first frame the
direction reverses.

### BLK8. The pose that carries the point gets four options
stages: plan, study

For the single key that tells the point of the shot, draw at least four versions that change the gesture, the use of
a prop or the direction of the body. Drop a version that adds action for its own sake, one that stays tight inside the
body, and one whose gesture crosses the body and closes its negative shapes. Prefer the open, leaning, prop-using
versions, and write why the chosen one won. Also sketch two or more breakdown options for each main pair of keys and
choose by the character.

### BLK9. Solve hard poses before blocking starts
stages: plan, blocking

A hand grip, an extreme angle, a contact or a strong twist is solved in a sketch or a test pose on the rig before the
shot is blocked. Blocking is the transfer of decisions, not the place where they are discovered. Compare competing
versions as motion, never as stills. Before keying a move, say it as an ordered list of controls and channels, such
as the hips back and up, then their twist, then the arm. A move that cannot be said that way is not ready to key.

### BLK11. Check that the plan fits before the first key
stages: plan, checks

Run five checks in order and write the answers on the plan. Room: each planned move stays inside the set and inside
the frame of the delivery camera, leaving space above the highest pose and below the lowest and room ahead of
where the character looks or travels, with no frame edge cutting through a joint, and every contact lands on the ground or on a prop at
true scale. Fit: the readable poses, each given its read time (at least 6 frames at 24 fps), add up to no more than
the shot length; otherwise cut moves or stage closer. Solved: no hard pose is left open. Full size: rough the main
keys on the rig and look at them through the shot camera, to see whether they relate as the sketches promised. Another eye: the plan is shown to the person
who signs off the shot, or, when no one is available, a critic reviews it and the human review stays owed.

A failure returns the shot to whichever step it failed.

### BLK12. Set up the scene once, before the first key
stages: rig, plan, blocking

Fix the layout first: the axis the action travels on and the centre line, placed at the most intricate part of the
action and never keyed. Show a ground plane in every render. Scale the set and the props to the character, never the
character to the set; a seat sits at about the character's knee height. Write the delivery camera's angle, height and
lens, lock it, and judge every pose through it with the controls hidden. Key a neutral pose on every control before
the first frame of the shot. Declare root motion or in place before any key; a loop is in place unless the author asked for root motion.

### BLK13. Write the rig inventory and probe every sign
stages: rig, plan, blocking, checks

List the controls blocking will key: the base system of each limb (IK or FK), one method for aiming knees and elbows
for the whole shot, and how the head follows the body. Leave advanced and sweetening controls out of the list. Put
every IK/FK switch and space change on the timing sheet with its frame. Before keying any rotation channel, key a
small positive and a small negative value and read which way the joint moves; write the sign down.

A key's comment, a sign table or a diagnosis handed over by someone else is only a claim. A foot keyed with the sign
its comment calls toes down can fold the foot up against the shin far past what an ankle allows, and a finger channel
that a table says curls can spread the finger instead. When a probe and a written claim disagree, the probe wins. Look
at a probe from two sides of the joint, because a sideways spread can read as a curl from one angle. Read which
channels carry which parts before compensating one part through another.

### BLK15. Choose the blocking order from the kind of shot
stages: plan, blocking

A cycle, or any shot built from repeated pose types, is blocked by pose type, one type over the whole shot before the
next: every contact first, then every passing, the downs, the ups and finally the rotations. A single action is blocked
in time order, one story key after another; each breakdown waits until both its neighbours exist, and the copy of the final hold comes last. A
travelling prop is built in channel layers (height, travel, then shape), each layer shown stepped. An acting shot is
blocked pose to pose, attitude poses first. Write the method chosen for each section and why; straight ahead is used
only in a section declared for it.

A walk is planned from its contacts by default. A walk invented for a character, rather than
taken from life, may instead be planned from its two low poses and the pose that passes between them, because that
settles the bob and the weight before anything else; then rework the contacts and check each one for a straight leg
and a planted heel. When the walk is about what the pelvis does, give the pelvis its own keys at its
highest and lowest positions.

### BLK16. Start from the root and work down the hierarchy
stages: blocking

Block the root on its own first, and stay with it until the rhythm of the full action works. Then go down the chain: pelvis, spine, head,
shoulders, upper arms, forearms, hands, each after its parent. For a body that travels on its legs, work from the
ground up: footfalls and track width, the hip's rise and fall, the pelvis rotations, the trunk, then the feet and the
arms. After any change to the hips or the spine, scrub the legs again in the side view.

### BLK17. Key every control on every pose, stepped
stages: blocking

Every pose sets a key on every control in the inventory, and the pass is reviewed stepped. The pops that stepped keys
produce are expected and are not faults. Give the hands one relaxed shape for all of blocking and never leave
any control at its default value. On a new frame, key all controls first, then pose. A blocking delivery shows the
whole body on its keys, extremes and breakdowns, arms and hands included, and leaves out fingers, hair and other
detail.

### BLK18. The first pose is the foundation
stages: blocking, weight

Build the first establishing pose with the most care and derive the rest from it. Weight belongs in the very first key.
Never leave a stance or a held load at the rig default to fix later. Place the feet first, then choose the
stance width and the energy of the posture. A pose very different from the last is rebuilt from zeroed values rather
than bent out of the previous one. Decide where the weight sits on each support foot and pose upward from it: weight
toward the big toe straightens the knee, deepens the hollow of the lower back and tips the pelvis forward, while weight
on the outer edge softens the knee and flattens the back.

### BLK19. Pose in a fixed order and check from more than one view
stages: blocking

Inside each full-body key, pose the centre of gravity, then the spine, hips, neck and head, then the hands (IK when
they rest or push), the shoulders, the feet and the elbows. Pose in a free perspective view, check it from two further
angles, and judge it through the shot camera, moving between the views until the full pose works. Before moving to the next
pose, check this one from the front and from the side.

### BLK20. Pose anatomically, with one control per job
stages: blocking

No limb or spine is stretched past its length. Use the fewest controls and the same control for the same job on every
pose. Spread a spine bend across all the spine controls instead of loading one. Rotations read zero at a vertical or
neutral pose. When a control's old values fight a new pose, zero that control and pose it again. Turn a joint along
its own axes, never by a free tumble, and keep the curves in view while blocking so a channel that jumps up and down
is fixed at once. Move the knee or elbow aim together with the foot or hand it serves.

### BLK21. Copies are exact where the body returns, and varied everywhere else
stages: blocking

A planted foot, or any part that returns to where it was before, copies its earlier key exactly. The opening
hold is a copy of the first moving pose and is copied again after every change to it. A copied story pose is never
used unchanged: vary the shoulders, the head and the neck, make a mirror by zeroing the rotations and posing again, and
turn a planted foot a few degrees off straight. To build three poses quickly, make the outer two from one copy, adapt
the later one, then pose the middle. A travelling gait can be roughed by copying a contact forward and moving only the
root and both feet, judged in the shot camera; two strides of contacts are enough for a first look.

### BLK22. No part moves alone
stages: blocking, weight

A key never moves only an end control. The body action that drives the part is keyed with it: bending a knee also tucks
the pelvis and changes the lower back on that very key, a push includes its answer in the spine, and a look moves the head, neck,
shoulders and arms together. A breath uses several controls. Every key states the force behind its move: what pushes
on what, and why at this moment.

### BLK23. Dirty is allowed, unclear is not
stages: blocking, checks

Stop working a pose once its shape and its story read. Rough poses are shown early so the big notes arrive while
changes are cheap. A pose may be rough; it may not be unreadable, and it may not sit at a default.

### BLK24. The pose list of a physical action
stages: blocking

For an action driven by weight and balance, the first stepped pass holds every contact, every take-off, the pose
furthest out of balance and the pose where balance returns. Feet stay flat in steps; passings and rise and fall poses
wait. Add a breakdown only when the story demands one. A landing gets its contact, an overshoot squash
that holds for about four or five frames to gather, then the rise. Hold copies reserve the time of beats not yet
animated.

### BLK25. Keys first, then extremes, then breakdowns, then parts
stages: blocking

Make the few story keys and test them alone, stepped. Add every extreme the motion needs: contacts, touches, antics,
changes of direction. Then add the breakdowns. A long action is never spanned by two keys and left to interpolation.
After that, work one part at a time, in the order of importance this shot gives them: the body first, next
whichever part carries the idea (the look, the hands, the prop), and the loose parts at the end. A key changed by a later part goes back through the
blocking gate. Do not polish a part to the end while its partner is still missing. Get the spine to a sound state,
add the arms, then return to the spine, since adding arms alters what the spine has to do.

### BLK26. A contact begins as a touch
stages: blocking

Key each hand or foot contact first as a touch: the heel down with the body still behind it and no weight on it yet,
the fingers on the object and open, before they close or pull. The weight, the grip and the bend arrive on the
following keys. A self-propelled figure lands on an extended leg before it squashes, and leaves the ground on an
extended leg after the body has already risen; a passive object that only rebounds gets no separate take-off frame.

Before any self-powered contact, whether the floor, the top of a box or a load to be gripped, give one pose in which
the body clearly gets ready for it: legs stretching toward the ground, hands opening into place. That pose is often the
most important picture of the move. At the top of a flight, where the push and gravity even out, the brief pause is
used only to adjust along the path. In blocking, a contact is met by placing keys; a constraint for a brief contact is
added only after the poses are approved.

### BLK27. Solve the technical problems inside blocking
stages: rig, blocking

Decide each limb's base system from what it will do: IK on planted contacts, FK from the frame a limb leaves the
ground on brief contacts and on swings. Make every IK/FK or space switch on a single frame with the rig's match tool,
with the hips travelling across it and the pose equal on both sides. Preview a large rotation once with splines to
see which way it turns, then return to stepped. A part that spins fast, such as a rope whirled overhead or a crank
turned by hand, may be keyed about 180 deg apart on each pose so the spline carries the spin; it is refined later.

### BLK28. Acting shots block attitudes first
stages: plan, blocking

Block the poses of each phrase before any mechanical key. Before a deliberate act, give the thought its beat: the
eyes and head rest on the target for long enough to be seen. Hold each phrase pose through its phrase and work small changes
inside it; an accent is a small move of the head or a hand, never a new pose. A change of head attitude gets its own
key before a broad move or at its end, never in the middle of it. A busy playblast is cleared by deleting poses until
the point reads.

### BLK29. Put lead and follow into the stepped keys
stages: blocking

Pose the spine upward from its base, every section trailing the one beneath it and the head last, so no two parts turn on the same
key. At a reversal the root reverses first and the head follows one pose later. The upper arm leads while the forearm
and hand drag; the thigh leads while the knee and shin drag. Every anticipation is a real key that stretches the
driving muscle, sized to the move; frozen, it should name the action to come. An antic is left out only by declaring
an outside force.

### BLK30. Build eases and settles into the poses
stages: blocking

Easing, antics, overshoots and settles are posed, not left to the interpolator. Only true holds are held. A pose held
for about 8 to 16 frames at 24 fps becomes two keys: the pose and a stronger drift in the same direction, so it
arrives softly. After a hard accent, the pose to hold is the recoil, not the impact.

### BLK31. Push the key poses before the breakdowns
stages: blocking

Once the story keys work, run an exaggeration pass on them. Build the pushed element at three levels side by side in
every delivery camera: the plain pose, a push of about double, and a dialled-back version; choose by eye at speed. A
push makes the plain pose more of what it already is, in the same direction; compare channel by channel and refuse a
sign change on a main channel unless it is declared. Plan the way out of a pushed pose at the same time, because a
pushed pose on a weak support limits the keys that follow. In an emotional beat, push only the part that tells the
emotion, never the whole body.

### BLK32. Rough timing first, then the timing revision
stages: blocking

Set the story keys at an even spacing as placeholders, for example one every four frames, and pose them first. Then set the
length from the target seconds, inside the limits of the brief. A count taken from reference is only a first guess:
find where it is slow and where it is fast, and make that contrast larger. Move whole poses, or a pose and everything after it, and play
the result stepped at the real rate. As the last blocking step, mark the slow sections on the first stepped playblast,
cut them, and add acceleration where the action builds up speed, timed for the character rather than the performer.

### BLK33. Story poses read, transitions pass
stages: blocking, passes, checks

The first timing is a rough estimate. On the stepped playblast at real rate, each storytelling pose stays on screen
long enough to read and each transitional pose is brief. When a story pose is lost or flashes by, take frames from the
transitional pose beside it, so the shot length stays exactly as it was. The shift is often large. For example, a
4-frame story pose may grow to 9 while the 8-frame transition beside it drops to 3, and the pair still spans 12
frames. Lengthen just the part that has to feel slower, and make the large change first, then pull it back. Poses
come first, timing second. Keep a table of the hold lengths. Key on odd frames and keep the gaps even; then each
breakdown can sit on a whole frame. Retiming is done when the gaps are wide enough to place the breakdowns.

### BLK34. A pose-only review is not a timing review
stages: blocking, checks

Story poses may be laid on consecutive frames to review the poses alone, but that playback is never judged for
timing. The blocking that is delivered for the gate is timed.

### BLK35. Hunt for holes after every retime
stages: blocking, checks

After any retime, first watch the stepped pass at its new timing. Then step from key to key in both directions,
in each delivery camera. Look for a key that kept the attitude of the key before it (a lean, an angle), which forces the next key to do
the whole change and makes a pop. Repair it on that same key, making it a real in-between of the keys on either side:
start at the root with the rotation of the body's centre, work up the spine one control at a time, and change only
what helps. Never add keys to hide it. A support leg
keyed a little less straight than the frames around it makes a knee bump on that key: compare the reach of every
support key with its neighbours and pose it in line.

### BLK36. Judge timing on a real-rate playblast
stages: blocking, passes, checks, self_review

Viewport playback drops and holds frames, so it lies about timing. Judge timing only on a rendered playblast at the
shot's frame rate, and render again after every major change. Show each pass to answer one question: does each beat
read as intended? A gesture that only its author reads correctly is swapped for one that most viewers read the same
way. Judge timing first, then poses, and fix both while still stepped.

To sense how a stepped pass flows, or to check that a large rotation turns the correct way round, the stepped keys
may be splined for a moment and rendered for viewing only. Judge that render for its one question (the timing, whether
the holds hold, the direction of the turn), then return the keys to stepped. Nothing is posed or fixed on the temporary
curve, and it is never kept.

### BLK37. The blocking gate is passed on stepped poses alone
stages: blocking, checks, self_review

Before any breakdown pass, spline or overlap, the stepped poses and their timing must pass on their own: the story and
the idea read; the pose functions suit the action and come in the right order; air time matches height, contacts
squash, and the beats avoid an even metronome; every key balances; the planned arcs are drawn and the keys sit on
them; a prop decays while a creature acts with purpose. The weight should be roughly four fifths of the way there. A
fault found here is fixed here by moving a key or changing a pose, never carried forward for the spline to hide.

### BLK38. The gate reads clarity and density
stages: blocking, checks, self_review

Someone who has not seen the plan must get the core intention from the fewest poses, with every beat present. Each
key, frozen, shows where it came from and where it is heading. No key or breakdown is empty of attitude; no pose
exists only to travel. In the fastest frames the head and its attitude still read at a small size, and a subtle beat
still has distinct keys. Record whether the beat lands the first time it is watched at full speed.

### BLK39. Read every key as a black shape
stages: blocking, checks

Play the block as filled silhouettes through the shot camera, the ground hidden. Each pose must read without its
face. The acting hand or prop breaks the outline, the chest is open to the camera unless hiding is the idea, and the
gaps between arm and body point toward the centre of interest. A reaction shows as a reversal of the silhouette.

### BLK40. The key pose test
stages: blocking, checks

A key pose reads alone as a small silhouette in the delivery camera, tells its moment with no caption and no
neighbour, is the extreme of the action and not a point on the way, sits on one committed line of action, and lets
the head lead the emotion. A pose that is not an extreme is really a breakdown. Keep it in that role, name the two
extremes on either side of it, and pose whichever of them tells the moment. The pose shows its own cause (a stumble shows the caught foot). It
sits just before the outcome lands; the outcome is the next key. A key out of balance must be declared as a fall.

### BLK41. No shortcut survives the gate
stages: blocking, checks

A copy or mirror taken to see the idea quickly, such as one step pasted down a whole run without the feet
alternating, is repaired at key level before the gate can be passed. Fixing shortcuts is key work, never breakdown work.

### BLK42. The stepped blocking is the reference for everything after
stages: blocking, passes, checks, self_review

The passed stepped blocking, played at its planned frames and real rate, is the pose test of the shot. Save it under a
clear name with its playblast before any conversion, and keep it renderable. Every later pass is compared with it at
full speed. When refinement blurs the idea, for example by burying it under overlap, return to the blocking rather than
adding more on top; going back is a valid fix. A cycle's blocking is played about ten times in a row before it passes.

Judge a spline pass with three versions side by side: the stepped blocking, the raw conversion and the refined curve.
The refined curve must hold the blocked poses and timing while stripping out any easing the conversion added.

### BLK43. Place breakdowns as choices in time and space
stages: blocking, passes

A breakdown usually sits halfway in time and favours one of its keys in space. Its frame is also a choice: a third of
the way, forty per cent, or early to carry momentum. Write its frame and its favour on the timing sheet. Start from
the value that favour gives (the first key plus the favour fraction of the difference to the second) and then pose on
top of it. Never accept the computed middle as it comes: left alone it is linear and in the wrong place. A short gap of
evenly spaced frames still gets a written favour on its middle breakdown.

A useful way to place a favour: set the two keys to straight-line interpolation, key the whole character at an early
point of that move, slide that key to the breakdown frame, set back to stepped, then pose it. Typical results put
about 15 to 40 percent of the travel at half the time. A calm, understated move can split shape from position: the
breakdown copies the whole shape of one key and is placed either about 35 to 40 percent of the way through the move or
about 60 to 65 percent. Its form therefore switches early or late in the move rather than blending evenly.

### BLK44. Inside one breakdown, parts favour different keys
stages: blocking

The leading parts take their values from the next key while the body still favours the previous one, and the head
trails a step behind, moving against the body. A support holds: a support foot stays copied forward until the body is
about halfway up, pushing hands stay down as long as they can, and straight pushing arms come from lifting the hips.
So the parts already travel at different speeds before any offset exists.

### BLK45. Breakdowns go in rounds, largest gap first
stages: blocking

Add one breakdown per remaining gap, starting with the largest gap or the one where most changes, and only where the
computed in-between is wrong. After each round, render a stepped playblast, judge timing and then poses, and retime by
sliding whole keys, never by editing tangents. Repeat until every gap is decided. After each breakdown, look at the gap into the
next key: a big jump there is solved by posing that key again, never by adding one.

### BLK46. Every breakdown is physically possible
stages: blocking, passes, checks

Check each transition the blocking jumps across: could a body really get there? No breakdown passes a limb through the
body or a prop, and the body stays supported where it rests. Where a transition lacks mechanics, as with a stand
straight up out of a seat, the breakdown adds what was skipped: the lean over the feet, a foot that slides, the
compression of an antic before the push, an arm leading on a curve. After moving or offsetting any key, check every
limb again; where a limb would pass through the body, add one lifting breakdown that favours the start. Read the
stepped root curves after each round: no value steps backward without a cause.

### BLK47. A breakdown says how, never what
stages: plan, blocking, passes

A breakdown serves only the move between its two keys. If a fresh thought or attitude turns up while placing one, it
becomes a key on the plan or it is dropped. A breakdown placed dead in the middle of two keys reads mechanical, with
everything moving at once, so each one carries a named variation: a delay, an advance, a lift, a drop, a one-sided
favour, a squash, a stretch, a tilt, a drag, a lead, an arc, an overlap, an expression. Sketch at least two options
between the same keys (simple, broad, stylish) and choose for the character. The first breakdown of an action carries
its idea; later ones refine it.

When the audience could not tell how the character reaches the next key without it, the breakdown belongs among the
storytelling poses, not a later addition. Between two strong poses the transition is quick, never a chain of flailing
in-betweens. A passive settle may take a plain in-between.

### BLK48. Force lives in the breakdowns
stages: weight, plan, blocking, passes

Two story keys alone carry no force. For an action against weight, write between each pair of story keys which part
leads and the order in which the parts move, before posing the breakdown, and label the breakdown with its leading
part and its favour. The leading part is visibly ahead and the following parts behind; a centred in-between shows no
effort and is not allowed there. Coming out of an antic into a large extreme, let the breakdown favour the antic: the
first gap small and the second large, so the move bursts. Judge a weight action first by that order of parts, then by
its timing and arcs.

### BLK49. The breakdown pass has three goals and a density
stages: blocking, passes, checks

Breakdowns are added while the keys are still stepped. The pass fills every gap with a chosen pose, defines each
part's path of action (at least the hips and the head are traced), and sets the spacing through the favours. Look at
the reference and the thumbnail for that beat before each breakdown. Settle the hips first; arm detail may stay rough.
Draw new breakdowns in a second colour against the keys so they can be told apart.

The pass is done when the main keys, every needed breakdown, the anticipations and the end settle are in, the movement
is broken up rather than moving as one block, the physics works where weight lands, and a stepped playback confirms
the timing. At its end no gap of moving motion is longer than about four frames, about three in a broad shot. Allowed
exceptions are holds, sections left for a later pass and an even move that was checked, each written down. A position
that is not a plain half, such as a heel that peels before the toe, is keyed rather than left to the curve. The pass
then closes with the retime of BLK50.

### BLK50. Blocking plus ends with a retime
stages: blocking

Play the breakdown pass and cut frames wherever a move floats or hesitates. Scale a section that is only too slow with
all its keys together, then snap every key to a whole frame. When a gap gains or loses frames, do it on the whole
character and return the same number of frames later so sync holds. If the shot is still too long, remove a beat
that repeats. Log each change in a retime table with the old frame, the new frame and the reason, and run the hole hunt after it.

### BLK51. The breakdown gate, then sign-off
stages: blocking, checks, self_review

Every breakdown sits on its arc with no tent and no flat shelf. The trails of the root, the head, the hands and any
tips read as clean paths. The spacing favours the right key and the order of action shows. Every breakdown balances
as a key does. The pass is still stepped. A person then signs off the blocking, and no spline starts before that. A
fault that belongs to a key returns to the blocking gate rather than moving forward.

### BLK53. Record the extent of the approved blocking
stages: blocking, passes, checks, deliver

When the blocking is signed off, write an extent record next to the plan: how big the character is in the delivery
camera, its top and bottom points, its furthest points left and right both on screen and in the world, each contact
with the set or a prop with its frame, body part and place, the opening and closing poses, and the path of the root. For a game clip add the root motion extent, the
clearance and the entry and exit poses. Every later pass is compared with this record. Any change is declared, and a
moved prop sends every pose that touches it back for a check. Anything added later, such as cloth, effects or set
dressing, must not hide the main action.

### BLK54. The spline inherits the blocking and adds nothing new
stages: blocking, passes

Splines start only after sign-off. Before converting, key every absorb pose of each landing, and list every shape
channel (squash, compression, a bend, a curl) with the frame of its cause so none of them starts early. Secure the ground
contacts before working on the hips. The pose frames never move. Nothing may appear that blocking lacked: no overshoot
that was never posed, no reversal that has no cause. A fault that is really a pose or a timing fault goes back to its gate.

### BLK56. In a skip each foot lands twice in a row
stages: plan, blocking

Give a skip an uneven rhythm on each side: the grounded step lasts about twice as long as the hop that comes out of
it, and in every loop at least one hop is made longer or shorter than the others, because even hops tick like a
metronome. Each time the feet change, the lean of the body moves to the other side.

The footwork behind that rhythm: a foot lands, pushes the body into a small hop and comes down again on that same
foot. About 2 frames after this second landing the other foot takes over, so the footprints come two by two.

A gentle skipping walk is blocked like a walk: contacts first, then the arms on a beat that does not match the legs,
then the downs, which give about three bounces per stride. The arms never reach an extreme on every contact.

### BLK57. A sideways start leads with the near foot
stages: plan, blocking

To start a walk sideways, move the foot on the side of travel first. A far foot that crosses in front to lead reads
as a mistake. The weight always leaves a foot before that foot lifts. If the start needs a wind-up, a short step back
is allowed, made by the foot that clears the path of the leading one.

### BLK58. A limited part is answered above it
stages: blocking, checks

Review a walk part by part from the ground up: the give of the foot, the strike of the heel, the push off the toes, the knee bend,
the side shift of the pelvis, its turn, its tilt with the counter-tilt above it, then the forward and side bend of the
trunk. A part that cannot do its share, because of a rigid boot or a sore ankle, hands its work to the joints above
it. Pose the main
ones in blocking and leave the rest, with their timing, to refinement.
