# Exaggeration, appeal and style

kind: principle

How far Kei pushes a pose or a beat, how it keeps a character appealing, and how the style of a project changes both.
Realistic and stylised work use the same method at different settings.

### EXG1. Name the essence and its cue before pushing anything
stages: understanding, plan, blocking

Before any push, write the essence of the beat in one or two words: tired, furious, proud. Then find the cue an
audience uses to read it, such as the slump that says tired or the sudden snap that says angry. That cue is what gets
pushed past life. Everything else in the pose is simplified so it does not compete. A bigger stimulus earns a bigger
response, so the push on the cue also sizes the reaction that follows it.

Having room to exaggerate is not a reason to do it. Push the element that carries the essence, and leave the others
plain.

### EXG2. Push from the plain version, in the direction it already leans
stages: blocking, passes, checks

Start with the straight, believable version of the action. Exaggerate one thing only: the trait that sets this
character or moment apart, sent further along the line the straight version already follows. The aim is a clearer
reading of the essence, never a change of shape for its own sake. A push never reverses the plain direction unless the
plan declares that reversal.

Make one pass over the whole event that overshoots the level the shot is expected to keep, then reduce only the parts
that stop being believable. To check, compare the plain and the pushed pass key by key: each main channel's change
from rest should keep its sign and grow in size. A flipped sign is a place to look. A push has gone too far when it
loses the viewer: the joke no longer reads, the trait blurs, or the pushed part steals the spotlight. A push that is
too small loses the viewer as well, so check in both directions.

### EXG3. Few poses, each taken to its extreme
stages: plan, blocking, passes

Broad, stylised work uses a small number of poses and drives each one hard. The low pose is truly low, such as a deep
lunge that brings the head near standing hip height. The high pose is truly tall, up on the toes with the active arm
fully raised. As a typical target, the pelvis travels 0.25 m or more between its lowest and highest key. Any change
worth making is made large enough to see on the contact sheets; a timid adjustment wastes a pass.

### EXG4. Rough extreme to extreme, then go back and push
stages: plan, blocking

Block the extremes in the order the action plays in the mind, without lingering on minor moments, so the first pass
keeps the energy of the idea. Once the run of extremes exists, return and push them further. In the first pose test,
give each thinking or acting beat slightly more frames than planned, then trim the stalls after seeing it. Cutting
excess is easy; rebuilding a beat that was squeezed too short is not. Size each small action by what it shows: once it
has said its piece, extra frames only slow the shot.

### EXG5. Set each character's exaggeration level from its role
stages: brief, understanding, plan

Write a level for every character before blocking: straight, light or broad. The role and the story's premise decide
it. A hero or a sympathetic lead usually sits near straight and is carried by staging, clear planning and sincerity,
not by broad acting. Comic and supporting roles take more caricature. Characters of the same role type in one shot
share one level.

For an animal or a creature, start from its true anatomy and layer the caricature over it, shaped by how people
picture that animal, so that ears, tail, neck and spine can all act. An animal built only for anatomical accuracy
tends to act too little; give its expressive parts room to perform.

### EXG6. One exaggeration level per project, scaled by screen size
stages: plan, blocking, self_review

Hold one push level across every clip of a set and write it in the plan. Poses go a little past the reference and hold
slightly longer than life. The smaller the character appears on screen, the further the push: a distant or overhead
camera needs larger-than-life actions, and only a close camera can carry subtle ones. Results the viewer must
register, such as the visible consequence of a heavy blow, are shown plainly rather than hinted.

A wide camera distorts shapes toward the edges of the frame, so a game character's proportions are usually designed
for that camera, often stockier and chunkier than a real person's. Judge every clip at the real camera distance and
field of view: it should read in a single viewing and sit at the same loudness as its siblings.

### EXG7. Hit reactions err toward exaggeration and follow the blow
stages: plan, blocking, checks

A damage reaction is scaled to the attack and pushed past realism so it reads from the playing distance. Each set
needs variants by direction and by body part. The first motion of the root and chest goes away from the attack, never
across or against it; a reaction that ignores the attack direction looks broken at once.

### EXG8. Contrast in time grows with the style
stages: plan, blocking

The broader the style, the larger the gap between slow anticipation and fast action. Cartoon work may spend one to
sixteen seconds on an anticipation or a hold, then deliver the action in two to ten frames, then hold the result.
Typically a broad build-up or hold lasts about 3 to 10 times as long as the action it releases; treat that as a guide,
not a limit. Realistic work keeps the same pattern at a much smaller ratio.

### EXG9. Quiet bodies for inner moments, big gestures for outer ones
stages: plan, blocking, passes

A thought, a realisation or a camera push-in keeps the body calm so the face and the idea lead. Large gestures belong
to moments aimed outward, at another character or at the world. Quiet is not frozen: a body holding a load or a
feeling keeps small living motion. Between exaggerated beats, keep a few realistic beats as anchors so the audience
can measure how far the others go.

### EXG10. A plain setup, a big payoff, then a still beat
stages: plan, blocking

When an action repeats, the first attempt is plain and easy to read, and the second goes far beyond it. Once the
peak has happened, hold a still moment so the payoff has time to land. A shot can push every element and still read, as long
as every pushed element points at the same single action.

### EXG11. One strong contrast for each key moment
stages: plan, blocking, self_review

Reduce each key moment to one clear opposition, for example stillness beside speed, a large shape beside a small one,
or an open pose beside a closed one. Remove anything that competes with it. The centre of interest in the shot carries
the strongest of these contrasts. Write what the audience should feel at the key moment and measure each pass by that
sentence, not by the amount of keying done. Several pleasures meet in the key moment together: a clean read of the
staging, the essence pushed, a line with appeal.

### EXG12. A change of state pushes both states
stages: plan, blocking

When a character switches from one state to another, exaggerate both sides: the expansive showman and the exhausted
drop that follows, not only the loud half. Make the switch quick so the contrast reads. Push the essence beat as far
as it goes in pose and in timing, and check it in silhouette. A performer who embodies the essence, studied for poses
from a described reference, is a good model for how far to go.

### EXG13. In stylised work the rig is a shape, but physics still decides
stages: blocking, passes, checks

Broad, stylised work may treat the rig as a design to bend into appealing shapes rather than as a literal human body.
A pose may also be cheated toward the camera so it reads better. Balance, support and the physical rules of weight
still win any conflict with a shape.

Some moves belong only to the stylised setting. Lifting the shoulders on a turn, for example, can suit a cartoon
design, but it is never a default for realistic work. A broad settle may also carry a secondary layer once the body
has come to rest, provided the settle runs 12 frames or more: an offset, overlapping motion in the arms, keyed on
every frame, in which the two arms circle against each other, one setting off later than the other, with the joints
bent against their natural direction.

### EXG14. Why stylised motion reads
stages: study, plan, self_review

Stylised work succeeds through a handful of tools: strong contrast, clean silhouettes, economy of poses, graphic
devices, and pose choices tied to who the character is. Graphic devices such as smears are declared as a specific
effect for a specific frame. Spacing remains the default way to show speed.

A shake on a part that is also moving is a small offset layered on top of the smooth move. The offset starts at zero
in the opening pose, flips to the other side of the path every frame or every two frames, swells, then shrinks back to
zero in the closing pose. Because the move underneath stays smooth, the shake grows out of the pose and dies back into
it; it is never random noise per frame.

### EXG15. Subdue a stylised character beside a realistic one
stages: plan, passes, self_review

A keyed or stylised character sharing a shot or a game with live footage or motion capture tends to look flat, loud
and weightless beside it, however carefully it is timed. Bring its broad actions down to the partner's register.
Match the partner's tempo, stride, perspective, light and texture, and plan the space together so there is room for
both. Judge weight side by side. A useful measure: during the same action, set the keyed character's highest root
and hand speeds and accelerations next to the realistic partner's.

### EXG16. Appeal is a separate review question
stages: self_review, deliver

Appeal is whatever makes a viewer want to keep watching the character: it reads at once, its shapes and poses are easy
on the eye, and it holds attention. A character can be unpleasant or frightening and still have it, so villains need
it as much as heroes; the failure is a character the viewer stops watching. Only simple, direct attitudes read well.
Ask about appeal on its own, on every key and on the full playback, and give it the first look. The person who signs
off the shot judges it in the end.

### EXG17. Animate inside the character's design
stages: understanding, blocking, passes

The design sets the shapes a pose may use. Soft, rounded masses with their weight low read as friendly and at ease.
Sharp corners and angles read as edgier or more refined, and they demand more of the viewer, so use them on purpose. A
pose that breaks the designed shapes or proportions breaks the appeal with them.

How menacing a villain is, and how much dignity it keeps, are chosen settings. When a creature puts viewers off, find
the single trait responsible and change that alone; the rest of the design stays.

### EXG18. Never invent a personality silently
stages: brief, understanding

Read the character notes in the brief: who the character is and the attitude of this move. If the brief does not
say, ask before blocking. Warmth and similar qualities cannot be applied like an effect. They come from the reason
the character acts for someone else, and that reason belongs in the brief.

### EXG19. Review the flow first, then each key alone
stages: checks, self_review

Watch the shot at full speed to see whether it communicates while moving. Then step frame by frame to find the key
poses, and confirm each one reads clearly on its own. The frames between keys serve the flow and need not read alone.
A separate appeal pass still looks for any frame that is plainly unattractive.

### EXG20. Use live reference as a guide, not a master
stages: references, study, blocking

Hold on to the timing and the staging found in the footage. Select what serves the point, push it, and rebuild the
pose in the character's own shapes. Step through real footage frame by frame: it usually deforms and overshoots more
than a first guess would. Transfer footage one to one only for rigid or mechanical objects. With an animal, understand
its real movement first and caricature second.

### EXG21. Keep only what a real person would do, and aim for a reaction
stages: passes, self_review

For each overlap or flourish, ask whether a person would really do it in this situation; if not, cut it. A pass works
when a viewer responds with the reading the plan intended, and fails when the response is silence. Ask a fresh viewer
what happened and what the character felt, and if the first answer is wrong, simplify. A weak beat may improve by
removing moves until one small, clear gesture is left. When a large change would make the shot better, make it, even
when it costs time.
