# Personality and the shot with a character

kind: subject

A character shot, an idle breaker above all, is won by who the character is, not by how well the body moves. These
rules turn a character into a performance: what it wants, what stands in the way, what it perceives, how that shows in
posture, timing and small tells, and how a short loop stays alive without a word. They add to the head and acting
topic; they do not replace its rules on looks, breath and phrasing.

The rules follow the order a shot needs them: the want, the personality, the status, the tells, the holds, the idle
loop, the habits to avoid, and the checks. Numbers in this topic are provisional starting points. Judge each by eye.

### PRS1. Write what the character wants before posing
stages: brief, plan

Every beat answers two questions: what does the character want right now, and what does it do to get it. Write both
in PLAN.md as plain words before the first key. Play the action, never the bare emotion. When a choice is stuck
(a pose, a timing, a camera angle), go back to what the character thinks and wants; it settles most of them.

### PRS2. An obstacle makes a beat
stages: plan, blocking

A want with nothing in the way gives no beat. Break the action into small, specific steps and name what resists
each one (does it see the thing, is it its own, does it test first). A setup followed by an outcome that differs
from it is a beat; an expectation that fails is the clearest one. Only an action that expresses a setup or an
outcome can be played.

### PRS3. Find the inner cause, then build the body from it
stages: plan, blocking

Settle why the character does the thing, then derive the body result. Make it true for the character first and
readable to a stranger second. A move with no inner cause is a mannerism stuck on: remove it.

### PRS4. Write the back story the viewer never sees
stages: brief, plan

What happened before the shot shapes how the character moves in it: it carries earlier effort, worry or pride into
the first frame. Write two lines of it in PLAN.md and let the first pose and the speed of the first move come from
them. Know where the shot sits in its scene, too: the same reaction fails in the wrong place.

### PRS5. Give a silent character an inner line
stages: plan

A character with no speech still thinks. Write its thoughts as short lines in order, and time the beats to them:
where it notices, searches, decides, gives up. An idle breaker with no inner line is a wiggle; with one it is a
person who just remembered something.

### PRS6. Personality is perception, then need, then action
stages: plan, blocking

Three things make a personality: what the character perceives (a filter), which need is strongest, and how that
need becomes action. In the loop these are three nodes: perception, thought with motivation (needs and thought are
one node), and action. Personality sits in the middle of all three; it is not a fourth step. Show the chain in
order: it perceives, it thinks, it acts, and the action changes what it perceives next. Perception and thought are
inside the character; action is the only visible part, so make the action show them. Never let it react to
something before it is perceived. Reacting early looks fake because the character already knows the future.

### PRS7. Make the personality clear at once
stages: plan, blocking

The viewer should know who this is early: by the first frame for a shot of about ten seconds, by the first minute of
a short. Put the clearest personality signal in the first pose and the first move, not in the middle.

### PRS8. Fix a centre of energy, a default pose, a tempo and a rhythm
stages: plan, blocking

Give the character four things that stay the same in every shot: the part of the body that leads (chest, chin, hips,
hands), a default pose it returns to when nothing is happening, a tempo (fast or slow in general) and a rhythm
(even, jerky, flowing). Take them from a real person, an animal or an object, and keep an animal's motion true to
its species. Two characters in one scene differ most clearly in tempo and in the size of their moves.

### PRS9. Derive each trait, do not paste it on
stages: plan

Ask what a trait actually is and let the behaviour follow. Shyness is an aversion to being seen, so the character
shrinks, looks away and starts to leave. Do this for every trait on the list, and build the list in order: inner
traits first, then the visible ones, then only as many as the shot's time allows. Let the story choose the traits.

### PRS10. One specific twist beats the stock trait
stages: plan, blocking

The stock version of a trait (the nervous character who wrings its hands) tells the viewer nothing new. Keep the
trait and add one specific twist that only this character has. Be efficient: one decision should say two things at
once, such as an eye line that shows both focus and habit.

### PRS11. Tie one small tell to an inner trait
stages: plan, blocking

A tell is a small habit that leaks the trait, like a poker player's tell. Pick one, tie it to the inner cause, and
trigger it at efforts: a sniff, a sigh, a gasp, an inhale, the end of a move. A tell that comes from nowhere is noise.

### PRS12. Give the character a contradiction
stages: plan

A single trait is a type. Two that pull against each other (tough and gentle, proud and unsure) make a person. Show
the strength of a trait by setting it against a big reason to drop it and letting it win, with some ups and downs
before it does.

### PRS13. Stay consistent, and change only for the story
stages: plan, passes

Check every beat against the default pose, tempo and rhythm. A character changes personality only when the story
says so, and then it shows another side of the same person, not a new one. A move that is out of character pulls the
viewer out of the shot.

### PRS14. Status is a want, and it moves
stages: plan, blocking

Decide each character's status (high or low) and how it changes during the shot as it gets or loses what it wants.
High status does less: it stays still, keeps a calm face and answers slowly. A low-status character that plays high
status is strong personality. A character that leans in intrudes; one that draws back is smaller or afraid. Height
and space carry status: standing and tall is power, equals share a level.

### PRS15. Under-gesture
stages: blocking, passes

Fewer gestures, smaller and more sincere. Pick the one moment that stands out and let everything else stay quiet
beside it. Check the four goals on every shot: clear, honest, sincere, simple. Do not tell the viewer what to feel;
show the want and the action and let the feeling arrive.

### PRS16. The same words need a different body for a different meaning
stages: plan, blocking

A small change in posture, one head move or one look changes the whole meaning of a beat. Plan the subtext at the
start of the shot with five questions in two groups. The character: what kind of person it is, what mood it is in,
what goes on in its head. The shot: what it reveals, and why it matters in the scene. When the thought and the
spoken line differ, the struggle between them is the performance.

### PRS17. Hidden feeling leaks through the body
stages: plan, blocking

A character that tries too hard to look relaxed shows it: folded arms, fidgeting, scratching, looking up and
around, a quick glance at the other character and away, a breath before the lie. The tell can be one clear gesture
held for a beat inside a held pose, late in the shot, while the rest stays small and not constantly fidgeting.
Plan the contrast with the neighbouring shot too: a quiet tell lands after a big, loose one. Mix the traits: a
proud posture with no eye contact and extra fidgeting shows hidden doubt. A character in control stays locked on
the other one; an uneasy one looks away and breaks off axis. Locked eyes are a special moment, not the default.

### PRS18. Show the thought before the act
stages: blocking, passes

A breath, a glance or a small head move shortly before the main action shows the character deciding. Give a nod or
a shake a reason; a nod on every word reads as a machine. Do not fill every silence with business: a pause is the
best place to show thinking, and broad moves read because quiet moments sit beside them.

### PRS19. A hold is small and alive
stages: blocking, passes

A hold must be still enough to read as a hold and alive enough not to be a statue. Never stop dead: continue the
last move into it with a tiny drift in one direction, or let a secondary action take the place of the stop. To get
the drift, move the start key of the hold a little off the final value and flatten the curve toward the end, so
the part arrives but never stops completely and keeps a slight slope. Stagger the arrival by a frame or two, for
example back, then upper chest, then neck and head. Give each mood its own hold. A quiet hold has small head
movement over a still body. A panic hold has flat hips, tiny back-and-forth rotations, a head that moves more than
the hips and a jagged hand path. Inside a hold of about ten frames, an ease-in and a small anticipation leave only
a few frames of true stillness, so plan the length with that in mind.

### PRS20. Hold the last shape, do not reset to neutral
stages: blocking, passes

Between beats the body keeps the shape of the last one and lets the new feeling shape the settle. A return to a
neutral default between every beat reads as a puppet being reset. Keep hands and fingers drifting, and keep a held
object dragging and settling after the body has stopped.

### PRS21. Pick tells that read on screen
stages: plan, passes

A twitch that is natural in live action can read as an error in animation, and a tell that is too small disappears at
game distance. Pick tells that survive the camera, test them at playing size and make each a little larger than it
feels. Some subtle moves last a single frame and are felt only at speed: keep these, but make sure they are felt.

### PRS22. Vary the gaps in an idle loop
stages: blocking, passes

If idle business lands every five frames, the loop beats like a metronome. Vary the gaps between fidgets, weight
shifts and breaths, and break a repeated timing with a secondary action. A weight shift onto the other leg is the
cheapest believable break.

### PRS23. An idle has a lifeline
stages: plan, blocking

A character standing still needs a small lifeline: a weight shift, a breath, one tell, one settle. Choose one or two
and do them well. Add a single small touch only (a finger drag, a collar fidget, a tiny extra step) and let it
support the character, never distract from it. Keep the drift small and in one direction: a random wander reads as
nervous noise.

### PRS24. An idle breaker is a short commercial
stages: brief, plan

A shot standing alone must show everything about the character and the situation: who it is, what it wants, and the
one thing that happens. Boil it to that and cut what a viewer would not miss. Keep three or four clear ideas in a
ten-second shot, with gaps between them so the character seems to think.

### PRS25. Match the size of the acting to the size of the shot
stages: plan, blocking

Wide shots need big, clear silhouettes; close shots need rich, small detail. A big move fails in a close shot and a
small one is lost in a wide shot. Decide the playing size before posing, and check the pose at that size.

### PRS26. Try several ideas, then choose
stages: study, plan

Do not take the first idea. Sketch two or three, act each one at real speed, then pick the one that fits the
character and gives the viewer most to read. If one fails, throw it out instead of forcing it. Act the reference at
real speed: slow-motion acting looks too busy when played back.

### PRS27. Avoid stock gestures, and your own habits
stages: plan, self_review

Stock gestures (wiping an eye for sad, a fist on the table for angry, pointing, symmetric poses) tell the viewer
nothing. A cliche is allowed when it is the best way to communicate or belongs to the character, but explore first.
Do not repeat your own signature moves from shot to shot, and do not reuse one big-emotion pose twice: escalate
through repeated actions, not a bigger pose.

### PRS28. Act the thought, not the word or the feeling
stages: plan, self_review

Indicating is acting out the word or the feeling (a pose for every word). Ask what the character is really doing
and find a simple physical action for it, one that can run under a whole phrase. Try three or four actions for one
beat and keep the one that gives the viewer the most to read. The action must look unaware: if the character seems
to aim at it or think about it, it fails.

### PRS29. Secondary action is behaviour, and it serves the main action
stages: plan, blocking

Secondary action here means tics, habits and small business, not overlap or follow-through. Choose it from who the
character is, what it would do here and its relation to the other character. It supports the main intention and
never covers it: if a hand crosses the face at the key beat, cut the hand. Keep it economical, one or two accents.
A repeated fidget can become the joke, but decide that on purpose.

### PRS30. Put a small tell at the end of a move
stages: blocking, passes

A small adjust at the very end of a move (a tie, a cuff, a fingertip wipe) or just before a larger action (a hat
before bending) breaks it up and makes it human. Use hand business to join one action to the next so the flow does
not read as separate steps.

### PRS31. Let the character touch its world
stages: plan

Give the character something to hold or play with, and let how it holds it say something. Quiet props beat obvious
ones. A character in the background stays alive with a small private action that does not pull focus; if the viewer
notices it, it is too much.

### PRS32. Add the last touches last
stages: passes

Small random touches (a twitch, a tiny asymmetry, a breath on head and chest) go in at the end, once the structure
holds. Add them so the audience feels them without seeing them. A small asymmetry in the head tilt, the shoulders or
the hands is the cheapest tell for a held beat; avoid twinned poses. One signature moment per shot is enough.

### PRS33. Run the sound-off test and the one-line test
stages: self_review, checks

With the sound off, the mood, the intention and the order of the beats must read. Then say the shot in one line
(who wants what and what happens). If the line takes two sentences, the shot has a second idea: cut it or give it
its own beat. Ask what each part of the shot is for; if removing it would not hurt, it is extra.

### PRS34. Personality is the top of a four-layer pyramid
stages: plan, self_review

Build in this order from the base: the body mechanics, then a body that feels alive and believable, then a thought
process, then the personality that only this character would think. The base two layers are the craft; the top two
are what makes the shot this character. Do not polish the top before the base holds.

### PRS35. Name the need, and say whether it is conscious
stages: plan

A conscious need drives a deliberate action. A subconscious need drives an instinct (shrinking back from fear) or
a habit (an act done without thought, like driving with no attention on the key). Write which kind drives each
beat. A deliberate action gets a thought beat before it; an instinct or a habit does not.

### PRS36. Give the character a world model, and let it be wrong
stages: plan

A character acts on what it believes, including what it believes others think. The belief can be false, and a
false belief is a source of story. One clip can serve as a wrong belief, as a filter that makes a character see
the bright side, and as the contrast between two characters. Write the belief in PLAN.md before posing.

### PRS37. Ask how the character feels about this moment
stages: brief, plan

Before any key, settle what the character feels about the moment, as a director would ask. The answer changes the
acting: the same exit can be taken with respect or thrown away. Add the character's background and culture where
they bear on the beat. Write the answer as one line in PLAN.md.

### PRS38. Know why the shot exists, and do not spend what a later shot needs
stages: brief, plan

Every shot carries at least one story point. Your shot may only set up a gag that pays off later, so do not steal
the punchline. Do not spend the biggest expression of the character if a stronger moment comes later in the
sequence. Write the one story point of the shot before blocking.

### PRS39. Watch the whole sequence, not only the shot
stages: understanding, plan

Look at what comes before and after the shot to learn where the character stands emotionally. If a planned beat
clashes with a neighbouring shot, change it or offer a different idea. An idea that serves what the sequence is
about lands; an idea that is funny but off the moment gets cut.

### PRS40. Progress the feeling in steps
stages: plan, blocking

Never go from nothing to full in two poses. Use steps such as 0, 20, 60, 100 and leave the character somewhere to
go. Progress the character across a run of shots in the same way, from calm to uneasy to angry. Grow one
expression or one gesture in the same steps, and plan them back from the full one. The step values are
provisional.

### PRS41. Let the timing arc follow the character's wind-up
stages: blocking, passes

Across a shot, begin with soft, slow eases while the character is not wound up, then tighten and quicken the
timing as it becomes agitated. Blocking holds only placeholder timing, so set this arc in the curves during the
passes.

### PRS42. Timing speaks as much as the pose
stages: blocking, passes

A slow head turn means something other than a fast one. An overshooting head reads as scattered; a direct move
reads as focused. Attitude lives in the curves. A plain cycle with plain A to B blocking has no attitude: add it
with the carry of the head, the timing and one clear fumble, and test it against reference. Treat an action as one
natural flow, not as separate steps with stops between them.

### PRS43. Build the subtext in the same pose list across the passes
stages: plan, blocking, passes

Depth separates a character from a talking head. Show who the character is without a narrator. Layout has nothing
moving. Blocking has a few clear poses. The second pass adds eye moves, breath and the small upper-body life that
sells the thought. The last pass adds hanging items, smooth transitions, a small breath on head and chest, and
one-frame touches felt only at speed. Each pass supports the subtext; none of them adds a new idea.

### PRS44. Micro accents come last, and stay very small
stages: passes

Add small sharp head moves on stressed beats (a little up on a question, a little up when the eyes go up) after
the rest holds. Keep them tiny. Breath matters most in agitated or extreme feeling and in the pauses between
actions.

### PRS45. Secondary action matches the neighbours
stages: plan, self_review

A secondary action must fit the shots around it, must not feel forced, and is left out when the shot does not need
it. A character that repeats the same business in every shot distracts. The viewer should sense it, not notice it.

### PRS46. A simple shot still gets its history
stages: plan, passes

A listener stays subtle: slight head shifts, a squeeze before yes, shoulders that drop before a gesture, blinks. A
plain act gets physical quirks: compress the hand when stopping, release it before letting go, add a drag or a
peel, give the chair a give when the character sits. No flourishes. Let an action cross the cut so the edit
follows it.

### PRS47. Spend effort by importance, and let the world answer
stages: plan, self_review

Find the peak shot of a sequence and give it most of the time. Say no to a flourish the shot does not need. Small
props react to what happens near them, and the setting reacts too: sand changes the step, wind closes the eyes,
nothing passes through the table. A world that ignores the character looks wrong.

### PRS48. Think about what the character does when the camera is elsewhere
stages: plan

Decide what the character does while it listens or while the camera is off it. The answer gives the first and last
frames of a shot their life, and it keeps a reaction shot from being empty.

### PRS49. Keep a hold alive with a drift that continues the last move
stages: blocking, passes

In a long hold, continue the motion the character was in: if it came forward, go a little more forward slowly and
come back a little. Use three keys, with the middle key near the middle of the hold and the last just before the
next move. Keep the spine rotation within about one degree. Never flatten the tangents at the peak. Add the body
first, then neck and head in the same direction, play the hold, and add more only if it is still dead. Try too
much first and pull it back. Keep the drift on its own layer so it can be turned down. The amounts are
provisional.

### PRS50. Every stop gets a settle
stages: passes

Ease into and out of a pose, even a one-frame pose. Add a small overshoot so a move does not stop and die, then
let the part settle so a stopped character keeps a little life in the hand, the fingers or the head. A settle is a
small dip and rise over about 5 frames; judge the number by eye. A machine character is the one that lacks arcs
and eases.

### PRS51. Add dirt last, then scale it down
stages: passes

Real gestures drift and wobble a little: a pointing finger does not hold perfectly clean. Add this in the last
pass, on a separate layer when the rig allows. Make the pattern large first, then scale it down until it is only
just there.

### PRS52. Contrast arcs and tension to mark a surprise
stages: blocking, passes

A run of curved, relaxed moves followed by one straight, arc-free move reads as an exclamation point. For a
surprise, tighten hands, brows and shoulders together, then relax them together. On a second tightening go less
tight so it does not repeat the first.
