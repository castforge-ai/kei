# Spacing and ease

kind: principle

Spacing is where a part sits on each frame between two keys. These rules cover how Kei chooses, builds and checks the
spacing of every move: eases, cushions, favours and the size of each gap.

### SPC1. Spacing is a decision of its own
stages: plan, blocking, passes

Timing says how many frames a move takes; spacing says how far the part travels on each of them. Two moves with the
same keys and the same frame count can read as different actions: one drifts off and lands softly, one snaps away and
cushions in, one eases at both ends. Close frames read slow and wide frames read fast, and that variation makes a
character look as if it moves by its own will rather than being pushed. When a move looks rough, look first for one gap
that is too wide or a stretch with too few in-betweens.

### SPC3. Name how each move starts and ends
stages: plan, blocking

Before touching a curve, record for every move how it begins (ease out, fast out or anticipation) and
how it finishes (ease in, overshoot or fast in). Choose them from what owns each end. When muscle effort begins or ends
the move, that end eases, with three or four frames of cushion even in a fast move; a one-frame reversal becomes a
rounded turn with a short hang. When gravity drives a body into a floor or a wall, nothing cushions the contact: it
speeds up to the hit and makes a hard corner there. When air drag rules a light thing such as a scrap of paper, it
slows into each turn, the reverse of the floor case.

Parts that cannot stop themselves and simply ride momentum, such as a dangling arm, a wrist, the fingers or a loose
head, overshoot and then settle. Matching starts and ends give symmetric timing, which is as weak as a symmetric pose:
make each curve lopsided on purpose.

### SPC4. Pick the stop from the speed of arrival
stages: plan, blocking, passes

A part that arrives slowly eases in. A fast one overshoots and settles. A collision stops on the hit. Two accented
stops add to these, and other rules refer to them by name. A hard accent comes in fast, turns round on the very next
frame and halves back into a held pose that sits short of the farthest point it reached, so the pose held on screen is
the one it bounced back to. A soft accent comes in fast and keeps travelling the same way past the frame where the hit
is felt, then cushions to a stop. Every overshoot is the same shape at another scale, and a large springy one may add
a second, smaller one.

A settle that rocks to and fro needs a restoring force: a pendulum, a slope, an offset weight, a living body. Each rock
is typically about four tenths of the one before. A light object rocks longer; a heavy one gives one tiny rock and
stops firm. A ball resting on level ground has nothing to pull it back, so it does not rock.

### SPC5. A start from rest eases out
stages: blocking, passes

A still body does not jump straight to top speed; only something like a rocket or a cannon, pushed from outside, can.
The opening frame or two of a move therefore stay near the start pose. A fast out belongs where an outside force or a
release launches the object; its first gap is already about 0.8 of top speed. A leap out of a held squash is a fast
out, because the slow part was the anticipation. Going down into a strong anticipation, skip the ease out of the hold
and put the whole cushion at the bottom, so the viewer feels the low point before the push upward. Games often keep an
instant start on a weapon swing and show its weight in the follow-through instead; an instant start with nothing after
it reads light.

### SPC2. Even spacing is a fault unless declared
stages: blocking, passes, checks

A run of equal gaps reads floaty and mechanical, like a moving walkway. Every part that starts or stops through its own
effort bunches its frames at one end or both. A clean, smooth curve does not prove the spacing right: a long stretch at
constant speed still looks dead, so judge the timing by eye once the shapes look smooth. Bunching hard at every end
also looks mechanical; the aim is contrast, not the most ease possible.

Three exceptions exist, and each is written into the plan with its channels and frames. A part may keep a constant
speed as it passes a key. A character straining to lift something almost too heavy for it may grind at an even rate,
with a cause at both ends: a jerk into it, then a hold or a collapse. A stylised mid-air glide may be even.

### SPC13. Weight lives in the spacing
stages: plan, blocking, passes

A heavy object in free fall hangs only briefly at the top, opens big gaps near the ground, and its bounces die fast. A
light one hangs long, travels on wide arcs and drifts. For a character, or a load a character moves, heavier also means
slower, and the extra frames go into the down. A hit followed by a slide to rest reads heavy when the slide is one
long, steady ease whose travel per frame tapers to well under 1 % of the distance; typically 20 or more gaps, from
about 8 % down to about 0.3 %. A short ease reads lighter.

### SPC6. Gravity sets its own spacing
stages: plan, blocking, passes, checks

From the top of a flight the vertical gaps grow in odd-number steps (1, 3, 5, 7 and on) while the horizontal steps stay
equal. The rise uses the same scale as the fall, mirrored. A falling object never slows down before the ground. Near
the apex three to five frames overlap, each step under half the object's width; just before contact the gaps reach more
than its width, typically 1.3 to 1.7 widths for a ball. Measured along the path of a rubber ball about one metre
across, the gaps from apex to contact grow from about 5 % to about 14 % of the fall.

Only a light, draggy object may stop speeding up over its last few frames of fall, and that is declared. An even,
floating glide through the air is a stylised device chosen on purpose, never the result of linear tangents.

### SPC24. A pendulum keeps its period
stages: plan, blocking, passes, checks

A swinging part takes its time from its length: a full period is two pi times the square root of length over gravity.
Choose the length so half a period is a whole number of frames, for example 14 frames for about 0.34 m. Halve into each
extreme and keep the bottom breakdown at full length. An oscillation loses height, never tempo: a settle keeps the
same half-period while each extreme sits closer to rest. Measure each extreme against the one before it: about 0.7
of it for the first two, then faster, about 0.5 and then 0.4. The settle may be cut short. A rigid pendulum can play its frames backwards on the return swing; a part with something trailing from
it cannot reuse them, because the trail has to flip side.

Key a vibrating, springy part with extremes alone, each one closer to rest, and let it leave every extreme slower than
it came in. A fast vibration runs on ones; on twos it slows down. A long, bendy prop has a natural sway rate set by its
length, and wind pushes it at that rate.

### SPC15. Each part gets its own spacing chart
stages: plan, blocking, passes

At any one frame, two parts can be at quite different points along their own paths. In one sweep of a broom, the hips
may ease at both ends while the arms travel at an even rate and the broom head leaves fast and halves into its last
frame. Write a chart for each moving part of a move. Down a chain, derive each section's chart from its driver and
mark where each joint bends. Two parts that start and travel together feel stiff: delay the secondary one and give it
its own ease. An arm that only follows the body gets motion of its own, and a flat plateau in its middle becomes a slow
continuation. A follower takes its cue from how its driver halts: an abrupt halt throws it forward straight away, while
a gradual one lets it overlap later and more softly.

### SPC14. Ease only the channels that slow down
stages: blocking, passes

Easing every channel into every key looks mechanical. Ease a channel only where that axis of motion really slows. In a
walk or a run, the body's progress forward keeps a steady rate, or the motion sticks; only the up and down eases. A
travel curve can often be cleaned to a start, one late key and an end: constant speed, then a single ease into the
stop. Time a rotation such as the hip tilt as a channel of its own, with its own speed in and out, and offset it from
the twist. A shuffle or a dragging walk has uneven travel by design, and the plan says so.

### SPC7. Build a cushion by halving
stages: blocking, passes

For a move that a muscle stops on its key, set the breakdown in the middle, then place each further in-between halfway
between the last one and the extreme. Each gap toward the key is then half the one before it, except the final two,
which come out equal. Put generally: with n gaps on one side of the middle, the gap next to the middle takes half of
that side's travel, each following gap half the one before, and the last two share what remains. In a continuous
swing, set the turn on a pushed extreme placed a little after the story key, about a twelfth of a second, and give the
side leaving the turn this same short profile.

Across a long move between two holds, halving on every frame closes the gaps to nothing far too early. Work in pairs of
frames instead: halve once per pair and set the second frame of each pair straight between its neighbours. The fast
middle stays even and both ends creep into their holds. Never cushion a contact with the ground. The cushion also buys
reading time, so place it where a face or a pose must register: a look might travel for about 14 frames, then take
about 6 to cushion.

### SPC8. Build an ease chart by division
stages: plan, blocking

Besides the halving of SPC7, the standard hand-built eases for a muscle-driven move are made by division. Pick a
divisor n from the frames available, 3 for thirds and 4 for quarters. Divide the travel into n equal parts and mark
the end of the first part and the start of the last; then divide each of those two end parts by n again and mark the
division nearest the key. The marks sit at 1/n and 1/n squared of the travel from each end. Proportions other than
halves, thirds and quarters rarely prove practical.

Halving is close to simple harmonic motion but not equal to it. Against the true harmonic curve it leaves the extreme a
little faster, then lingers near it, then crosses the middle faster; both read, and halving gives the sharper snap. For
a mechanical oscillation, such as a piston, a spring or a body sawing back and forth, use the harmonic spacing itself:
step an angle evenly from 0 to 180 deg across the swing and set each position at (1 minus the cosine of that angle),
divided by 2, of the travel.

### SPC10. Favour a key in space, not by moving it in time
stages: blocking, passes

A breakdown sits on a frame chosen on purpose, often near the middle in time, and its pose leans toward one of its two
keys. Lean it by changing the pose; never slide keys along the timeline to do it. The computed middle value is never
accepted, because spacing left to the interpolator comes out even. Out of an anticipation, favour the anticipation so a
large gap remains before the next pose and the move bursts into it. To make a move pop, lean the frames toward the pose
that comes before the extreme as it arrives, then toward the extreme as it leaves. Decide the in-between part by
part: in one breakdown the hands can favour the grab they have to show while the head and body sit straight between
their keys, and another part can vary. Planned in-betweens read; mechanical ones look mushy.

The strongest favour reuses one key unchanged, placed at roughly 40 % or 67 % of the travel. Work in this order: arcs,
then spacing, then overlap, then overall speed.

### SPC12. A strike ends in the air or on a contact
stages: plan, blocking, passes

When a blow is stopped mid-air by the arm's own effort, it leaves the wind-up slowly, crosses the middle very fast and
cushions into the hit pose. Its largest step at the tip is at least four times its smallest. Weight and force come from
the speeding up and the slowing down; a heavy object nearly always shows both. A strike that ends on a contact reaches
it at full speed with no in-between in front of it. The cushion then moves to the recoil and into the wind-up, and the
struck part is already displaced on the contact frame.

A violent point, punch or kick that stops in the air without hitting anything eases out of its anticipation and
reaches a farthest pose, beyond the final one, on its biggest gap: the overshoot happens across that widest gap, with
no slowing into it. That farthest pose is a key of its own. The part then returns fast and halves into the held pose
as in SPC7, which crowds the last frames near the hold. A soft, gentle point has no overshoot and uses plain halves at
both ends, spread over about two thirds of a second.

A gesture made purely for emphasis is the one case that arrives with a little
slowing. Its slow start ends only a quarter of the way along, by which time three quarters of the move's frames are
spent. The next single frame carries it on to the three-quarter mark. One shorter step then reaches a stretched
extreme past the pose, the frame after comes back halfway, and the part halves into the pose and then into a quieter
final pose. Because the widest step comes one frame ahead of the extreme, the extreme is reached slowing slightly.

### SPC25. A small sharp move pops in a busy frame
stages: blocking, passes

In a realistic or crowded shot, a reaction can be one short, sharp move from a still head, done in three frames: the
middle frame carries two thirds of the whole distance, and the remaining third is split evenly between the frame before
it and the frame after it. A cushion of a little under a third of a second then brings the head back into a moving
hold. The stillness before it and the size of the jump are what make it read while everything else moves.

### SPC16. A plain walk step has two cushions and two fast passes
stages: plan, blocking, passes

A conventional step of half a second at 24 fps has four key-to-key moves: contact to down, down to passing, passing to
up, and up to the next contact. The body is fast where the foot strikes and where the legs cross, and slow at the low
and high points. So the moves that leave the contact and the passing start on their widest gap and halve into the next
key, and the moves that leave the down and the up halve out of their key and end on their widest gap, using the halving
of SPC7. Plotted on pelvis height, the frames crowd at the low and high points and open wide at the strike and the
cross. In a faster step that leaves only two frames per key-to-key move, the single in-between sits halfway.

### SPC17. A step or a turn needs three frames to read
stages: plan, blocking

Give a rotation or a step three frames at the least; at two, the eye merges both images into one double picture. In a
very fast cartoon run, work in pairs of frames: favour the in-betweens toward the extremes so that each pair barely
changes while the jump from one pair to the next is big, and let the passing position be implied rather than drawn.
This pairing works best when the run is seen from the front, from behind or at three-quarters, rather than in pure
profile. In a game, blur comes from the engine's motion blur, never from the keys.

### SPC9. A camera eases on a ramp
stages: plan, passes

Over roughly the first half of a camera move its speed climbs at a constant rate, and over the second half it falls at
that same rate; plotted, the speed is a triangle. So the gaps grow and then shrink by an equal amount per frame, and
the widest gap is close to double what an even move would use. Never cushion a camera by halving, or the frame lands
the way a character settles. As a check, a camera ease keeps its final gap at about 0.8 of the gap before it, while a
character's cushion cuts it to about 0.5.

### SPC18. Repeating and small fast elements strobe
stages: plan, passes, checks

Evenly spaced elements crossing the frame (spokes, rungs, treads, gear teeth, blades, fence posts, stripes), whether
they move or the camera sweeps past them, start to flicker once each one shifts more than about a third of the
spacing per rendered frame. At half the spacing the direction becomes ambiguous, and beyond it the pattern appears to
run in reverse. For more speed, make one element unlike the rest, open up the spacing, or replace the pattern with a
blur. Always test at the delivery frame rate: a wheel that looks fine at 60 fps may spin backwards at 30.

A small fast piece such as a drop, a spark or a pebble that travels further than its own length in a frame is stretched
along its path until consecutive frames overlap a little. A continuous jet is broken into pieces with gaps, or it
flickers. Each piece thrown off a moving source keeps the path it had when it left.

### SPC19. Spacing comes before smears and blur
stages: passes

Speed and impact come from spacing first: a wide gap just before an impact, a frame leaned toward one pose, a shape
stretched where it travels far. Put squash, stretch and distortion into the poses, so that motion blur adds only a
small share, about 10 to 20 %. Use smears rarely, and only for a specific effect.

### SPC20. Shape an ease on the curve
stages: passes

To ease into a hold, pull the value of the key that starts the hold back to about 85 to 95 % of the move and tilt its
handle until the curve passes smoothly over it; the remaining few percent creep in over the old hold. An ease out is
its mirror. Two other methods also work. Stretch a weighted handle far out from the final key. Or add one key at
roughly the time midpoint and lift its value to about two thirds of the travel. The longer the handle, the longer the
ease; the steeper its angle, the faster the arrival.

Build a fast in or fast out from a linear key, or break the tangent and make one side steep. A fast in keeps close to
full speed to the end: its final gap is about 0.8 of the top speed before the part stops dead. Dense keys, even one on
every frame, are allowed only when the curve through them stays smooth.

### SPC26. A tangent never carries the curve past a key
stages: passes, checks

Tilt a key's handle so the motion flows through it, but never so far that the curve swings beyond the value of the
next key. Where a key has been lowered and its handle left flat, the part goes slow, then fast, then slow again. Tip
that handle too far and the curve overshoots on its own, an unplanned reversal. Either ease the tilt back or turn the
overshoot into a posed key. Automatic
tangents through a one-frame spike do the same damage, bending the segments around it away from the key values. Fix
such a fault by keying the shape on its frames, not by adjusting handles.

### SPC23. The last in-betweens follow the breakdowns
stages: passes

Where the breakdowns already carry the variation, the remaining in-betweens go straight between them, with a cushion
into the final pose of a slow move. On a violent move, loose parts such as hands, a hat or a brim reverse, overshoot
and trail on their own while the body itself goes straight between poses. Add grace notes by hand at chosen moments: a
small overshoot, a quick S flip that gives a part somewhere to go, a reversal. Played at full speed, all of it must
still look natural.

### SPC21. Read the spacing one frame at a time
stages: passes, checks, self_review

Step through the move with stepped playback, or show a mark for every frame along the path. Steps of one size mean even
spacing; steps that get longer mean an ease out; steps that get shorter mean an ease in. Read the centre of gravity
from the side, and the wrist for an arm. Uneven marks point at the fault; a snap that is wanted is built into the
spacing on purpose. After each breakdown, judge the gap into the next key, and fix a large jump there on that key. When
a part jumps ahead, turn its parent back on that key and let the tip follow late.

### SPC22. Know the common spacing faults and their cures
stages: passes, checks, self_review

Three faults sit inside a single curve. A hitch is an uneven patch in the spacing, a flat tangent in the middle of a
move included: move the keys on their frames, take out the extra in-betweens, or keep only one. A part meant to hold
that creeps: flatten the holding keys or copy the first held value onto the second. A stop and restart where one
continuous ease was meant: move the key further, smooth the curve, then check the related channels.

Two more show across frames. A large move over too few frames, which reads as duplicate images: reduce it by reposing
or by scaling the curve, while any contact still lands on its target. A control turned against the direction of the
move: rebuild the pose so that every curve travels the move's way.

A move that feels too soft gets more range, with in-betweens favouring the start so it leaves later, or tighter timing,
until it looks as solid as its stepped version.
