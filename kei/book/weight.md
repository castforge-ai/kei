# Weight, mass and balance

kind: principle

How Kei makes a body or an object feel as heavy as it is: the weight class, what weight changes and what it does
not, momentum, the centre of mass over the support, loads, and how a body keeps or wins back its balance.

### WGT1. Measure the character and give it a weight class before planning
stages: rig, weight, plan

The same request for a walk must give an ordinary walk on a slim biped and a thundering one on a huge monster. So,
before any plan, Kei measures silhouette, size and mass and files the character under light, average, heavy or
massive. The scale is written next to that category. Kei also notes whether the rig lets mass travel: can the back
arch, can the shoulders lift or sag, can the chest swell forward? A rig too stiff for this is a limit named before
planning. Each later step refers to the category, and motion outside its band needs a stated reason. A brief may set
a different category, openly.

Mass is one input and mood or energy is the other; they combine. Heavy and gloomy gives the slowest mover, slim and
cheerful the quickest and springiest.

### WGT4. Light and heavy fall alike; the difference starts at the contact
stages: plan, blocking, passes

Mass does not change fall time, so two objects released together from one height touch down on the same frame. What
separates them comes at that touch and after it: the amount of deformation, the height of the rebound, the time spent
on the floor, the time needed to come to rest, and the reaction of whatever was struck. A light ball keeps bouncing;
a heavy one thuds, stays down and rolls away. A drawing or model carries no weight by itself; the spacing around the
stops carries it.

### WGT5. A reference set for free objects of different weight
stages: plan, blocking, checks

Dropped from 1 m under the same rules, three balls give these typical values:

- Rubber: restitution near 0.8, squash to 0.65, stretch capped at 1.3, bounce durations of about 11, 17, 14 and 11
  frames, then a 10-frame roll that eases out fast.
- Bowling: restitution near 0.25, squash to 0.93, stretch capped at 1.04, a single hop of about 5 frames, then a
  30-frame roll that eases out slowly, since it resists stopping.
- Beach: restitution near 0.7 with air drag, squash to 0.75, stretch capped at 1.15, bounce durations of about 12, 12,
  8 and 5 frames, then it halts quickly.

How long a free object stays airborne follows from how high it goes; its weight shows in its behaviour at the floor.

### WGT9. Every move names its force
stages: understanding, plan, blocking

Before keying, Kei writes down who or what powers the move (the character pushing or letting go, a load dragging,
gravity, a second character) and what triggers it at that moment. Moves that have no cause get one, or get removed. A
character's move should also expose three things: the pull of gravity, the muscles of this particular build working
against it, and the intention, including a moment of deciding. Objects get gravity alone and never decide. If
something looks physically off, first identify the force behind it and check that it reads. Moves under direct player
control skip the deciding moment; other characters and cut-scenes keep it.

### WGT10. Weight is the load measured against the strength
stages: weight, plan, blocking, passes

Equal mass travels quicker in a strong body and slower in a feeble or exhausted one, which is why a weak character
reads as heavier at the same mass. Duration signals effort: a move that takes long is costly for that body, and a
snappy one looks powerful. Kei sets each beat's length from the effort it costs, and only then works out the spacing;
as a starting ratio, a heavy body spends about one and a half times a light body's frames on the same action. On
landing, a body sinks only as deep as its legs can support.

### WGT11. More weight means more anticipation, slower starts and stops, and lower poses
stages: weight, plan, blocking, passes

The heavier the character, or the load it handles, the larger its wind-up, the longer it takes to get going and to
come to rest, and the more visible force goes into both. Poses sink: hips dropped, knees flexed, mass sagging toward
the floor. A heavy body also needs a longer recovery after a fall, and after any very quick reaction it carries on
slowly and clumsily so the mass can tell. Its long hanging parts trail it as a chain (FXC12).

Weight belongs in the very first key. An upright heavy body on locked knees is a weak pose, and a neutral hold shows
no mass, strength or effort, so it is never blocked neutral with a plan to add weight later. Free falls keep their
gravity timing regardless of mass.

### WGT12. The heavy low point has phases
stages: blocking, passes

For a heavy step or landing, the bottom spans several frames, not one. It has three jobs, each with frames of its own
and more of them as the mass grows: the legs brake the falling mass, the body catches it, and both legs push it back
up. The foot plants fast and the impact still compresses within 1 or 2 frames; the easing lives in the rounded trough
and the climb, with the hips gliding into the bottom and out of it into the push. A held crouch at the end of a
landing is set as in ACT8.

### WGT13. Weight lives in the up and down, pushed past the measured value
stages: weight, blocking, passes, self_review

Mass is read on the down position, where knees flex and the body sinks, especially right after a straight leg meets
the floor. A walk traced exactly from footage or from gait tables looks floaty, so Kei sets the vertical travel by eye
inside the band for the weight class and makes a measured bob somewhat bigger. The category decides which extreme
gets the extra: a heavy body sinks deeper at the bottom, a light one climbs higher at the top. A lively bounce reads
light and happy; a smaller, lower one reads heavy or sad. Enlarging the bob never compensates for a pelvis that does
not twist.

### WGT14. The side shift of the hips grows with mass
stages: weight, blocking

A light walker's hips stay close to the centre line. A heavy walker swings the full mass of its hips across that line
until all of it sits above the supporting foot, and the rest of its walk mechanics scale up with it. A large belly
moves the centre of mass ahead, so the trunk tips backward to bring it over the feet.

### WGT15. Load makes motion tighter
stages: weight, blocking, passes

With nothing to carry, a body stays centred over its support and spends no motion it does not need, with no stray
pops. Stiffness always comes from something visible: a load, a throw, rough ground. As that cause grows heavier, the
motion gets stiffer, smaller, tighter and more clipped: loaded limbs drawn in, a wide stance, less loose
follow-through. Arcs stay, and soft flesh still jiggles after an impact. A quick hinge at the hips on straight legs
with a fast return reads as a light lift, so a heavy lift never uses it.

### WGT16. Check the weight first, and step every in-between for float
stages: passes, checks, self_review

Every polish pass begins with weight, before any other note. Computer interpolation drains weight through soft,
drifting curves, so Kei steps through each in-between looking for float. A character that floats fails review. A
heavy object is made convincingly heavy, with strain and force on show. Overlap that is too small, or that dies away
too soon, also makes things read light. Weight is judged alongside anticipation, squash and stretch, arcs and overlap.
Get the weight most of the way there in the curves, before any polish: exaggerate the timing, shorten how long the
feet stay up, and let each descent carry its own recovery. Play it at speed and decide whether it has gone too heavy
or still floats.

### WGT17. Weight is a personality lever
stages: understanding, plan

Exaggerating weight beyond what the body requires, with deep downs, slow recoveries and hard contacts, makes a
character feel strong and threatening; give the identical large body a middling weight and it seems harmless.
Deliberately little weight suggests a cheerful, naive or feeble character. Sadness shows as slow, wandering, uneven
motion. Inside one world, a happy character can sit a touch lighter than the others: a slightly longer hang in the
air, softer landings, and now and then a declared bend of the physics.

### WGT18. One truly weighted object anchors a wild action
stages: plan, blocking, passes

When the action is broad, cartoony or frantic, Kei keeps at least one mass behaving for real: a carried prop, a
weapon, or a heavy part of the character itself, with true arcs, real timing and real weight. The rest of the body may
break the rules by declaration; the one honest mass gives the viewer a fixed measure of weight to read the chaos
against. A heavy thing never ripples, floats or whips about as if it had no mass. It slows at its extremes and travels
on thrown or swung paths.

### WGT19. Set several characters' weights side by side
stages: weight, plan, checks

When several characters share one move set, Kei builds the idle, the walk and the jump for each first and plays them
together in sync, so every character reads as its own weight while the group still belongs to one world. For a
ladder of attacks, the medium attack is timed first; the heavy version then gets more frames and the light one fewer,
with that order held across the whole set. No two tiers run the same length.

### WGT20. Weight and balance are the first question of every pose
stages: blocking, passes, checks

For every key and on every pass, Kei asks where the weight sits, what drives it, and whether the effort fits the mass
being moved. Motion begins at the hips. Poses grow from there: upward through spine, arms and head, downward through
legs and feet. The top half of the body offsets the bottom half. No style rescues a move that shows no mass or no
muscle doing the work.

### WGT21. The plumb line runs through the support
stages: plan, blocking, checks

When one foot carries everything, a vertical dropped from the notch at the base of the neck meets that ankle. When
both feet share it, the vertical lands between them, closer to the foot that carries more. Kei checks this in both
front and side views. On slow or held frames the centre of mass is above the support; in motion it rides over
whichever leg is taking the weight, and at rest between the feet. Hip line and centre line are the first marks on
every thumbnail and every key. For a pose on the hands or on a single foot, the base of support and an arrow for the
weight are drawn first, and the pose is built upward from them.

### WGT22. Balance is a seesaw of mass about the support
stages: blocking, passes, checks

Balance works like a seesaw: what matters is each mass multiplied by its distance from the pivot. Draw the vertical
through the support; the weight ahead of it and the weight behind it must roughly match, otherwise the viewer
expects a fall. Count mass, not the area on screen, since trunk and pelvis weigh far more than arms and legs. A
carried load is weight ahead of the line, so hips and back shift behind the line to offset it. When one part thrusts
in one direction, another moves the other way, which keeps the centre of mass from travelling as far as the part
that leads. On a long reach forward the hips move back as the arm extends. The centre of mass may project anywhere
from heel to toe of the support; once it leaves that range, the body must step or fall.

Any bend forward from the hips is balanced this way. Bowing, picking something up or starting to sit, the head
lowers while the pelvis slides behind the heels, and the standing line turns into a triangle whose peak is the hips.
Squatting low, the counterweight to the hips is the head plus whichever arm reaches out ahead. Leaning forward at a
constant speed also needs the buttocks held back.

### WGT23. Balance depends on the centre of mass and its speed
stages: plan, blocking, checks

What decides stability in motion is the extrapolated centre of mass: its position plus its velocity divided by the
square root of g over l, with l about 1.24 times the hip height. With a leg of 0.94 m, a speed of 1 m/s places that
point about 0.34 m in front of the centre of mass. The body is stable while this point stays inside the support.
Typical smallest margins are about 6 cm standing on two feet and about 1.5 cm on one; up on the toes of one foot the
margin is below zero, so that pose can only pass through, never hold. A body whose centre of mass is inside its feet
may still be toppling, and one whose centre of mass is outside may be recovering. While walking, each new foot lands
2 to 3 cm outside that point; to stop, the final foot must land in front of it. Foot placement is a result of the
pelvis swaying, so Kei keys the sway first and then sets the feet where they catch it.

Sources: Hof AL, Gazendam MGJ, Sinke WE (2005), The condition for dynamic stability, Journal of Biomechanics 38(1):1-8, doi:10.1016/j.jbiomech.2004.03.025. Hof AL (2008), The 'extrapolated center of mass' concept suggests a simple control of balance in walking, Human Movement Science 27(1):112-125, doi:10.1016/j.humov.2007.08.003. Hof AL, van Bockel RM, Schoppen T, Postema K (2007), Control of lateral balance in walking, Gait and Posture 25(2):250-258, doi:10.1016/j.gaitpost.2006.04.013.

### WGT24. An off-balance pose is falling, and something catches it
stages: plan, blocking, passes

Any held pose whose weight lies outside the support has to read as mid-fall. A larger step throws the body further
out of balance and demands a larger recovery, a stronger line of action and a bigger change of shape. Kei exaggerates
the reference, and then exaggerates the thumbnails a bit beyond that. The further outside the base the centre of mass
lies, the quicker the return to balance must be. A staggering body leads with its hips; its legs always follow and
land late beneath it.

Without a catch, a lean has only one path: rotating around its support on an arc whose centre is the contact. The
alternative is a fresh contact that stops it. Which way the mass tips off its support tells the viewer where the
momentum is headed: ahead means travel, behind means braking or toppling backward. To begin an effort the body tips
toward it; to halt, the body tips away.

### WGT26. A start from rest is a fall caught by a step
stages: plan, blocking

The wind-up is the visible origin of the force that sets a body moving. The cheapest way to start is to tip the
weight past balance and let gravity do the work. The hips sink and slide above the foot that stays, the other foot
comes up carrying nothing, the body tilts beyond its base, and the first new contact stops the fall, with the pushing
leg stretched out behind and the mass in front of it. From there, momentum takes the hips through the midpoint. Under
weaker gravity, less of this is needed.

### WGT27. A weight shift goes balanced, unbalanced, balanced
stages: plan, blocking, passes

A sideways step goes from balance, through a planned loss of it, and back to balance. Kei builds it part by part:

- Feet and legs: open a little wider than the pelvis so the shift will show, set unevenly, knees soft; the free foot
  lifts and tucks only once the mass is over the staying foot; the leading leg lands straight, heel down and toes up;
  the trailing leg straightens and its heel peels as the mass crosses; the trailing foot is drawn in at the end.
- Centre of mass: travels over the staying foot first, rising a little; stays there while the leading leg reaches;
  crosses onto the leading foot after the low.
- Pelvis: lowest with both feet down, nearer the back foot; rises into a held final pose at the end.
- Upper body: at the reach the shoulder lines up with the leading heel in one straight line.

After a strong reach, the following pose arrives sooner, and the final pose is reached early and held. The supporting
foot is copied exactly from key to key until the weight comes off it. Loose arms rise outward to help the shift and
fall back afterward. Parts come to rest on different frames, never all together.

### WGT28. Sitting and standing exchange the fulcrum, balanced on every key
stages: plan, blocking

Sitting down, the hips travel back while the trunk tips forward above the feet, and the seat is lowered onto the chair
under control, never dropped. Once seated, the buttocks become the pivot and the chair takes the load. Standing up,
the body leans forward until its centre of mass is above the feet while still touching the seat, and only then takes
weight off it. A controlled sit or stand never passes through a fall: on each key a vertical through the present
support has equal mass on either side. Holding something in front, the body needs to lean forward less, because the
load already offsets the hips.

### WGT29. A knee bend moves the whole body
stages: blocking, passes

As soon as the knees flex, the pelvis tilts under and the lumbar spine changes shape. A deeper squat brings a more
relaxed spine, with the chest tipping forward so the mass stays above the feet. A back that stays stiff through a
bend looks false. Even a shallow dip and rise conveys weight, strength and effort.

### WGT30. The joint that balances depends on the support
stages: blocking, passes

On ordinary ground, a gentle shove is answered by the entire body pivoting at the ankles like a single rod, with hips
and knees barely moving; the first correction shows 2 or 3 frames after the shove. On a narrow or tiny support, such
as a beam, a kerb or tiptoes, the hips do the work instead, folding and unfolding fast, chest going one way and pelvis
the other. With the feet side by side, the hips handle sideways sway and the ankles handle forward and back sway. With
one foot in front of the other on a beam, the hips handle forward and back, the feet roll on their edges for sideways
sway, and the arms stay out, making short fast corrections in opposite directions; arms hanging still on a beam look
wrong. Skilled balancers move less than beginners. A push the character expects is met by leaning into it and
tensing in advance.

### WGT31. A real push is answered early by a step or a grab
stages: plan, blocking, passes

Real people step or reach for support well before their centre of mass gets near the edge; a long wobble with the
feet fixed is a cartoon device and must be declared. Balancing at the ankle shifts the pressure by only about 2 cm
and needs about 5 frames, while a step needs at least 7 frames and gives about 20 cm of correction. When a forward
lean is suddenly released, the body tenses within 1 or 2 frames, the head moves passively almost immediately, and the
stepping foot lifts at frames 6 to 8 and lands in one large, fast step. Lifting at frame 2 is quicker than any
reaction, and lifting later than frame 12 suggests a slow, elderly or startled person. A single step can save a lean
of up to about 32 deg in a young body and about 24 deg in an old one; beyond that, a second step or a hand is needed.
If a rail, wall, table or person is close, a hand reaches for it within 3 or 4 frames, fingers spreading on the way.
If nothing is in reach, the arms fling outward to counter the fall or go forward and down for protection. A step made
in reaction has no weight shift prepared before it.

### WGT32. A side push bends the waist, then one step goes out
stages: blocking, passes

A standing body shoved from the side first gives at the waist within 1 to 3 frames, with hips and spine doing most
of the recovery. Then the leg on the shoved side, which the shove has already loaded, takes one step outward; an older
body instead crosses its legs or shuffles several times. While walking, the supporting foot tips onto its edge within
about 5 frames, and the following foot is placed wider on the side of the shove some 7 or more frames later; two steps
on, the walk is normal again. A stance with one foot ahead of the other resists pushes along the feet but gives way to
pushes across them, so feet are braced in the direction the push is expected.

### WGT33. In a trip, the foot's phase picks the strategy and the arms tell the outcome
stages: plan, blocking, passes

If the swinging foot is caught early, while it is still behind the body, it is jerked up and over the obstacle while
the standing leg pushes up onto its toes within 2 frames. If it is caught late, already in front, it is driven down
short and flat, and the opposite leg takes the long recovery step, becoming loaded within about 4 frames when the
stumble is saved and 6 or more when it ends in a fall. The arms react within 2 frames and never as mirror images: the
arm on the tripped side swings backward, the other thrusts forward and outward, and the hips rotate so the recovering
leg can reach further. In a save, the trunk stops tipping forward by the first recovery contact; in a fall it keeps
tipping and the recovering knee folds. Both arms thrown forward with palms out signal a fall about to happen, not a
recovery. A body that already stoops forward goes down more readily.

### WGT34. In a slip the feet go forward, the body back, and the arms go up
stages: blocking, passes

When a heel skids forward, the body falls back and both arms are flung upward and forward, never swept behind. The
arms start 3 to 5 frames after the skid, the one opposite the sliding foot rising most. They reach their highest at
about frame 12 or 13 in a young body and around frame 20 in an old one, which also sways more from side to side. The
other foot jabs down next to or behind the first to broaden the base, and the sliding knee bends to pull its foot
back. In any unexpected loss of balance, legs respond before arms. Slipping a second time on the same surface looks
calmer and lower.

In a broad, cartoon slip the feet leave and the body drops at once while the head lags behind it: thrown upward over
the first couple of frames, keyed on every frame, it follows the body down on a curve and snaps past it at the very
end, so it lands last. Give the impact the squash of ACT54 and climb out of it more slowly than the fall came in;
nothing freezes at the moment of the hit.

### WGT35. A fall folds, twists and rolls into the ground, the hand first
stages: plan, blocking, passes

The cause of a fall is shown on screen. For older people the most frequent cause is a misjudged weight shift (turning,
reaching, missing the chair when sitting), followed by tripping, with slipping a distant third. In a sideways fall
there is a step toward the fall if time allows; then knees and hips collapse, the body rotates as it drops, the hand
on the falling side meets the ground about one frame ahead of the hip, and contact travels along shin, thigh, buttock
and back in sequence, never as a stiff board. In a forward fall the arms go forward and down with open hands and
elbows a little flexed, about 10 to 40 deg at impact, then flex more to cushion, while the head is kept up; a weak arm
buckles. In a backward fall the body sits down with arms reaching behind, and the impact frame shows a brief, hard
squash through pelvis and spine. A frail body twists less on the way down.

### WGT37. One-leg holds, reaches and leans have limits
stages: plan, blocking, checks

Balanced on one foot, the centre of mass hovers within about 1.5 cm of a spot directly above that foot, and small
corrections at the toes and hip remain visible; the body never freezes, and a free character raises its arms to help.
An elderly body manages a one-leg stand for only a few seconds. With both feet planted, a forward reach extends about
0.4 m beyond arm's length, the hips moving back while the arm moves out; any further and a heel comes up and a step is
needed. Leaning while standing works only to about 8 deg forward or about 4.5 deg backward; past that, the heel or the
toes come up, the hips bend, or the body steps.

### WGT38. Stopping a run, and how heavy, old and tired bodies balance
stages: weight, plan, blocking

Stopping from a run takes several braking steps. Each foot lands 0.3 to 0.5 m in front of the centre of mass, heel
striking first and then the sole slapping down, while the trunk tilts back behind the feet and the knees bend deeply,
and each contact soaks up the impact hard in its first frame or two. Braking from a sprint typically needs about 36
frames and about 7 m. A heavy body resists being knocked off balance from a standstill but, once moving, is harder to
halt and needs more steps or frames to recover. An older body uses more, shorter and crossing steps, reaches out
protectively and stoops forward. A tired body sways more, walks with wider feet, reacts late and lands stiff. None of
these bodies is timid.

### WGT49. A held load is a live force
stages: blocking, passes, self_review

As long as a load is being held, or any force is acting, no part rests at its neutral position: each part shows what
it contributes, and body and load keep up small corrections. A load may be motionless only when something else is
supporting it. Kei keys these corrections on keys and breakdowns with smooth curves, never as frame-by-frame noise.
The action also gets an inner layer: the character glances at the load, touches it in anticipation and shifts a hand
on it at least once. With a carried load, one arm can act as the rigid prop taking the weight while the other keeps it
level, and that rigid arm restricts how far the body can get ahead of the load.

### WGT50. Decide per beat whether the body or the prop leads
stages: plan, blocking

When the body is in charge, the hips begin every change of direction before the prop's mass gets there. When the prop
is in charge, its momentum drags the body along: the feet step to new spots to keep balance, hips and legs brace, and
the body hauls back. A heavy prop held in both hands acts as a lever around the grip, so the grip is set first. The
hand closer to the heavy end lifts, its shoulder raised and the leg on that side straight and pressing into the
ground; the other hand presses down with its shoulder lowered. The nearer the hands are to each other, the more
strain both must exert, which shows in the shoulders, in one foot taking most of the weight and in the overall stance.

### WGT45. One straight line of force runs through an effort pose
stages: blocking, passes, checks

For any push, take-off, heave, drive or rise, Kei sets a single straight line starting at the planted feet, running
up the legs and trunk and ending in the load. Its direction follows the path of action; every part is posed along it,
and the legs straighten to serve it. The force reads in both directions: upward into body and load, and downward into
the ground, so the support looks pressed against. Before throwing a heavy load upward, the body crouches very low, feet
beneath the hips, back fairly flat, load hugged in, head tipped back; then both legs drive along that line. Maximum
force recruits the whole body. A rise is prepared by a dip, the weight moves onto the supporting foot, the hips sink
and then lift, and the shoulder furthest away rises last. Arms working alone look casual.

### WGT43. A heavy action starts in the pelvis, and the back reverses its curve
stages: blocking, passes

A heavy action begins at the pelvis; chest, arms and tool follow in turn, and the tool comes last. Light work with the
hands starts at the elbow instead, and the body stays where it is. In heavy work the two hands never set off on the
same frame, and the head arrives late. Key the curve of the back for each phase: hunched over the load, flipping as
the load is drawn in and lifted, straight at the top, and flipping again at each fresh push. At peak effort the one
joint carrying the load (the knees during the rise, the elbows overhead) may shake slightly, keyed as a declared
tremor on that joint alone. Fast beats like the dig or the break-free contrast against slow ones like the strain.

### WGT47. A load keeps its own momentum
stages: blocking, passes

A heave is a wind-up followed by a drive, and the body moves first. When the body halts, a load that is free to move
keeps going, reaches its top a frame or two after the body does and then sinks lower than the body; a load held
tight against the body lags only slightly. The plan marks the frame where the force flips. After it the load moves
down first, and every part resists it: knees yielding, back hauling backward. A load dragging on a straight arm draws
the body forward, and the body tilts and shifts the opposite way; the centre of mass is checked on that frame. A hand
changes its grip on a heavy load only when the load is at its highest and slowest, and the freed hand hurries to its
new hold; how far the load drops before the catch shows the effort.

### WGT42. Sell the weight before a heavy lift, and lift with the legs
stages: plan, blocking, passes

Establish the weight before anything moves: the character looks the load over and sizes it up, steps in close, sets
the feet wide and bends the knees. A one-handed reach on straight legs makes any load look light.

Through a heavy lift, track each part on its own:

- Hips: from a standing start they hinge forward over the load; they are lowest at the grip, sunk between knees
  spread either side of it; through the strain they ease back and down, drawing the load toward the body before it
  rises; once it breaks free they climb under the leg drive; at the finish they stay pushed back with the back arched.
- Back: held as flat as the build allows at the grip, where it may curve by no more than 20 to 30 deg, and straight
  once the rise is complete.
- Arms: long at the grip, holding the load near the thighs on the way up and pulling it in at the top.
- Head: lifts as the grip is taken.
- Legs: do the driving, along an almost straight line from heel to head, and the body ends upright and stiff.
- Load: within about 25 to 30 cm of the point between the ankles throughout; a box wider than the feet calls for a
  wide stance with only moderate knee bend.

From grip to lift-off takes roughly 3 to 6 frames for a light load and 8 to 16 for a heavy one. Mechanics may be
borrowed from a reference of a light lift, but the timing comes from the weight rules here, scaled to a load-to-body
mass ratio written on the plan. Only one beat, either the strain or the break-off, gets the extra frames.

### WGT48. A resisted set-down holds the shoulders up until last
stages: blocking, passes

Putting a heavy load down under control, the feet spread, the load hangs low from straight arms, and knees and back
flex at the same time into a deep squat with the knees opening around the load. The shoulders remain lifted while the
arms lower the load, the body working upward as the load drags down. The hands let go only after the load is resting.
Lifting under strain gets more frames than letting go; a simple drop is quick, but a controlled lowering is given its
own time.

### WGT41. A load is a second support until it leaves the ground
stages: blocking, passes, checks

A load still resting on the floor takes some of the body's weight through the hands that grip it. At break-off that
support disappears all at once, so on that exact beat the weight moves backward into the shoulders: head and chest
rise and pull back while the hips are still down, and the legs drive only afterward. From then on the shared centre
of mass of body and load sits over the feet. Before it breaks free, the effort is smaller than the load's weight and nothing
moves; once the effort exceeds it, the load lifts. A set-down runs the other way: the moment the load touches down it
becomes a support again, and the body shifts its weight back to its feet before the hands open. While the load rests,
it is copied exactly from key to key, with no slide or pop where it lifts off or lands.

### WGT52. Name the material before a drop or a fall
stages: understanding, plan, blocking

Before animating anything that falls, write down what it is made of and choose four behaviours from that: the fall,
the contact, the rebound and the settle. The fall is either slowed by air and eased into each turn of a swaying path,
or straight and accelerating. The contact may squash, keep its shape, burst, or splat with no in-betweens. The rebound
is high, tiny or absent, and the settle may flutter, roll, heap or simply stop. A brittle thing may also survive the
landing, sit still for a moment, and only then come apart. Liberties with reality are allowed as long as the result
still looks believable.

Typical choices: a sandbag drops straight, thuds with no rebound and slumps into a heap; a table-tennis ball keeps its
shape, springs high and chatters to a stop; a sheet of paper sways down, easing into each turn of its path; a stiff
plywood panel comes down tilted and strikes with one corner first; a water balloon bursts on contact; a heavy log
lands with a dull thump, barely lifts, then rolls. A splash and its ripples sell the mass of what fell in. Check one
frame before and one after the contact: the shape and the rebound must fit the material.
