# Staging

kind: principle

Staging decides what the viewer sees and where the eye goes. These rules cover the point of a shot, the camera and
its lens, the silhouette as the camera shows it, the centre of interest, screen direction, cuts, and how a fast action
reads as a shape.

### STG1. Write the point of the shot first
stages: brief, plan, blocking, self_review

Staging exists to make the shot clear, and for nothing else. Before blocking, write a single sentence naming what the
viewer has to come away understanding. Hold every part of the shot up against that sentence: the action, the props,
where the camera sits, who stands where, the idea behind it, and the set. Show one idea at a time and the strongest
moments in order, and give each beat a pause in front of it, either its own anticipation or a quiet stretch. Each pose
carries one story point and faces one way.

Cut any business placed ahead of the main action that the sentence does not need (a stray glance, fidgeting, a
change of mood), or the main action gets lost. In a crowded frame, only the part doing the acting moves. A camera move,
an effect or a particle is added only when the sentence calls for it. Judge a shot by what it says, never by the tool
or the quality of the render.

Pace a sequence of beats, such as a cinematic or a reel of moves, as a slope: generous at the start, where the viewer
is still getting their bearings in the place, the character and the mood; tighter as the climax nears; and with the
biggest beat kept for the climax itself. A loop the player drives does not get this pacing.

### STG17. A shot carries a change and ends on a closing pose
stages: plan, blocking

The shot leads from one state to a different one by its end. Repeated actions get poses that differ from each other.
The last beat lands on a clear closing pose. A beat that does not advance the main want is cut, however much fun it is,
and once a statement has been made the next beat begins.

### STG33. When the acting cannot carry a beat, change the staging
stages: plan, blocking

When blocking shows that a change of state does not read, because it rests on very fine acting, try a staging fix
before adding keys. Hide the change itself, behind an object, off screen or across a cut, and show the state before
and the state after clearly; or rewrite the wants of the characters so that the beat can be told in broad attitudes
that read. After three blocking attempts that do not read, the plan is at fault, not the keys. A state change the
player must see in a game is never hidden.

For menace, dread or a hard fact, show a warning before the act and the aftermath after it rather than the whole act:
a light going out at the end of a corridor beforehand, then a door found ajar, a cup still steaming beside an empty
chair, footprints that stop. Underplay: the acting stays below the size of the feeling. A careful approach toward
danger is never a plain walk: it slows down, the body turns partly side-on, the path bends to stay behind whatever
hides it, and each step tries to land lightly. When a literal shot turns comic inside a grim beat, swap it for another
idea rather than refining it. The reverse holds for a magical or uncanny change, which the viewer sees completely.

### STG13. The eye goes to contrast
stages: plan, blocking, self_review

The viewer looks at whatever differs from its surroundings: the one moving thing among still ones, the one still thing
in a busy frame, the tallest, the nearest, the strongest value step. A value step sets the character apart from the
background, and the background holds less contrast than the character. Acting detail lives in the mid tones. A dark
figure goes on a lighter ground, never dark on dark. Pure black anywhere on screen, whatever its size, reads as a hole
cut through the picture rather than as a dark surface, so keep it out. To make a figure feel alone, set it small and
deep in a wide, empty space, with the light coming in from one side.

Name one centre of interest for each beat. Every other movement plays as an accent in service of that centre, never
stealing the eye. Besides contrast, the point can be set apart by isolation from a group, by the looks of the other
characters, or by focus. Shallow focus suits only a long lens; a rack focus can move the eye between two characters at
different depths without a cut, and a wide shot is never blurred, since it then looks like a model. To test a frame,
squint at it: whatever mass or cluster dominates must be what the shot is about.

### STG29. Every placement says something
stages: plan, blocking

Set the aspect ratio before composing, then write, for each element of the frame, what its position tells the viewer.
Lines carry meaning: straight lines feel stable, soft curves calm, diagonals active and fast, opposing lines tense or
dangerous, so action poses and paths use diagonals. The eye runs along a long pointed shape, such as an arm, a leg or
the line of action; a round mass holds the eye; a square is steady yet colder than a circle. Let curved contours on
the outside enclose the centre of interest, and let straight lines inside the frame aim at it.

When a gesture has to land on a target, the limb lines and the eye line join into one pointing shape aimed at it. A
limb may leave the group only if it leads the eye back in. These guidelines help and never confine, and one is broken
only on purpose. The shape readings are design defaults, never a check.

### STG14. Play the action against a simple background
stages: plan, blocking, checks

The area behind the figure and along its path stays plain and close in value, or sits in a pool of light, with a
solid floor under the feet. Detail goes to the edges of the frame, and a close-up gets a plain backing. The more
extreme the action, the fewer things behind it. If a prop, an effect or set dressing added later buries the action,
the addition changes, not the action. If the layout fights the move, change the camera rather than the readability.

Review renders use a plain, value-separated backdrop with a ground plane. Two useful checks: the contrast in a band
around the silhouette, and how much of the silhouette is hidden on each key frame.

### STG12. Build depth cue by cue
stages: plan, blocking

Depth is made, not hoped for. The cues are perspective, layers of size, overlap, motion into depth, parallax, softened
distance behind a sharp subject, and tone. A low horizon feels dramatic and a high one calm. With a player camera most
fixed-frame cues belong to the level, but tonal separation and true depth in the path of action work in every view.

In 3D the lens does the growing. Keep the stride constant in world space, or change it on purpose, and let the camera
make the near limb larger. In a fixed camera, depth can be sold three ways: swing the limb further toward the lens,
pick a wider focal length, or enlarge the nearest part as a stated cheat. Gameplay never gets a scale cheat. A dash
from near the lens all the way to the horizon, its anticipation included, usually fills half a second to two thirds
of a second of screen time, keyed on ones.

### STG32. Distance and orientation set a relationship
stages: plan, blocking

How close two shapes stand sets their relationship: near reads friendly, far reads distant. A shape that points at the
other turns the meeting into conflict. As a conflict grows, two figures that began upright and apart move closer and
lean in; the speaker's shape pushes into the listener's space, and that push changes sides when the other answers.

Posture toward the other grades the attitude. A trunk leaning away reads cautious or yielding, upright reads balanced,
forward reads confident, and past the feet reads confrontational; that last lean is declared in the plan. Low
shoulders read relaxed or submissive, raised ones read challenging, and the height of the hands and the distance add
to it. Change these placements only when the attitude changes. With a single character, the goal, a prop or a threat
plays the role of the other shape.

### STG30. The frame's weight and balance set the feeling
stages: plan

The same action staged two ways tells two stories. Weight low in the frame reads as calm, stable and strong; weight
high and crowded reads as uneasy and closed in. An edge lined up with the frame or the ground feels steady, while a
tilted, lifted one feels adrift. A figure tucked into a lower corner under a lot of open space feels lonely; one high and
overhanging feels in danger.

Uneven balance is stronger and more cinematic. Even, mirrored balance is orderly and quiet, which weakens drama; it
suits a peaceful beat, where all the lines converge on a single spot. Set the head and the main action close to where
the lines dividing the frame in thirds cross, and keep the horizon off the centre. The plan writes the feeling of each
beat and sets the figure, and how it leans, relative to the borders of the frame to suit it.

### STG2. Question the shot before placing the camera
stages: brief, plan

Three things are settled on paper before a camera goes in: that the shot is needed at all, how near the camera must
come, and whether the whole body must be seen. Then name whose shot it is and the shooting style: a camera that stays
put or one that travels, a level frame or a tilted one. Decide too if the viewer must follow the route from one place
to another. When the action travels, the full path fits inside the frame. Pick the framing first; then animate in
detail only what that framing shows and the point needs.

A shot is one unbroken view from one camera, so no cut can save a beat that fails inside it. Treat blocking versions
as takes: try several and keep the best one.

### STG31. Each shot size has a job
stages: plan

A wide shot, from above the head to below the toes, serves large physical movement. A medium shot, from the belt up,
serves beats where the hands speak. A medium close-up, cut below the collarbone, serves head gestures; a close-up, cut
below the chin, serves reactions of the face; an extreme close-up isolates the eyes. Keep roughly the same space above
the head whatever the size. Never cut from one framing straight to another of equal size on the same subject.

### STG3. The camera spec gives size, angle and height
stages: plan

Subject size comes from how far the camera sits and which lens it carries. The character is never scaled or shifted
to fill the frame. A full-body action takes a full or long shot, composed loosely so the whole path has room. A camera
square to the side or the front looks flat: swing it off the axis to a three-quarter seat so that leans and depth
show.

Keep the horizon level unless unease is the intent; a tilted frame reads as chaos and wears out fast. Height follows
status. Eye height is neutral, a low camera gives power, and a high one makes the subject small and weak or shows the
floor plan. A figure placed high in the frame looks large. Set-ups that look past a shoulder, cut to the reverse, or show what a
character sees are kept for dialogue.

### STG6. In gameplay, every angle must read
stages: plan, blocking, checks, self_review

A player camera can sit anywhere, so a gameplay move is never staged or cheated toward one view. Staging there lives in
level design and lighting. Give every move some sideways component as well as its main direction. End poses keep every
limb off the world axes, and the line of action stays off the axes and changes from key to key. A path that cuts
across several axes reads better than one that runs along a single axis.

Approve the move from all sides, then polish the view the game's camera shows most often: the side view when the
camera is fixed beside the action, as in a one-on-one fighting game, and the view from behind for a third-person game.
A presented shot is judged through its own camera, yet each pose should still hold up when seen from elsewhere.

### STG4. Choose the lens on purpose
stages: plan

A normal lens of 35 to 50 mm is the default for a presented shot. A short lens close to a character bends it and
pushes the viewer away; around 10 to 20 mm looks close to fisheye. A long lens of roughly 100 to 200 mm flattens the
planes together, keeps focus shallow and feels intimate.

For a cutscene in a game, pick a lens narrower than the one used in play, from the list the project approves, and
let the camera rest only on a value from that list. A bigger subject comes from backing the camera away and choosing a
longer focal length. A wide lens is never brought close to a face. Gameplay keeps the field of view of the game.

### STG9. Pose for the lens
stages: blocking, passes, checks

A pose points somewhere: the eyes look and the hands reach. Both eyes and all four limbs are visible, above all in the
first eight frames of the shot. Avoid tangents and avoid a limb set square to the frame edge. Show the corner of each
elbow and knee; a hinge seen edge on looks like a flat plane. No moving part ever crosses in front of the face that is
acting.

A turn added to show the face to the camera eases out of the pose before it and stays small at the breakdown. A back
view may keep the face away from the camera to show vulnerability, holding back its first turn until the key moment;
the plan then states that choice and the frame of the reveal.

The angle of the face to the lens sets how close the viewer feels. Facing the lens is the most intimate; past about 60
deg the far eye is lost, and a profile at 90 deg shuts the viewer out. When the face carries the feeling, hold the
face inside roughly 60 deg from the lens axis, and inside about 10 to 20 deg for an intimate beat. On every beat that
presents an idea, the chest and the gesturing arm open toward the lens, three-quarter or more. A character that enters
with its back turned turns open before its action reads.

### STG5. Lock the shot camera and judge through it
stages: plan, blocking, passes, checks

The camera spec sits on the plan, and the shot camera is set at the delivery resolution with every channel locked.
Pose in whatever view is handy, but keep the shot camera open in a second panel and judge each pose there. Zoom by
cropping the view, never by moving the camera, and look with the control overlay hidden. Each pass also plays in
perspective and from the front.

The camera may be restaged only at the blocking review, as a staging fault. Play the whole blocking through it; if the
character leaves the frame or runs out of room, move the camera, write the new spec and lock it again for every later
pass.

### STG11. Main motion reads best across the screen
stages: plan, blocking

Give the main motion a part that travels across the screen. When it has to travel into depth, push the side parts
out, the elbows, knees and feet, so the bend shows in the outline. Never fold a joint straight toward the lens. Motion
into depth looks deeper but reads harder; take that trade on purpose.

### STG10. The faster the action, the simpler the shape
stages: plan, blocking, passes

A fast move reads as a single shape, so it gets one line of action with no parts pulling against each other, a clean
silhouette and clean arcs on its key frames. Stage it from the side where the limbs swing clear of the body, most
often profile, sometimes the front when the arms spread up and out.

When an action lasts only a handful of frames, the viewer no longer sees inner detail, only a run of filled shapes.
Design each of those frames as a silhouette in its own right, at its own point on the arc, and let its outline follow
the force acting on the body on that frame. A push stretches the shape out along the push, as at a push-off; weight
pressing down gathers it in and lowers it, as at a landing or in a crouch; and with no force on it but its own travel,
the shape streamlines along its path, flat where it skims and narrowed to a point where it drops. ACT26 sets out the
full order of shapes for a creature hop. No two neighbouring frames share a shape. Check by playing the fast part as
black shapes only.

### STG18. A reaction flips the silhouette
stages: blocking

The pose before a reaction and the pose after it are opposites in outline: bent over against upright, a curve against
its reverse, compact against open. Both should read as black shapes on their own. The character then works inside the
new pose and presents the next idea in a clear outline of its own.

### STG16. Watchers point at the centre of interest
stages: blocking, passes

A character who watches turns the whole body toward the one acting, not only the head, and attention passes back and
forth between them. In a group, every body and every head points at the single centre of interest. To catch one who has
drifted, measure the angle from where each watcher faces to the direction of that centre.

A crowd never shares a single cycle: give it several, no fewer than four, varied in speed and in start point. Two
figures side by side never reach one pose on the same frame. How far apart they are sets the reading: an offset under
about a tenth of a second keeps them together as one group, and one of a sixth of a second or more breaks them into
separate individuals. Perfect unison belongs only to a group trained to move as one, such as a marching column, or to
a mechanical group, and the plan says so. Block a group that moves as one mass first, the leading figure first, then
split it into individuals who stay inside that mass and act in their own way. The near figures get their own
performance; the far ones nearly hold.

### STG15. Cause, anticipation and result stay in frame
stages: plan, blocking, checks

The cause of an action, its anticipation and its result all sit inside the frame, each long enough to read. While an
anticipation plays, nothing else in the frame competes with it. A shot may choose to hide an action, for example a
change covered by a splash or a threat kept in shadow. Then the sign of it and its outcome stay visible and readable,
and the plan records the choice.

### STG28. A look into the lens shares a reaction
stages: plan, blocking

When an action ends in a cinematic or a presented shot, the character may glance straight into the lens so the viewer
shares its feeling. Use it once per idea and hold it long enough to read. Clips played under a player camera never
use it.

### STG23. Time a camera move like an animated move
stages: passes, checks

Inside a shot, a camera move runs as a held start, an ease out, an ease in and a held end, with a sample on every
frame. Position, focal length and rotation all follow the same normalised ease, which keeps any channel from landing
first or creeping after the others; at 10, 50 and 90 percent of the move all
channels sit within a frame of each other. Check the first and last frames for a jump.

When a camera must catch up, pan ahead or stop, the change gets an eased chart of its own. That chart begins at the
current speed rather than at zero and lands on a set frame. The character's ground speed stays constant through it.

### STG25. The character keeps the camera's rate
stages: passes, checks

Under a camera that moves smoothly, the character never holds a pose or steps at a rate below the render; it plays on
ones, and so does any shot where the feet or another contact show during a pan. A player camera moves on every frame,
so a game character plays on every frame too, with the feet locked whatever the camera does. Work for a high frame
rate may be authored at a lower one if the engine fills each rendered frame, but no pose is held across two rendered
frames. Flag every frame where the camera moves and the pose matches the previous one.

### STG22. The camera follows late and settles late
stages: passes

A following camera begins to move a few frames after the subject does and stops only once the subject has settled.
While it catches up, the subject may come close to the frame edge for a moment. The camera moves ahead of the subject
only when the subject's path is straight and predictable, such as a walk across the screen. Cut rate and camera speed
match the intensity of the gameplay on either side of the cinematic.

### STG21. Every camera move has a written reason
stages: plan, passes

A move reframes the shot for a story purpose: to follow, to reveal, to show a reaction, to go closer or further, or to
make chaos. It never pulls more attention than the subject, so it is slower and smaller than the action. Name its type
(pan, track, dolly, crane, tilt) and also the physical support that would carry such a camera, for example a person's
hands, wheels on a track, a boom arm or a vehicle mount, and keep the move within what that support could really do.
A realisation may earn a slow, small push-in. A move added for decoration is cut.

### STG24. A move that spans a whole shot never stops at the cut
stages: passes

When a camera move runs from one cut to the next, its opening frame is already in motion and its final frame keeps
some speed. A slow-down to rest gives the cut away. If the move reaches its target early, it keeps a slight
drift instead of freezing. Only a move with held frames on both sides inside the shot follows the full ease of STG23.

### STG27. Nothing intersects where the shot camera can see
stages: passes, checks

Every interpenetration visible through the shot camera is removed, and each fix is judged through that camera. Use a
held constraint only where a contact lasts several frames. Any prop the character leans on, rests on or brushes against
reacts, for example a seat sliding back as the character rises.

### STG20. Keep screen direction
stages: plan, blocking, checks

When two characters face off, give each a fixed side: one looks toward screen right in every shot size, the other
toward screen left, and the same holds for looks up and down. The camera never crosses the line that joins them.
Across a cut, the direction of the look carries the connection between them, even when the head positions do not
match from one shot to the next. Reverse shots mirror each other: a matching
offset from the eye line, typically near 10 deg, the same lens and the same brow-to-lens distance, so the two heads
come out the same size. A tighter offset feels more intimate. For a single character, whatever it acts toward stays on
one side of the frame, and direction holds across cuts. A character travelling toward one side of the screen keeps
going that way until it reaches its goal, because the viewer remembers the direction. A reversal needs its stop or its
turn shown on screen. The direction a limb travels also holds across a change of view.

A player camera breaks screen direction and back-view reveals, so those devices belong to fixed-camera cinematics.

### STG26. Cut during the action and check every cut
stages: passes, checks

Make the cut while a movement is still going, for example while a character is still turning toward a door, so the
motion carries into the next shot. Frame the following shot with the moving part close to where it was on screen. A
small tracking motion that begins as the outgoing shot ends, and completes as the incoming shot opens before it comes
to rest, carries the eye over.

Review every shot for how it is composed, the angle it is seen from, whether a close-up is used where it earns its
place, how it continues from the shots on either side, and the cut itself. Step through each cut one frame at a time,
looking for any prop, light, foot or piece of cloth that pops. A cinematic runs no longer than its message needs; any
beat it can lose is cut.

### STG19. Entrances and exits take at least five frames
stages: blocking, checks

A character entering, leaving or crossing the frame keeps some part of itself visible for a fifth of a second or
longer, counted in whole frames and rounded up (so 5 at 24 fps and 6 at 30); any shorter and it pops. The count runs
from the first frame showing any of it to the last frame where it still touches the edge. Fixed cameras and
cinematics follow this; a player camera falls outside it.
