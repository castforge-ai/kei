# Reference and observation

kind: subject

Kei learns a move from real motion before it keys one. These rules cover how Kei finds reference, vets it, measures
it, studies it without copying it, and decides where the shot leaves it.

### REF1. Reference is a target, never a law
stages: study, plan, checks

A clip shows one way a move can happen. It does not outrank physics, anatomy or plain sense. When following the clip
would make the next frames impossible or absurd, the rule wins and the clip loses. Every result made from reference
still passes the same checks as any other shot.

### REF2. Leave the reference on purpose and say so
stages: plan, blocking, self_review

A shot may depart from its reference, or break a physical or continuity rule, only where an event is declared for it,
such as an impact received or a push for style. Mark each one on the timeline with its frame range and its reason.
While planning, also write down every surprise the reference showed and every place where the shot will move away from
it. A departure that nobody wrote down is a fault, however good it looks.

### REF3. Keep the hands and sides the reference shows
stages: references, study, plan

Fit the performer as filmed: the same hand holds the same prop on the same side. Mirroring a take, a camera side or an
idle is a tool used only when someone asks for it. A move into or out of an asymmetric idle, or one made with a
one-handed weapon, never mirrors. Deliver the unmirrored version.

### REF4. Study reference; author the keys
stages: study, blocking, passes

Reference gives the timing, the order of events and the staging. The keys are posed by hand in the character's own
proportions. Never trace poses, copy joint coordinates or align poses over the clip as an image plane. A traced copy
keeps the moves and loses the life. Tracing a clip on paper to read its timing is study, not keying.

Rigid things are the exception. A weapon, a shield or a vehicle keeps its exact shape and may ride a path that was
measured or simulated, rotated to suit the posed body. A body or a performance never does. Matching footage one to one
is a separate, declared rotoscope job, never a quiet shortcut in a keyed shot.

### REF5. Learn motion visually, in simple shapes first
stages: study, understanding

Knowing a principle as a sentence is not enough. Read drawings and footage as an animator reads them, starting with
simple shapes. Rebuild a studied move from its rules rather than its frames. Then prove the rule with a variation the
source never showed, and compare with the source only after. Look at the result at every stage, not only at the end.

### REF6. Predict the poses before you look
stages: references, study

Before watching or choosing any clip, write the poses and beats you expect the action to need. Then use the reference
to correct that guess. This keeps reference a tool and not a crutch. Search the whole family of the action, not only
the exact move, and study it at the frame rate of the shot.

### REF7. Choose takes for the body action
stages: references, study

A take earns its place by how clearly the body moves, not by the face, and a physical take that shows no real weight
and balance is replaced. Reference acted or directed for this very shot comes first; found footage comes last and is
labelled as found. Frame the recording the way the shot will be framed, at the planned angle and distance, with room
for every step and prop, and hand the performer a task when the movement on its own is thin.

Plan on keeping only a small share of what is recorded: of roughly ten takes, two or three survive, and the first few
are warm-up. Look at each batch straight away, mark what is worth keeping, and record the next batch aimed at what is
still missing. A take that ticks off the brief but has no life is dropped. A good take with one dead stretch, such as
hands hanging without purpose, keeps the rest and has only that stretch recorded again.

Assemble the best take of each beat into a single clip about as long as the shot, cutting on the action. An accident
that works stays exactly as it happened; it is not acted again. Reference is never faked: imagining an effort does not
replace filming one.

### REF8. Reference life before other animation
stages: references, understanding

Real people, real animals and real experts come first. Shipped games and films come after, as style options, because
motion copied only from other animation loses what made the original feel alive. An invented creature borrows each body
part from the closest real creature, with one sentence on why. A fictional body always has real stand-ins; no move is
invented from nothing. A move nobody can perform is learned from an animal or an insect whose motion resembles it.
Classic cartoons and films may be studied for how clear and how pushed an attitude pose is, never for their
choreography.

### REF9. The reference body must match the character
stages: references, study, plan

Before planning, record the sex, build, mass category and skeleton of the reference performer or of the style reference
set (height, leg and trunk length, arm and upper arm length, the span across the shoulders) beside the character's own,
and compare them. A mismatch is written
into the plan before any amount is taken, and that clip's amounts are not used. Look for a matching set first, then
fall back on measured real-body values. Even a clip with the wrong body can still supply the sequence in which things
happen, if that use is stated.

The arms give a mismatch away first: how they are held, how far the elbows sit from the body, the route of the hands
and what the shoulders do. The hips come next, then the overall read. Gait and stance count as well: heavy footfalls
do not suit a slight body, and an untrained character should not stand like a trained fighter.

### REF10. Transfer the taste, not the body
stages: study, plan, self_review

When the only style reference has a different body or prop, split the style in two. The project's taste carries over.
Measure how far the reference strays from real motion on its own body, and write it as ratios and fractions (speed
against a real move, the anticipation, action and recovery split, hold and hit-freeze lengths, how far poses pass real
joint ranges, ease shapes, smears, squash and stretch, and how much the loose parts drag).

The body and the prop stay behind. How the arms hang, how wide the feet sit, how fast the limbs go, the mass and the
weapon's mechanics are all rebuilt for the new character from its own data. Apply the taste ratios on top of that
realistic base. Then render the new move beside two or three reference clips through the same camera and ask two
questions: does it look like the same game, and does it avoid looking like the reference's body?

### REF12. Research a move nothing in the method covers
stages: understanding, references

When the method holds no recipe, no rule set and no measured values for an action on this type of body, research it
after the brief and before the thumbnails. State in the plan what was searched and what was missing. Then work in this
order: write the gap and the body it is for; choose the real stand-ins; read published biomechanics first, recording
each number with its source and confidence; watch and measure footage of the stand-ins; look at stylised versions as
options only.

Scale borrowed numbers to the character's size and mass (a bigger creature hangs longer in the air and lands harder on
the ground) and mark each one as scaled. Write the result as a rule set with each rule marked measured, scaled or
inferred, and with every conflict listed. Prove it on simple shapes before the character. The research never stops the
shot: work continues with the rule set as a provisional recipe until the shot is reviewed. Skip the research, and say
so, when the method already covers the move.

### REF13. Score what you know, and always take a short look
stages: understanding, references

Before research, score how well the method covers this action on this body: a recipe, measured real data, a shot
already passed by a reviewer, coverage of each phase (start, loop, stop, settle), data for the asked version (weight,
load, prop, emotion, speed) and style data. Research only the parts that score low. A passed review raises the score
for that action and version; an open note lowers it until the note is closed. The weights are guides, tuned from real
verdicts.

Whatever the score, every shot gets a short look: two or three vetted clips of the exact version, about 20 to 30
minutes, before the thumbnails.

### REF14. Vet every reference before using it
stages: references, checks

Give each clip a source tier: live-action footage (ground truth for how real bodies move), shipped or credited
professional work, a credited professional's reel or other craft reference, or unknown work. Unknown work is used only
when it passes the checks and nothing better exists, and it is marked.

Hold the clip to the standard of your own shots. Look for sliding feet, pops, step timing that wanders, shaky arcs,
mass that appears and vanishes, matched twins and machine-like sine waves. Stylisation pushes things the same way
every time; weak animation pushes them at random. Never rely on one clip: gather three to five per version and keep
only the common ground. An odd one out is flagged and set aside. Confirm that each clip suits the shot in body type,
register, speed and version. End each clip with keep, flag or reject and one line of why. Each time a reviewer
overrules a verdict, write the criterion down so the next vetting applies it.

### REF15. Keep notes and measurements, not footage
stages: references, study

Link to a clip at its timestamp; never download it against a site's terms, store it or republish it. Keep written
notes, measured trails and small private sketches, and show those measurements rather than the clip's frames. Private
study frames and any copyrighted study material stay local and are never published. Favour footage under an open
licence and public research data. Treat this as a working policy; it is not legal advice. Local storage, private study, changed timing and different proportions do not by themselves give permission. Check your rights before you copy a reference, upload it to an AI service, or publish anything made from it.

### REF16. One question per viewing, with the reference always open
stages: study, blocking, passes

Write the questions down before watching, and keep the answers with the plan. Watch a clip once for each question: the phases of the action, screen direction, the hand-off points, the order of the
parts. Keep the reference beside the work at every pass, in a player that can scrub and step frames, not only during
planning. A shot is easier to judge next to its neighbours: the shots before and after set its first and last poses. From the spline pass on,
run the side-by-side comparison with the reference at every gate, not only before delivery.

### REF17. Measure the reference frame by frame
stages: study, plan

Step through the clip at its true frame rate. In your own words, note how many frames each part takes, the spacing,
each contact, the main silhouettes, which part moves first, the curves drawn by the head and the extremities, and
where the weight sits. Before keying a beat, list each contact and change of direction and estimate how many keys it
needs. After each key, compare the size of the move with the reference over the same interval and shrink any jump that
is bigger.

### REF18. Track the hips first
stages: study, blocking

When a balance move fails, look in the reference for the one part that differs, which is most often the hips. Make the
hip action visible: picture a pole across the pelvis or draw the hip line, and note the size and the frame of every
tilt change. Take stride and hip action from a performer of similar build. When the clip is unclear, choose the hip
action that best sells the load and the intent.

### REF19. Depart in timing first, posing second
stages: plan, passes, self_review

Act the reference naturally and stylise only while animating. When the shot has to go beyond the clip, change the
timing before the poses: tighten it first, usually, and more so for a game, and push the poses only after that.
Compare phase by phase: the shot's frames over the reference's frames. A ratio of exactly 1.0 on every phase points to
a copy. Expect a game to replay a clip faster than authored, and make sure the key poses still read at about 1.25 to
1.5 times speed. Local storage, private study, changed timing and different proportions do not by themselves give permission. Check your rights before you copy a reference, upload it to an AI service, or publish anything made from it.

### REF20. Learn how the real action works before keying it
stages: understanding, references

Study the true cause and effect of the move: how a weapon is handled, how a trained person sets up and recovers. Real
mechanics are the base. Flair goes only where the project and its tone ask for it, and it is declared. Budget for
versions that multiply, such as one per weapon or per state.

### REF21. Observe people for habits
stages: brief, references

Fix the broad type of the character first. Then give it one or two particular habits taken from watching real people,
such as a way of gripping an object, a favourite way to stand, a nervous gesture or a small detail of dress. Each
habit is body business with a place in the shot and a beat that shows it. Two characters doing the same action should
be told apart by habit, not only by size.

### REF22. Compare like for like
stages: checks, self_review

A match, a style match included, is claimed only after a side-by-side look: the shot beside two or three reference
clips of the same action, through one shared camera, framed the same, beat after beat, with both the clip's frames and
the shot's at full size in a single image. A strip of thumbnails or a single overlay proves nothing. Judge the match by
eye, and track the hand path as well as any prop tip.

### REF23. Settle the camera and the facing first
stages: study, plan

With one camera, a turn of the camera and a turn of the performer look the same. Read the clip at full size, every
frame of one loop, and settle the camera and the facing of the body on each frame before comparing anything. Read
facing as a band of about 45 deg around where the eyes point; the shoulders and hips decide the turn.

### REF24. One camera cannot give depth
stages: study, blocking, checks

A single view can match well in its own picture and still fall apart from the side, the top and in perspective.
Resolve depth on purpose, frame by frame: from foreshortening (a part at about two thirds of its full screen length is
roughly 48 deg out of the picture plane; at a sixth or less it points almost along the view), from what passes in
front of what, and by eye. Write the chosen depth direction per frame. True depth needs either another camera filming
the same action, or key poses placed in 3D by eye from the side and top, with the in-betweens filled around them.

### REF25. Check every change in every view
stages: blocking, passes, checks, self_review

Before a change is shown, view it from the front (the shot or reference camera), the side, the top and in perspective.
A pull added in the camera view, such as a stronger line of action or a turned foot, is usually paid for in depth or in
another view. A lean, a rotation or a cheat toward the lens must hold up from each view. Some faults hide from the
shot camera entirely: the small rock back before a walk sets off only shows from the side, and vertical arcs are read
in a side orthographic view. A bend of the trunk at the hip or spine of more than about 35 deg from the side, or 60 deg
from the top, that neither the camera view nor the reference shows, is an error.

Before the final, play the shot through two or three extra views as well and repair any knee pole flip or broken pose
that the shot camera conceals, since later work will see it. Letting body parts pass into each other to get a deeper
squash is a film cheat that holds only while no view reveals it; a game never allows it.

### REF26. Trust the eye over a tracker
stages: study, checks

Pose trackers lag on fast moves, miss props and raised arms, and swap left and right. Where the clip clearly shows a
foot on the ground, the foot is on the ground, whatever the tracker says. Mark props, grips and missed limbs by hand
on the key frames, and assign left and right over the whole loop at once. Treat a tracker's 3D output as a weak hint:
use only its relative depths, weighted by visibility, and trust it less where it jumps. Cap a measured length where an
uncapped read would force a joint past its limit.

### REF27. Smooth measured paths as a whole
stages: passes

Filtering each channel on its own barely calms the pops and can move a part by tens of centimetres. Smooth the whole
loop at once on the 3D joint paths, harder in depth (which the camera cannot see) than in the picture, tight in holds
and loose on declared strikes.

### REF28. Matching the clip less can be better
stages: checks, self_review

Planted feet and steady holds raise the distance from the reference and are still right. Choose contacts by eye, lock
planted feet flat, and name the holds and accents. A real sweep around the body can fail a jitter limit: decide it
with a reviewer rather than flattening the sweep to pass a number.

### REF29. Critique the reference part by part before using it
stages: study, plan

Mark the clip's key poses on a strip and write beside each what is at work, grouped by kind: the paths (the arcs of the
hands and feet, the reversals, the spacing); the shapes (the line of action, changes of shape, the space between limbs
and body, and the tilts of head, shoulders and pelvis); the balance (the centre of mass against the feet, and how the
hips carry it); the delays (each joint bending after the one that drives it); and where the eyes look.

Give each viewing a single region, in the order that region decides the pose. The trunk and head decide the most, since
they set each pose and how the body comes into it and goes out of it, so they are watched first. The arms decide the
next most, because they carry the gesture and change the most from pose to pose, so they come second. The legs, which
mostly hold the body up, are read third, once the upper body is known. A last viewing takes in the whole body. Write
notes after each viewing. For each part note its first move, what pulls it and its counter move, such as a hand still
going forward while the body goes back.

For each finding, answer two questions and write the answer beside it. Does the shot use it at all? If it does, is it
taken as it is, made bigger, or reshaped? A stock gesture is reshaped only for a stated reason. Note what the clip
lacks, such as no squash before a take-off, and the depth a single camera hides, checked in a second view. No thumbnail
is drawn until each pose and the lead and drag into and out of it are known. After the shot, write what rose above the
clip and what was lost.
