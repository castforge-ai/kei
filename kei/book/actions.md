# Actions

kind: subject

Jumps and landings, lifts and carries, pushes and pulls, throws, strikes, hit reactions, and the transfers between
sitting, standing and the floor. These rules cover how Kei plans, keys and checks an action that a body powers or
receives. Walks and runs are covered with locomotion.

### ACT1. Name the situation of each key and pose the real body first
stages: plan, blocking

Before posing a key, name what the body is doing at that moment: resting, changing support (sitting, standing,
starting, stopping, leaving the floor), handling a load, travelling, recovering balance, reaching, throwing or
striking, jumping or landing. Pose the measured default of a real adult in that situation first, then stylise away
from it on purpose and write the departure in the plan. Give each arm a job (rest, support, carry, counterweight,
reach, gesture, strike), and shape its hand for that job; an arm with no job hangs at rest. For each foot, write what
keeps it planted and which event releases it. Use the frame counts of the situation as first timings, then retime by
eye.

Joint angles carry over as measured; distances are scaled by the character's own limb lengths, since stylised
proportions differ from a real adult's. The numbers in this topic are diagnostics, never targets: a pose inside every
range can still look wrong, and judging by eye settles it.

### ACT2. Find the source of the force before keying
stages: plan, blocking

A move the character powers itself starts at the root. The hips go first, then the chest and shoulders, then the arms,
and the load or tool moves last. A move imposed from outside runs the other way: it begins where the force is applied
and spreads back toward the root, joint by joint, and no part sets off until every link between it and the force has
been drawn taut; the feet come last. A self-powered pull that begins in the hands looks feeble. If the body moves
while the yanked limb is still bent, the body seems to be pulling itself. Even raising one limb begins in the core:
the trunk braces, then the limb lifts with its end trailing.

### ACT3. Self-powered moves wind up; outside forces do not
stages: plan, blocking, self_review

Muscles can only pull, so a body prepares by stretching the muscles that will drive the move: a thrower draws the arm
back, a jumper folds hips and knees, the trunk coils before a turn. By default every self-powered move is prepared,
and one that simply starts reads as a surprise. For every start, note in the plan where the energy is stored and where
its release sends the body. A blow, a yank or a shove from outside gets no anticipation at all. Any other omission
needs a stated reason: an animal acting on instinct, a menacing creature that holds dead still and then reaches full
speed within a few frames, or a wacky surprise or broad comic beat that wants the jolt. Semi-realistic and realistic
work always keeps some anticipation. The plan writes every omission down, so an anticipation is never missing by
accident.

### ACT4. Check the feat against the body before animating it
stages: plan, blocking

A small push loads only the lower joints. A big push recruits the whole chain in order, ankle, knee, hip, then the
arms swung back, and each joint added buys more height or force. Check the action against what this character can do
in its world before animating. If the feat is beyond it, change the action or the world. A longer wind-up never makes
an impossible feat believable, and a real effort with no wind-up at all fails.

### ACT5. Size each action to its own job
stages: plan, blocking

Every push-off and every landing is sized to its own height, distance and load, and a landing includes the strength
not to get hurt. Never copy the push and landing of one jump onto another. When the character chooses to go further or
higher, both the wind-up and the landing change with that choice. A leg that has just buckled, weak or hurt, cannot
lift the body quickly, so the keys that follow stay lower and more hunched.

### ACT6. The trunk drives every effort
stages: blocking, passes

Loads, push-offs, landings and heavy efforts are carried by authored rotations from the pelvis up through the spine to
the chest. The spine rounds into a coil while loading, unrolls in a wave from pelvis to chest on the push, and on a
landing the hips then the chest absorb the impact before a small rebound. A spine that only lags behind the root still
reads as a rigid block. Breathing shows the effort: a deep breath in before, panting after, a sigh at the end.

A broad emotion is an effort too. A jump of delight still loads and absorbs through the legs like any jump; anger, a
heave or a lift involves the legs, hips and spine, not only the arms. A gesture where nothing below the shoulders
changes is unfinished.

### ACT7. A rise climbs the body; a descent starts at the top
stages: blocking, passes

When planted feet push the body up, the pelvis rises first, the middle of the trunk a step later, and the chest and
head last. On the way up the push tips the upper body slightly forward, and it straightens at the top; the hips land
in place a few frames ahead of the chest and head. On the way down the upper body bends first and the hips follow. A
body rising as one block shows no effort. Every character lifts its own weight, so a rise is always a little slower
than the drop into the low pose, even on a light character.

### ACT8. Underline weight on one beat
stages: plan, blocking, passes

Pick one beat to carry the weight, such as a landing, a lift or a sit. That beat gets more frames and a lower, deeper
pose than its neighbours. When a heavy body steps down, lengthen the absorb by two or three frames before it pushes up
again, then ask if it can bear still more. Weight is set beat by beat in the timing: contact, absorb, push. A heavy
low point moves in phases, absorb, catch, then a rise on both legs, eased in and out (WGT12). When a landing ends in a
held crouch, set the hips at the average of their touch-down height and their lowest height; the climb out of the sag
is slow and ends on only a slight bounce.

### ACT9. Push effort past the reference
stages: blocking, passes, self_review

A faithful copy of reference is the baseline, never the result. In an effort action (a throw, a climb, a pull, a push,
a jump) push the key poses further than life: a deeper wind-up, a higher top, a harder landing, a stronger end pose, a
hit that truly connects. Squash the load and the landing, stretch the extremes while keeping the volume, and keep the
free limbs dragging. The whole project shares one level of exaggeration, and each push past a measured value is
declared. Physics still holds under the push: nothing falls faster than gravity, and no prop passes through the body
or the floor.

### ACT10. Flight time follows height, under one gravity
stages: plan, blocking, checks

With take-off and landing at the same level, flight lasts twice the square root of (2h / g), which is the same as
h = g t² / 8. At 24 fps that is about 5 frames for 0.05 m, 7 for 0.10 m, 10 for 0.20 m, 12 for 0.30 m, 14 for 0.40 m,
15 for 0.50 m and 17 for 0.60 m. The centre of mass spends about half its airborne time near the top, within the upper
quarter of the height, so a natural top already hangs. A top held flat for 12 to 15 frames between rises and falls of
two or three frames reads as unreal.

Use one gravity everywhere. Bounce rhythms taken from one example and heights taken from another usually imply two
different gravities, and the small hops float. Either derive each height from its air time or each time from its
height, then confirm that air time divided by the square root of height stays constant across all hops. Every hop in
a shot uses that gravity, including a hop added in a late pass: derive the constant from the hops already approved and
recheck it whenever a hop changes. Across a whole project a given height always takes the same time to fall, and every
character and prop keeps its weight ranking against the others in each clip. A stylised gravity is fine when the
project writes down a single value of it, and a game uses its own tuned gravity for every jump in the game.

### ACT11. The root of a jump behaves like a ball
stages: blocking, passes, checks

Block the root of a jump first, on its animated channels only. The height climbs out of the crouch on a steep, almost
straight push, then rounds into one smooth parabola: a sharp corner at take-off and at landing, a flat tangent only at
the top. Forward travel stays linear through the flight. Reject an even glide that drifts upward, a pointed peak, a
plateau, a slow start followed by one big gap, and a drop from a ledge that falls in one or two frames between holds.
A short ground contact is what pays for a long hang at the top.

When a weight reads wrong, solve the timing with a ball before the character: leaping from a desk is the same problem
as a ball rolling off a ledge. Gravity, spacing and contact squash carry from the ball to any creature; the ball's
passive, decaying bounce does not.

### ACT12. Snap the launch and the landing, ease the top
stages: blocking, passes

Weight in a jump comes from speed at both ends: a quick launch and a quick landing that squashes. Key by key, the
take-off and the contact snap. Only the top and the settle get an ease, and nothing else does. The shape channels get
no ease out of the take-off stretch and none into the contact stretch.

### ACT13. A push-off is a vector
stages: blocking, passes

Pushing off the ground drives the body forward as well as up. The travel keeps its approach speed, slows as the body
ducks into the crouch, then surges with the vertical push. Constant forward speed through a crouch and a leap reads as
if nothing pushed.

### ACT14. A standing jump dips slowly and pushes fast
stages: blocking

For a realistic maximal standing jump the centre of mass drops about a fifth of body height over about 17 frames, and
the push lasts about 7; first movement to take-off is about 21 frames. At the bottom the knee is near 108 deg of
flexion, the hip almost as bent, the trunk well forward, the heels down. The push opens the hip, the knee follows
about a frame behind, then the heel rises, and the toes leave last; every joint firing on one frame reads weak or old.
During the dip the arms go down and behind; they turn upward about two frames into the push and reach high and forward
by the moment the feet leave. A rushed jump dips less, stays more upright and takes about 12 frames. A strong jumper
shows strength in push speed and height, never in a different order.

### ACT15. Prepare the crouch, and size it to the obstacle
stages: plan, blocking

From a stand, the body first lifts a little before it ducks, a small preparatory move ahead of the crouch itself. The
crouch must be deep enough to show the push, and its depth is judged in the viewport. The crouch comes from the legs
letting go, so the body drops between them while the feet stay fixed and the toes are the final part to leave. An
obstacle close to the character's own height is near the limit of what it can clear, which calls for the lowest crouch
and the strongest launch. A small step up stays a step.

### ACT16. Size wind-up and follow-through by the direction of the jump
stages: plan, blocking

A jump upward fights gravity, so its crouch is the bigger beat and its settle is light and slow: the energy went into
the climb. A jump or step down is driven by gravity, so a small wind-up is enough, and the landing carries the larger
follow-through. Write both sizes in the plan before blocking.

### ACT17. Jumping up onto a surface and stepping down from one
stages: plan, blocking, passes

Up: everything in this jump serves momentum along one path. The crouch gathers it into a tight, compact shape while
the mass is still over the take-off point, hips back and chest forward. A quick extension then sends the mass along
the path ahead of its support, so the body flies tilted and the tilt shows where it is heading; the arms leave first,
the hips next, the feet last. Airborne, the body tucks with the arms back, and any short hang at the top serves only
to adjust along the path. The higher surface catches a body that is still tilted: it absorbs a second time with the
arms forward as a counterweight, then straightens and stands.

Down: gravity supplies the energy, so the wind-up is a small dip. The mass tips forward past balance, one leg steps
off and reaches for the lower surface, heel first, and on landing the body leans back behind the landing foot to
cancel the forward motion. A heavy body compresses in one or two frames, sinks as low as its legs allow, and rises
more slowly. The arms lift during the drop and press down after the impact, and soft masses overlap after it too. In
front view the loaded leg is a hinge: the pelvis tilts on that side and the torso hangs off that hip. A long settle
follows.

### ACT18. Breakdowns of a hop
stages: blocking

A hop between two landing keys needs one to four breakdowns. Typical ones: the last frame with the toe on the ground,
the peak, the first frame of contact, and the compression that leads into the following key. On the landing breakdowns
the arms stay in motion, and the curve of the spine flips before the next key arrives. The anticipation before a jump
is held; as it releases, the hips sink, the upper back lifts and the arms swing through to add momentum.

### ACT19. A jumping leg arrives straight, then folds deep
stages: blocking, passes

A jumping leg leaves straight, folds in the air, reaches for the ground straighter than the reference shows, then
compresses deeper than the reference. A leg that lifts bent and arrives straight before it bends sells weight while
the upper body and the other leg keep travelling. A flip gets two springing pushes. In a jump that rotates, follow the
spine through the flight: it leaves the ground straight, bends into one large curve as the body turns over the top,
swings into the opposite curve on the way down, and is straight again just before it compresses on landing. Its
changes overlap the legs and never fall on the same key as theirs.

### ACT20. A figure's jump, pose by pose
stages: plan, blocking, passes

Key a figure's jump as crouch, take-off, top, fall and landing: a squash leaning away from the path, a stretch where
the legs extend fully while the toes still touch (arms raised for a big jump), a tuck or one long line at the top, a
stretch on the way down, then a squash on the frame after the heels touch. A figure meets the ground with at least one
straight leg before the body compresses, and keeps at least one straight leg touching after the body has started to
rise.

By default a jumping figure does not bounce like a ball. Outside a declared squash-and-stretch look, its single line
of action bends forward along the jump at take-off, typically near 33 deg, and turns smoothly backward, typically near
17 deg, by the landing. The take-off stretch eases off through the flight, with no squash at the top and no fresh
stretch on the way down. Start that turn early, so the head, the last part to arrive, has gone back before the feet
touch. A declared cartoony style may instead treat the figure as a ball, with the knees tucked up at the top: plan
every pose between the two stands (crouch, launch, top, fall, landing) and check each one.

The trunk cannot turn in the air on its own: a change from a forward lean to a back lean needs a cause, such as the
legs swinging forward beneath the trunk while the arms circle behind, or spin carried from the take-off. Start the
push with the hips over the support. Poses held too long turn the jump into a series of stills.

### ACT21. Keep action inside the flight, and never twin paired limbs
stages: blocking, passes, checks

In the air the limbs keep changing shape: one leg trails then comes through late, the arms reverse. One held shape
riding the arc floats. Legs lead and trail in a small cycle, never a full pedalling circle, so one foot touches down
before the other. When both feet take off, the driving leg is the last to leave and the other one lifts roughly a
frame sooner.

No living body moves with exact symmetry. Left and right limbs never land, lift or hit an extreme together, and they
differ in pose as well as timing; mirrored values shifted by a frame still look symmetric. Offsets of one or two
frames read as twinned, while four or five frames on a heavy landing read as alive. Choose a lead side per action and
let the body tip onto the first contact until the second one arrives to catch it. Keep a contact table per limb and
flag any two paired contacts within three frames unless the plan declares them.

### ACT22. Land toes first and keep sinking
stages: blocking, passes, checks

Just before contact the toes point (ankle about 30 to 40 deg), the knee is nearly straight (about 12 to 30 deg), the
hip slightly bent and the arms forward and a little out; legs reach for the surface. The ball of the foot meets the
ground first and the heel comes down zero to two frames later, and the impact is over in three or four frames. At peak
force the knee is about 45 to 55 deg and it keeps sinking afterward. Choose depth by intent: soft landings fold the
knee 90 to 125 deg with the trunk forward, normal ones 40 to 90, stiff ones 25 to 80 for a tall, harsh, hurt or clumsy
body. A one-foot landing tips the trunk about 18 deg forward with the knee at 40 to 55. A landing that springs
straight back up spends about five frames on the ground with a shallow bend; eight to ten frames in a deep bend reads
as a landing followed by a new jump.

### ACT23. Land between frames, then tip and tilt
stages: blocking, passes

The landing contact falls between two frames: one frame shows the body still airborne and the next shows it already
bending. Forward spin from the jump continues into the touch-down until the legs check it, so the body tips past,
swings back and settles. The side tilt leans toward the foot that lands first and resolves unevenly, never as two keys
with matching tangents. A body that lands off balance may add late stutter steps. The landing must show its effect in
the body; a slam followed by almost nothing reads false.

### ACT24. Hops, running take-offs and leaps
stages: plan, blocking

Small hops in a row are springs at the ankle: the heels barely touch, the knees give a little, about two hops a second
(11 to 12 frames each). Only a big hop bends the knee far. A running jump plants a nearly straight leg in front of the
body, bends it only about 20 deg and leaves within three or four frames; a deep crouch on that step reads as stop,
then jump. The lowering is spent on the approach instead: in the last strides one step rides high and the next sinks
low, and the take-off itself stays short and stiff. Across the approach the spine reverses its bend from one step to
the next. Clearing an obstacle, the head travels out ahead of the body, and on landing the body gathers small and low
before it rises back into the run. A leap opens its legs as it rises, is widest at the top, keeps them open through
the slow part of the flight and closes them for the landing.

Numbers for the one-leg take-off from a run, as typical values. During the penultimate flight the pelvis sinks some 6
to 9 cm; the final stride comes slightly short and quick. Touchdown is far in front of the pelvis, with the shank
tilted roughly 27 to 29 deg back from vertical and the knee almost straight (166 to 170 deg between thigh and shank).
That knee yields by 20 to 30 deg to its lowest point near 141 to 146, and reaches about 173 as the toes leave. Key the
roughly three-frame contact as three beats: touch, yield, drive. Seen from the front, the jumping leg slants under the
body by between 1 and 8 deg. The pelvis on the swinging side surges ahead as the chest rotates against it. Raise the
swinging knee hard, since it supplies part of the lift: jumps off one foot and off two feet from a run end up close in
height, and a lazy swing leg reads feeble. Treat a layup and a high-jump take-off as the same mechanics, and a bound
as running with long airborne phases and a knee that clearly yields; the measurements for these last cases are few.

### ACT25. Space a standing jump phase by phase
stages: blocking, passes

Give each phase of a clean standing jump its own spacing. The push and the landing are spaced evenly, and so is the
rise back to standing. The crouch, the top and the final stand are eased: arriving at the crouch or at the top, the
last two gaps are equal and each is half the gap before them; leaving the top, the same three gaps run in reverse. A
loose, cartoony version on ones adds more stretch and compression, delayed parts and arm reversals, with halving steps
into the top. Declare the looseness level in the plan. Cartoony timing can reach take-off in about half the 21 frames
a realistic maximal jump keeps. An even glide through the air, or a single exaggerated stretch frame on the push or on
the drop, is a declared device.

### ACT26. A creature hop is a purposeful action
stages: plan, blocking, passes

A living creature hopping is acting, not bouncing. The order is a moving hold, an anticipation squash leaning away, a
launch frame stretched along the path with the bottom still touching, the flight, the top, a contact stretched and
leaning back along the fall, then a landing squash that also winds up the next hop, or a chosen stop and settle. As
typical timing at 24 fps, give about 4 frames from the squash to take-off, about 6 from the launch to the top and 6
more down to the contact, then 1 frame into the squash and a long settle; for a hop of another height, take the flight
time from that height under the shot's one gravity (ACT10) and split it evenly about the top. The landing squash is
much smaller than the anticipation squash, and the anticipation is sized to the hop it promises. Heights and timings
are chosen, not decayed like a dropped ball, and a creature that has a tail keeps itself upright instead of rolling.
Each landing holds three or four squashed frames, deepest one or two frames after the contact.

### ACT27. Hop rhythm comes from intent
stages: plan, blocking

Never repeat matching hops on an even beat. Every beat has a reason for its moment and its size. A reflex gives a
quick skip with little ground time and a light push; a doubt gives a press that holds; a commitment gives a stronger
push; the wish to halt gives the final small hop. No two neighbouring beats share their air time, height or ground
time, and all of them share one gravity. Draw a beat chart in the plan with a bar for each stretch on the ground and
each stretch in the air, labelled with its frames and height, and read it at the blocking check. When retiming, keep
contacts that were already approved where they are.

For a creature that hops, its lowest squash after landing and its coil ahead of every jump get keys of their own at
the blocking stage; leaving them to in-betweens loses the choices the creature makes there. Moving one frame from the
following airborne phase into a brief press can change a landing that reads as sluggish into a clear moment of doubt.

### ACT28. Read a jump on its trail before calling it done
stages: passes, checks, self_review

Trace the root, head and feet frame by frame. A gap in the dots is a pop. A straight run where the motion should
curve, or an inverted arc on a drop, is a fault; fix the drop by pushing the root of that frame further along its
direction of travel. Letting a compression go across two frames feels odd unless a snap is wanted, and a landing
spread over three frames that needs two feels soft. Sharpen an even follow-through by taking frames out of the
compression. Confirm even spacing on the rise, no twinned feet, no two-frame pop, and only tiny foot adjustments after
landing. Apply each correction fully in a single change, then re-trace the nearby arcs, since a fix disturbs its
neighbours.

### ACT29. Game jumps and landings
stages: plan, blocking

Jumps the player does not control, and cinematic jumps, keep a readable wind-up; everything below applies to the
player jump. Its arc height is one named value the game can tune.

The landing is a clip of its own, with variants for the height of the fall and for landing into a walk or a run. Push
its impact lower than real to sell the weight, declared, so that one or two frames of detection delay and the blend
still leave it visible, and extend the legs before the contact.

The take-off has no crouch keys, since a visible crouch before an action the player repeats constantly feels slow; the
body starts to rise one frame after the button press. Its opening frame is a crouch pushed past what a real body could
do, declared as a pose that exists only inside the blend so the checks leave it alone; the previous clip blends into
it, it is never seen on its own, and limbs and props trail behind it, against the jump. In the air, poses follow the
arc, stretched while rising and legs reaching down while falling, and a falling loop plays until the landing
interrupts it.

### ACT30. Climbs and vaults at standard heights
stages: plan, blocking

In a game the climbable heights are agreed with design as fixed metrics, and each height or band gets its own climb or
mantle. On the grab frame the hands meet the edge exactly at its listed height, starting from the agreed distance.
Clips that are blended together keep identical timing and pose order, so their keys and contacts fall on matching
frames. When a height sits between two climbs that differ a lot, cover it with two variants, each matched to blend
with one neighbour.

### ACT31. Sit to stand: fold fast, unfold slow
stages: plan, blocking

A natural rise from a seat at knee height takes about 38 to 50 frames; under 24 reads as a jump up and is declared.
The trunk folds to about 35 deg from vertical (30 when quick, 45 when slow; beyond 50 is kept for a body that is weak,
heavy or on a low seat, and is declared). Its angular speed while folding is roughly twice its speed while unfolding,
typically near 115 deg/s down and 60 deg/s up: the dive is quick and the straightening spreads over the push up. This
describes the trunk's speed in a natural rise; an acted rise may still give the whole fold phase more frames than the
rise as a timing choice, declared in the plan. The centre of mass travels
forward first, about 0.20 to 0.30 m, then up about 0.24 m, its path turning from forward to up as the hips leave the
seat. Sideways sway stays within about 3 to 4 cm. A rise that lifts vertically from the start, or sways, looks false.

### ACT32. The feet stay planted through the rise
stages: blocking, checks

Before rising, the feet slide back so the heels sit at or slightly behind the knees. As the hips leave the seat the
shins tip about 9 to 13 deg past vertical, with the knees near 80 to 105 deg and the hips near 90. The moment of
leaving the seat is a transfer of weight: the heels stay down throughout an ordinary rise. A shin that is upright at
that moment means the feet are too far forward, so the body must fold deeper or push with its hands.

### ACT33. Arms and head during the rise
stages: blocking, passes

Free arms drift forward 10 to 20 deg with the fold as a counterweight, then lower as the body straightens; they never
swing behind the chest. A body that is tired, heavy, old or sitting low presses on its thighs, the armrests or the
seat edge; the hands stay down while they bear weight and roll off the fingertips once the legs take over. With a load
in the hands there is nothing to push with, and the trunk fold and the legs must do the whole job. The head stays
nearly level while the chest pitches: the neck extends so the face and eyes stay on the destination, and a small nod
may start the move. A head that drops chin to chest reads as heavy or ill.

### ACT34. Choose how a character leaves a seat
stages: plan, blocking

A young, fit, unhurried body leaves with momentum: the hips lift while the centre of mass is still slightly behind the
heels, carried by the forward fold. An old, careful, heavy or hurt body stabilises first, bringing the chest over the
toes, then lifts. Choose one per character and keep it.

The seat, the load and the body also change the rise. A low sofa needs a quicker, deeper fold, with the feet drawn
further back and often a hand push; a bar stool needs almost no fold. A box held close means less fold and a slower
rise; a bag hanging away widens the stance. A heavy body widens and pulls back its feet and uses its hands, and a
tired body folds deeper on each later rise and sits down a little harder.

### ACT35. Sit to walk: the foot leaves when the body is nearly up
stages: blocking, checks

The weight begins moving onto the stance leg at the moment the hips leave the seat. The foot that will step stays down
while the body unfolds; its heel comes up about seven frames later and its toes leave about 10 to 11 frames after the
seat, close to full height. Never release the stepping foot while the trunk is still folded or before the shin has
come back toward vertical. A careful body stands fully, pauses about 20 frames, then sets off. The first step is short
and slower, and normal walking returns within two or three steps.

### ACT36. A first step begins with a visible weight shift
stages: blocking

From a still stance a chosen step takes about 9 to 11 frames to prepare. The pressure moves back and toward the
stepping foot, which presses down for an instant, and this sends the mass forward and across onto the stance foot. The
pelvis shifts at least 2 cm toward the stance side with a slight forward lean, and only then does the swing heel peel.
Even a forward step opens with a small sway backward, so the hips never start by moving ahead. A foot never leaves the
ground while the weight is still centred between both feet. When a shove forces the step, this anticipation is absent.
People usually step off with the unloaded leg.

### ACT37. Sitting down is a controlled lowering
stages: blocking, passes

Sitting takes as long as rising or longer, about 2.0 to 2.4 s. The hips travel back and down while the chest tips
forward, and the thigh muscles brake the knees. The seat is lowered onto, not landed on, and the trunk straightens
only once the seat bears the weight. Falling the last part of the way onto the seat is a declared choice for a tired
or careless character.

### ACT38. Getting off the floor is a chain of supports
stages: plan, blocking, passes

Rising from the floor passes through stages: a side sit or a sit-up, all fours or a half kneel, a hand braced on a
knee or the floor, then standing. Each new support lands before the previous one lets go, and all fours is the easiest
launch point at any age. A young body skips stages, usually going from sitting to crouching to standing with at most
one hand on the floor, in roughly 2 to 3 s (an estimate). An older healthy adult most often sits up first, side-sits or
rolls over, uses every stage and takes about 110 to 150 frames: about 6 s on average, within a range of 4 to 12.5 s
(about 100 to 300 frames). At their peaks the hip bends about 90 deg, the knee 118 to 129 and the ankle 29 to 32. A
frail body takes two to three times longer, adds stages (tucking, crouching and kneeling, hands and knees, walking on
hands and feet) and can stall partway, held in a pose that prepares the next stage. A heavy or old body needs its hands
to kneel. When hands load a support before pushing off it, the shoulders lift and draw back while the hands take the
weight, then drop clearly on the push.

Getting down has no measured data, so treat this as a starting point checked by eye: reverse the rise, with more hand
support early. Step back into a half kneel, put a hand on the floor or the front knee, lower the back knee, then go to
a side sit or all fours. Each stage is slower than a fall, and the hands land before the hips drop.

### ACT39. The grip follows the box
stages: blocking, checks

The size of the load decides the grip first, then its weight. A small, light box narrower than the shoulders is held
by its sides: palms pressing in low, fingertips hooked beneath the lower edge, elbows close to the sides, the box near
the belly. A medium or heavier box keeps that side-and-bottom hold with the fingers further under and the box drawn
against the body, often with one hand lower than the other. A box wider than the shoulders is taken at opposite
corners, one hand beneath the closer lower corner and the other on top of the further upper corner, or it is hugged
from below against the chest. A box with handles is held by them, wrists close to straight. A perfectly mirrored
two-hand hold reads staged, and a flat tray of hands under a small box reads wrong.

### ACT40. Keep a heavy load close and turn with the feet
stages: plan, blocking, passes, checks

Body and load move as one mass, and on every frame that combined mass sits over the feet. Once a load passes about 15
% of body mass, its centre (or the hands) stays within about 25 cm forward of a point halfway between the ankles. Held
at arm's length, a load reads very light or its bearer very strong. Knuckle height is the easiest height to carry at;
the floor and above the shoulder are the hard cases. Under load the feet turn or step, and the trunk does not twist. A
heavy tool obeys the same rule and is never raised at arm's length, where nobody could hold it without tipping. Before
it rises, check:

- the body has stepped in to the tool before taking the grip;
- the hands hold it toward its heavy end;
- its mass is drawn close and sits over a wide base;
- the rise opens with a small swing back that starts the mass moving.

When a heavy tool comes down on a target overhead, the body first gets its head moving, then gives the push with a
single sharp drop of the torso forward and down. From there the head picks up speed under its own weight like any
falling object, the arms only guide it, and the body moves out of its way.

### ACT41. Choose the lift from the shape of the load
stages: plan, blocking

When the load fits between the feet, the lifter stands astride it in a half squat, the knee near 70 deg for a skilled
lifter, the back fairly straight. A load too wide for the knees to pass calls for feet set wide, knees bent moderately
and the trunk tipped forward; a deep squat behind it pushes it away. Even a straight-back lift rounds the lower back
about 20 to 30 deg at the grip, keeps that rounding for the early half of the rise, then arches for the later half.
Sequence: feet fixed, the load comes unstuck, knees and ankles straighten first, the hips and chest take over for the
rest, and the head is the last part to come up. A heavier load makes the hips rise early; a weak or tired body moves
more in sequence, an expert more at once. A one-handed pick braces the free hand on the thigh, a knee or an edge, with
the trunk bending and twisting slightly toward the load.

### ACT42. Weight is read from time
stages: plan, blocking, passes

Viewers judge a load mostly from how long the lift takes, and the time from grip to lift-off tells the most. For a
light box (below about 5 % of body mass) allow about 3 to 6 frames between gripping and breaking contact with the
ground; for a heavy one (above about 20 %) allow about 8 to 16. Bringing an ordinary box from the floor up to a stand
takes about 24 to 36 frames. A large box that comes up quickly looks empty, and a strong character raises the same box
in less time. A heavy lift needs set-up time: a deep fold over the load, then a lean back against it as it rises,
knees bent, base wide, loose parts swinging, strain in the face. Setting down is not lifting in reverse: a careful
set-down is slow with knees and back bending together, a casual one ends in a short drop.

### ACT43. Handling has a before and an after
stages: plan, blocking

Most real handling slides, tips, pivots or rolls the object before or after the lift. Add a beat before the lift, such
as sliding it to the edge or tilting it so one hand fits beneath, and a small slide after placing it. A long carry
tires the grip first: regrips, the load shifted against the body, a change of hand.

### ACT44. The carrying body
stages: weight, plan, blocking, passes

A light load is carried upright and smoothly. In a front carry the elbows bend about 80 to 120 deg, tucked low at the
ribs, the upper arms hang near vertical, the shoulders are drawn down and forward, and the load rides snug against the
body between knuckle and elbow height. Elbows sticking out, or a load held away from the body, look light. A heavy
front load tilts the trunk backward at the pelvis, by about 4 to 6 deg for each 10 % of body mass, and deepens the
hollow of the lower back; the tilt of the head shows the lean best, with the head kept over the shoulders. The stride
stays close to the unloaded walk: a heavy carry walks with confidence, never with short careful steps. With no arm
swing, the pelvis and chest move a little more. At the start of a carry the body tips slightly forward, and at the end
it tips back.

A one-hand load such as a bucket tilts the trunk and head away from it, lifts the free arm out from the side, drops the
loaded shoulder and hangs the loaded arm straight, a bag on it swinging like a pendulum. The same weight split between
two hands walks upright. A backpack or any load on the back stops the pelvis and chest twisting, tips the trunk and
head forward and shortens and quickens the steps. A load at the edge of what the carrier can manage may get a declared
struggling carry: slower pace, flexed knees, the passing foot kept low, feet turned out, an irregular rhythm, and still
a committed push-off.

### ACT45. Push by leaning in, pull by sitting back
stages: plan, blocking, checks

For a hard push, stagger the stance so the rear leg works as a strut, the weight forward over the forefeet and the
combined mass ahead of the feet. The lean of the mass line is roughly the angle whose tangent is force over body
weight: about 6 deg at a tenth of body weight, 17 at three tenths, 27 at half. The start is the effort beat, when the
lean is greatest and the hips lowest, at least about 1.3 times the lean that follows; once the load moves, the body
rises slightly and walks behind it. A gentle push on a low handle drives downward as well as forward, from a body that
stays fairly erect. A pull puts the mass behind the heels and walks backward more slowly. A huge lean against a tiny
force reads as falling.

### ACT46. Shape and recovery of a push and a pull
stages: plan, blocking, passes

On the push key, line the body up from the rear toe through the hips and spine into the hands, the head on the same
line, one straight line pointed at the contact. The hands act as a support, with the mass between them and the rear
foot, so taking the object away would make the body fall. Show the force going both ways, into the object and down
through the locked back foot into the floor. Write in the plan where the strength, the mass and the power lie, because
whichever side is weaker gives way: a load heavier than the effort stays put and the pusher slides back, a stronger
pusher moves the load, and against the floor, which cannot give, it is the body that moves. Pick the height at which
the hands meet the load on purpose, then check the body's line again for that height. In the recovery the hands come
away, the body drops forward onto its front foot, and the rear foot steps up only after that foot has taken the
weight. For a pull, plant the feet close to the object, put the mass well behind them, keep the loaded arms straight
in line with the rope, and lean along a single diagonal that runs from the grip down to the rear heel.

### ACT47. Each shove is a cycle
stages: blocking, passes

Treat each shove or heave as one cycle that starts and ends at rest. Before the load gives, the body leans into it and
the effort builds against it; the load moves fastest at the moment it breaks free and slides more steadily after it,
and the cycle closes on the full extension of the arms or the driving leg and a small recoil. The next shove starts
while the previous one recoils, and the steps between shoves are walking steps. Against a load that will not move, the
extension of the rear leg sets the hips and the reach of the arm sets the distance to the hands; hips keyed freely
make the arms overreach. The body commits its weight forward before the arm straightens, and as the limbs extend the
lower back answers, rolling chest and hips back a little. Escalate repeated shoves with a deeper wind-up, a longer
line and a higher rear heel, since equal shoves read as one held pose at small size.

### ACT48. A throw starts in the hips and hands off cleanly
stages: plan, blocking

A throw winds up by stretching its drivers: the throwing arm goes back and the trunk coils. It then unwinds from the
hips through the chest and shoulder into the arm, and the object leaves last. The body bends into a curve on the
throw; on the release, its straight lines mark the balance axis. When a thrown object is handed to a physics
simulation, its speed and spin at release carry on with no jump.

An overhead throw flips the curve of the spine two times: a forward curl, an arch behind the head, and a second
forward curl as the object leaves. Each joint, and the head, arrives at every flip a little behind. If the thrower
dips first to collect itself, the pelvis heads the descent and the arms are the last to fold. Coming back up, the
pelvis initiates, yet the visible lead belongs to the shoulders, with the upper arm next and the arm's swing after
that. Squatting and standing up bend and straighten hip, knee and ankle in unison; while dropping, the trunk pitches
ahead as the arms swing behind.

### ACT49. The phases of a strike
stages: plan, blocking, passes

A strike runs wind-up, swing, hit, settle. The wind-up curls to feed the swing, and the swing leaves it slowly, then
is fastest in the middle. On a contact the hit is the fastest frame, with no in-between just before it; a swing that
stops in the air arrives cushioned. Typical timing gives the wind-up three to four frames to reach the hit at 24 fps
(four to five at 30), a hit hold of about 10 to 12 frames, and intervals between beats that differ by at least two to
one. The final anticipation, a step together with a twist of the trunk, supplies the power. Every strike starts in the
hips; the elbow leads up and down while the hand drags, and the hand or tool arrives a frame after the arm. A
fighter's own wind-up is short and small, while a big readable wind-up is an enemy's warning.

### ACT50. Drive to full extension, then recoil
stages: blocking, passes

Key the end of a punch or a thrust first: a true full extension, where the joints lock or an impact stops it. Then
shape the speed that leads into it: a brief, slow start, top speed reached early, and a steady stretch only a little
below that peak through the middle of the travel. After the extension comes a small recoil, the fist backing off as
the shoulders turn back, then rest. A hard strike turns back one frame past its extreme, and the pose kept on screen
is the bounced one, while a soft accent carries on and cushions. In a combination the next fist starts while the first
returns. A violent strike that stops in mid air hits its overshoot across the widest gap, then returns fast and
settles in halving steps. In a series of chops, each one first gets a commitment key, the weight already moved forward
and the elbow not yet opened, ahead of the arm's extreme. A heavy overhead tool gets its push from one torso drop, as
in ACT40.

### ACT51. Every hit has a contact frame and an accent frame
stages: plan, blocking, checks

Mark two frames on each hit. The contact is where the two parts first meet; on a punch shown without a touching frame,
it is the earliest frame where the fist has gone by and the target has moved. The accent carries the shake, the freeze
and the effects, and its place depends on the hit: on a hard contact it is the rebound, one frame after the contact;
on a whip it is the frame that follows the snap; on a blow that carries through, it is the first frame where the
target is displaced. A punch never holds on the touching frame. To make a hit land harder, take out the final frame in
flight and stretch the shape of the touching frame back across the gap that leaves. A camera jolt on the accent lasts
only a few frames, and the recovery after it is slower.

### ACT52. Nothing reacts before its cause; the struck part moves on contact
stages: blocking, checks

No squash, bend, splash or reaction begins ahead of the frame that causes it. Until then the shape stays as it was in
free motion, though a stretch along the path is allowed. Check every shape channel keyed at both ends of a contact,
because interpolation averages into exactly this mistake. On the contact frame itself, not a frame later, displace the
struck surface or body part a little along the line of the blow, and let it spring back afterward; the shake, freeze
and effects still wait for the accent frame of ACT51. A struck part that does not move on the contact frame is a
fault.

### ACT53. The order of a hit reaction
stages: plan, blocking, passes

A reaction begins the moment the blow peaks, without a pause and without any anticipation. The hit part leads, on the
frame of the hit, and the body follows it in sequence down toward the feet, which can remain planted. The reaction has
two beats: a reflex in the struck part while the head and face keep their old intent, then a look toward the cause and
the emotion. A surprise hit with no wind-up gets a hold so the viewer can register it. In gameplay the look is kept
brief so the player can cut it off; a cutscene may let the look last longer. The attacker's wind-up is sized to the
hit; on a big punch the back turns toward the target, driven by the hips, torso and shoulder.

### ACT54. Knock-downs keep the head arcing
stages: blocking, passes

In a slip or a knock-down the head arcs in after the body. The impact gets two squashed frames (two or three at 30
fps) and the rise afterward is slow. A heavy part may break its arc after a hit to show weight, declared. A heavy
weapon follows an honest arc, with realistic timing and mass, even in broad, stylised action; it never bends or drifts
as if it had no mass.

### ACT60. Take the frame budget first
stages: plan, blocking

For a gameplay move, the hit frame and the overall length come from what the design needs, and they go into the plan
before any pose. The skill lies in the spacing within that budget, never in stretching the budget. Time the medium
attack first and scale the heavy and light versions from it, keeping the response on the frame after the input in
every tier. A quick weapon whose trail has to bend may take many keys, all on a smooth curve.

### ACT56. Player moves answer at once and show weight after
stages: plan, blocking, checks

A player-controlled move changes visibly on the frame after the input, with no dead hold before it; even a start from
rest leans and pushes from that frame. Any wind-up lives in the ready pose or in one or two frames, so the pose carries
much of it. A fighter's own anticipation stays short and small, because a big one is seen and dodged. If the result
must take time, a tell shows immediately (a lean, a turn of the head, a weapon pulled back and kept there) and remains
on every frame up to the result. The weight shows after the action: in the slowdown, an overdone recoil, a final pose
that is held, and the response of whatever was struck. Speed alone makes a move feel empty, and a weapon under player
control starts at full speed with no ease. Author the follow-through in full and open an interrupt window early;
without one, the recovery is the commitment and stays as short as the weight allows. Enemy and non-player moves keep a
readable warning wind-up.

### ACT58. Build combos from clips that cock the next hit
stages: plan, blocking

Each attack in a combination is a separate clip that starts at the combat idle and returns to it, because its trigger
frames move with design. Its recovery already sets up the next attack: the hand or weapon that strikes next ends the
recovery drawn back further than a plain recovery would leave it. The first hit is near-instant; follow-ups may afford
a little more wind-up. The opening pose of a follow-up matches a frame late in the window that triggers it, and no
blend ever stalls or runs backward. A combination travels forward and a little side to side.

### ACT55. Strike into the target zone
stages: plan, blocking, checks

Before keying an attack, get from design the target volumes and the zone each attack aims at, such as the head, chest
or legs. On the hit frame the striking end rests on the target's surface within its volume, never sinking into the
body, and the victim's reaction is chosen by the zone. Next to the hit frame, write the zone and the active range. The
range opens when the striking end first shows inside its zone, and it shuts on the last frame from which a hit that
lands late could still be credited to this pose. Author that range on the same timeline as the interrupt frames and
the effect events.

### ACT59. Size damage to its role in play
stages: plan, blocking

A light hit is a partial reaction layered over whatever the character is already doing, and a dodge or roll can cancel
it. A large hit is a full-body clip that only further damage can interrupt. Mark the frame where control returns as a
design value. Give damage variants by direction and body part at standard contact heights; travel from a hit is a
short stumble moving both feet the same distance in every direction. Where a blend would cross an ugly pose, use a cut
or a transition made for it. A paired action such as a grab is one authored timeline, synced from the contact, with
the hands locked to the partner.

### ACT57. Freeze, shake and effects are engine events
stages: plan, deliver

Plan the hit freeze, the camera shake and the effects on the accent frame as engine events, never as held keys; the
clip itself keeps the recoiled hold. If the game holds the attack pose inside the clip instead, that is a declared
choice, and the attack then needs a separate version for when it connects with nothing, or a miss would stop dead as
if it had hit.

### ACT61. Kicks and cuts plant hard and fast
stages: blocking

An instep kick comes in over two or three steps at an angle of up to about 45 deg, ending in a long stride, and a
support foot beside the ball, a little behind, toes toward the target. The pelvis rotates first and the thigh swings
through while the shin trails, heel folded toward the buttock, before the knee whips straight; from the deepest
backswing to the contact takes two or three frames, and the contact lasts under a frame. The opposite arm swings out
for balance and the support knee straightens a little at contact. A side-step cut at speed plants for 4 to 6 frames
(10 to 12 when slow or surprised), the foot set out wide and ahead, the knee near 15 deg as it lands and 30 to 35
under peak load, the hips sitting into it and the trunk over the plant foot. When the cut is planned, the slowing
happens one step earlier; a startled character has to slow and turn on a single step, which makes it look heavier.

### ACT62. Key the rests and the extension first, and overlap the cycles
stages: plan, blocking

Plan each action as one cycle from rest to rest, and key the two rests and the full extension before anything between
them. The extension is the pose a viewer keeps, so it gets the strongest key of the action and a moment on screen to
register before the next move begins; the approach to it may be quick. Cycles in a series overlap instead of queueing:
the rest that ends one cycle is the first frame of the next, and a second limb sets off while the first is still on
its way back. In a two-fist exchange, as a typical value, the extended fist is back in about 7 frames, and the other
fist reaches its own extension 3 frames after that, about 10 frames after the first one left.

### ACT63. Give every shape an opposite to travel from
stages: plan, blocking, passes

A shape reads through contrast with the shape before it. Start a body curve from its reverse: a lift begins hunched in
one C and ends in the opposite C, and a gesture or a line across the frame travels from one side to the other instead
of sitting in the middle. Just before each extreme, move the part slightly the other way: a little bent before it goes
straight, a little straight before it compresses, a little forward before it bends back. Add this small counter move
when the footage lacks it. A change of expression in the face starts the same way, from the reverse of the shape it is
heading for.
