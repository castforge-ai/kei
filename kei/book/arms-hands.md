# Arms and hands

kind: subject

After the face, the arms and hands carry most of what a character means. These rules cover what each arm is doing,
how arms swing, reach, grip, brace, strike and throw, how fingers bend, and how held props are set up.

### HND1. Every arm has a named job at every key
stages: plan, blocking, self_review

At each key, name what each arm is doing. The jobs are: rest (hanging, or laid on the body, a surface or the other
arm), support (carrying body weight on a surface), carry, balance (out as a counter-mass on a narrow base, in a fold,
in a trip), reach and grasp, gesture, and strike or throw. The hand shape follows the job. A hand left hanging in space
without a job is a habit, not a rest. Arms need not swing all the time: if the acting calls for it, an arm may
hold one pose. Where nothing calls for the free arm, keep it at rest, unless a long reach needs it as a counterweight
or a nearby surface offers it support.

### HND4. The hands are the second acting tool
stages: plan, blocking, self_review

Only the head tells more than the hands, and when the face is simple the hands do the acting. Block first the single
pose that tells the shot best, posed down to the fingertips. On every story pose the fingers are set for the emotion:
spread, curled or gripping. A hand left wide with its fingers fanned shows fear; a hand kept shut and held back shows
cunning or control.

### HND40. The acting hand is read in silhouette, and the other hand stays alive
stages: blocking, passes, self_review

Check each key as a solid black shape. If the acting hand or a prop is lost inside the shape of the body, turn the
body or move the limb so the hand stands clear of the outline. A presenting or pointing hand works on the side of the
torso away from the camera, never crossing in front of the chest. Name the one part that reads in each beat, for
example the gesture arm at about 45 deg with the wrist broken, give it its arc, and set a small counter-move of the
body against it for 2 to 3 frames. Give the other hand some small, slow motion of its own so it is never frozen.

### HND41. Two arms in step only on purpose
stages: plan, blocking, checks

A symmetric two-arm gesture is allowed for authority, declaration, harmony or wholeness, and it is declared. Break it
for a personal or pointed beat. Otherwise loosen the match in one of three ways: set the two arms on different depth
planes, tilt another part of the body, or offset one arm by about 2 frames by default (two to three times that offset
reads as a deliberate, visible delay). Two hands arriving within 2 frames of each other on an undeclared gesture are a
fault to look at.

### HND39. Point with the eye line; gesture around the words
stages: blocking, passes

For a far point, the arm is nearly straight with the elbow slightly bent, the head turns to the target first, and the
fingertip lies on the straight line running from the dominant eye to the target, checked through the camera. For a
near point, the elbow bends and the forearm does the aiming. Gestures happen in front of the torso. Each one is set up
before its word, strikes on the word, holds, and goes home when the thought ends. Leave a gesture out for a few extra
frames before it returns; on the way back the wrist rolls up and the hand cups a little early. Between ideas the hands
rest. Hands loosely joined keep the turn open; hands laid open on the body or a table close it.

### HND26. The shoulder girdle joins the raise, late and modestly
stages: blocking, passes, checks

While the arm rises through its first 30 deg or so, the shoulder blade and collarbone stay almost still. Between 30
and 120 deg the blade turns upward by roughly half as many degrees as the upper arm gains, and over a full raise it
turns up 40 to 50 deg and tips back 20 to 30, mostly past 90. The collarbone lifts only 6 to 10 deg in total and pulls
back 15 to 20, most of it near the top. A low or forward reach therefore brings the shoulder point forward 2 to 3 cm with no lift, and an
overhead reach raises it and swings it back. A real arm tops out near 150 deg against the chest; anything higher needs
the trunk to arch or bend sideways. The shoulder rises with each raise of the arm and sinks with each lowering, and the
two shoulders contrast. A shrug during an ordinary reach is a declared effort or tension; a raised arm on a still
girdle looks like a doll's.

### HND25. Keep an IK limb short of full stretch
stages: blocking, passes, checks

Near full reach a two-bone IK chain amplifies: at about 0.99 of its length, each thousandth of reach turns into about
1 deg of elbow or knee. A limb held at 0.985 to 0.99 of reach therefore jitters with tiny control changes. Hold a
supporting limb nearer 0.97 of its reach so it keeps some bend, and never pin it to one exact near-straight value.

### HND27. An arm is four bones that break in succession
stages: blocking, passes, checks

Treat the arm as four links, collarbone, upper arm, forearm and hand, each turning on its own timing. Onset runs from
the body outward: the shoulder moves, the upper arm follows, and the forearm, wrist and fingers trail one after
another and catch up in the follow-through. Arrival runs the same way: the elbow reaches its new place before the
wrist does, and the wrist before the hand. The hand is therefore both the last part to leave its old position and the
last to settle in the new one; on a rise, the elbow can climb for a frame or more before the hand lifts off. The wrist
bends against the direction of travel, whichever way the arm goes.

Check: if all four links reach their fastest turning on the same frame, the arm moves as a single block; offset them.
A change of arm shape travels down the joints and is never switched on one key. A wave runs this chain in a loop:
upper arm, forearm, hand and fingers, with the fingers closing and spreading so the hand stays loose. A walk uses the
same chain, smaller.

### HND29. Choose the reversal or the whip, on purpose
stages: plan, blocking, passes

Contrast of shape keeps an arm alive: bend it one way on a key and the other way on the next (the forearm hanging from
a raised elbow, then the elbow dropped with the hand up), or go from a curve to a straight line. The same holds on any
chain, base first and tip last. For each arm action, choose between a reversal and a whip in which the wrist snaps
last, and add one deliberate finishing touch. Never add motion at random.

### HND28. When a limb reverses, the parent turns first
stages: blocking, passes, checks

When a limb reverses, the upper segment turns back first and each lower segment keeps travelling its previous way for
a frame or more, so the joint briefly bends against the move. Check it by stepping through the reversal, whichever way
it goes: on the frames where the upper arm has already turned, the forearm is still moving the old way, so the elbow
angle changes against the new direction of the move. Shoulder, elbow, wrist and fingers must not all change direction
together. A realistic rig keeps this counter-bend within the real limits: the elbow opens toward straight and the
wrist bends back no further than it can. Bending past straight belongs to a style, and the plan declares it.

### HND16. Decide how much each action breaks
stages: plan, passes

Decide per action how far the overlap goes: none, a single break, or a full cascade through the chain. A part that
only goes slack, such as fingers loosening once a grip lets go, or a part coming to rest with nothing driving it, gets
a simple in-between that slows into its end, with no extra flourish. If the overlap is what a viewer sees before
anything else, there is too much of it; it should support the move, never show off.

### HND15. The hand has weight
stages: blocking, passes

A hand is never lowered flat along a straight line. The wrist leads and the fingers hang behind. Before a grab the
hand opens as its anticipation, then settles on the contact. A part that was pressing before it moves, such as a
finger held against the thumb or a palm pushed on a desk, springs off with a big first gap, as if released. When a
part swings back through empty air with nothing to push against, its far end trails behind on the frames between keys.

### HND42. An arm over the head clears it on a planned arc
stages: blocking, passes

When an arm arc breaks because it lacks breakdowns, delete the faulty middle keys. Build one new breakdown in their place, read
from the reference in a side or a front view, set high enough that the hand clears the head. A hand may pass through
the head only where the shot camera hides it. The arm speeds toward the top of its arc, slows across it, and speeds away again. To make the
curve tighter, bend the elbow and turn the upper arm together on one key, which breaks the arm.

### HND2. Free arms answer the trunk
stages: blocking, passes

Free arms are driven by the body and never start a move; each kind of trunk move gets its own answer. A shift of
weight from one foot to the other lifts the hands a little away from the body as a balance, and they drop again once
the weight has arrived. A turn throws them outward; they swing past the body, fall back in and settle. A sideways
slide of the hips leaves them hanging and trailing behind. Whatever the move, the hands come to rest later than the
pelvis and the chest, and they never stay pinned against the thighs. In a walk each arm swings against the leg on its
own side and shows where the body came from and where it goes. Arms held out from the body, elbows bent and hands near
hip height, belong to a body that has not solved its balance yet, like a toddler's.

### HND30. The walk swing: upper arm on the contact, hands widest just after
stages: blocking, passes, checks

The upper arm hits its extreme on the contact pose, within about a frame of the other foot's heel strike. A plain
realistic swing is small, about 25 to 30 deg of upper arm peak to peak, centred near vertical. The elbow is never
straight: it holds about 20 to 35 deg of bend and swings about 15 deg more, so the hand mostly rides along with the
upper arm. The forearm drags behind it, so the distance between the hands peaks from the contact to about 3 frames
after it, never before. At the down, the back upper arm is already swinging forward while its forearm still goes
back. The hand is lowest at the passing. Build this drag into the blocking poses so it reads even on stepped keys. If
the arm reads too big, cut the forearm range first. Judge the drag through the shot camera first; only then tidy the
curves. Larger, cartoon swings are declared. When one arm is too bent on a key, fix it on the key before, then compare
both arms on the same poses.

Arms hanging straight look sad. Elbows held at 60 to 90 deg suggest a power walk, anger, fear or a run. Real swing is
a little uneven between sides. The arms cancel the twist of the legs: arms that are held, carrying or in pockets stop
the chest from counter-turning and quicken the step slightly, and arms locked while the hips still twist look
mechanical. At a slow stroll, chest and pelvis turn nearly together; a clear counter-twist only shows at a normal pace
or faster.

### HND31. A reversed elbow is a style choice
stages: plan, passes

Bending the elbow backward is a declared style, never the default. In a loose or stylised swing the elbow may break
backward for about 4 frames around the middle of the forward swing, so the forearm and hand drop behind the line of
the upper arm. Played, it should read as suppleness; frozen on any single frame, the arm must not look snapped. Bones
keep their length, and each limb stays a set of straight segments that bend only at the joints. A realistic walk keeps
the elbow bent throughout, with the forearm dragging a frame or two behind. Looseness in a gangly character comes from
a chain of joint breaks, while the body keeps its weight.

### HND46. A pendulum-arm idle bobs twice per swing
stages: blocking, passes

In a guarded stance idle with one arm swinging like a pendulum, count the vertical bobs of the whole body in one
complete swing of that arm: there are two. The other, held arm stays still, and the swinging arm's cycle sets the loop
length.

### HND17. A reach moves the body before the arm
stages: plan, blocking, passes

The eyes reach the target 1 to 3 frames before the hand leaves, and the head follows. In a fast standing reach the hips
and legs set themselves 2 to 3 frames before the arm. Reaching down to the floor, the knees and the head move first,
then the trunk, and the wrist last. A target close enough to touch leaves the trunk nearly still: the chest covers no
more than roughly 15 % of the distance the hand covers, twists about 4 deg toward the target, and the shoulder point slides forward 2 to
3 cm. A target past arm's length, or a hand carrying weight, gets the trunk moving together with the hand or ahead of
it, and the trunk keeps settling 1 to 3 frames after the hand stops. A limb never lengthens to cover the distance
(lean, step or rise to the toes instead), unless a stretch style is declared.

### HND19. Inside the arm, the shoulder and elbow lead a reach
stages: blocking, passes

The shoulder starts, the elbow carries forward a frame later, and the forearm and hand stay folded back until the
elbow reaches its peak; only then do they extend, opening out toward the target. Build this into the breakdown:
between two clean keys, pose the arm curled with the hand still behind, and it unrolls when played. Spend the full
version where it helps the shot most.

### HND18. A reach has one speed peak and a long brake
stages: blocking, passes, checks

The hand travels a nearly straight path with a slight bow. Its speed rises once and falls once. For a grasp the peak
comes early, at about 30 to 55 % of the reach, and the last half is a slow closing brake: in a 24-frame pick-up the hand
is fastest around frames 9 to 10. For a strike or a throw the peak comes later and there is no brake at the end.

### HND20. A grasping hand opens early, widest late, and closes fast
stages: blocking, passes, checks

The hand begins to open almost as soon as the arm sets off, reaches half its opening about a third of the way through,
and is widest at about 60 to 80 %. Its widest opening is the object's width plus about 20 to 50 mm: wider when the
reach is quick, careless or unsure or the object is fragile, narrower when the reach is slow and precise. It closes
over about the last 4 frames, across the last 5 cm of travel, while the arm brakes, and touches the object as the arm
stops or just after. For a 20-frame reach: opening from about frames 3 to 5, half open near frame 7, widest near frames
14 to 16, and shut around the object during the final 4. When the hand is at its widest on contact, or starts closing
too early in the approach, it reads wrong.

### HND21. Pose a contact from the arm that arrives
stages: blocking, passes, self_review

Before solving a hand onto the body or a prop, pose the arriving arm in its comfortable range (about 70 to 95 deg at the
elbow for a hand at chest height, the wrist near neutral) and see where the palm lands on its own. Choose the side of
the contact from that approach: an arm coming from below holds from under or beside, one coming from above lands on
top. Solve both the position and the facing of the palm, with a limit on the elbow. Then judge the contact close up from
two angles and look hard at that arm's elbow and wrist. A contact met by a cramped arm, a folded elbow or a cranked
wrist is a wrong pose, however close the hand sits.

### HND8. Touching and pressing look different in the whole body
stages: blocking, passes

With the face hidden, a viewer must still tell a touch from a press, and the difference runs through the whole body.
Read a real press from the ground up. As the push grows, the body sinks lower and leans further over, and once the
push is more than the floor's grip can hold, the feet slide. The trunk puts its weight onto the pushing arm, that arm
locks straight, the wrist lifts, and the contact itself flattens or bends back. A soft surface gives under the hand
and the fingers fold. A light touch shows none of this: the hand stays rounded and the body upright. A pull is the
same arrangement turned around: feet planted, loaded arms straight, the trunk leaning away on a long diagonal, the
strain showing in the head.

### HND22. A bracing hand keeps the wrist out of end range
stages: blocking, passes, checks

A flat palm under an upright forearm needs about 90 deg of wrist bend, past the normal 70, and real people avoid it.
Instead the heel of the palm goes down, fingers pointing ahead, with the forearm leaning out over it, or the fingers hook
over an edge, so the wrist stays at about 30 to 70 deg. A palm bearing weight loads its heel first and most. The fingers
rest flat, the pads touching lightly, the tips flat or a little bent back. More weight spreads the fingers a little and
presses the tips; a light touch keeps the rest curl with only the pads down. On the thighs, the hands sit just above the
knees with the elbows slightly bent and pointing out, straight under real weight. Against a wall, use the forearm, or
the palm near shoulder height with a bent elbow. A hand on the floor beside the feet makes a tripod: keep the centre of
mass inside it. A support hand stays planted until the weight has left it. A push-up or handstand palm is the declared
exception.

### HND23. Hands pushing off a seat stay down as long as they can
stages: blocking, passes

In a push to stand, the hands stay on the seat or the thighs until the body is about halfway up, rocking forward over
the fingertips as a pivot, the arms close to but never past full stretch. Straighten the push arms by raising the hips,
not by moving the hands. Place the hands with keys; no constraint is needed for a brief push.

### HND24. Leaving a support: switch cleanly, then let the arm drag
stages: blocking, passes, checks

To hand an arm from IK to FK, key the whole arm on the last IK frame and on the switch frame, match the FK arm to the IK
pose on the switch frame, delete any old FK keys that would fight the matched pose, and then set the next FK key where
the arm's following pose falls. When both hands lift off a support, stagger them by one frame and check the first one
for over-stretch. Step through every switch frame: a small pop there is common. The arm then leaves as overlap: the
upper arm leads, the arm straightens a little to drive the hand, the wrist drags and the fingers curl to clear the
surface. A hand that seems stuck in the air is fixed at its parent, the upper arm.

### HND32. Power starts in the hips and arrives in the hand
stages: plan, blocking, passes

A forceful arm move goes from the most folded body the action allows to the most extended one. The force passes
outward through the hips, the torso, the shoulder, the elbow and the hand, which comes last and fastest. Read the
order of a strike by where each part reaches its top speed: the head turns to aim, the chest and shoulders peak next,
then the upper arm, the forearm and finally the tip of whatever the hand holds, the arm and its object whipping
through in only a few frames at the end. Space these peaks about one frame apart, often exactly one; a heavier held object, or a harder blow, widens the gaps. Never let every part reach a pose on the same frame,
including the recovery pose.

### HND35. A punch is a push of the body
stages: plan, blocking, passes

In a straight punch the rear leg drives until the knee is nearly straight, the hips and shoulders turn, and the elbow
extends only in the last 3 to 5 frames. The fist travels straight forward. From the first body move to contact takes
about 8 to 10 frames; from the forearm's launch, 3 to 5. A hook has a larger counter-move and a flat curved fist path,
taking about 11 to 12 frames, with more speed at the fist. A lone power punch starts with a wind-up, a small dip of the
fist against a counter-move. Combinations skip it, at about 8 to 11 frames per punch including the return. The fist
comes back to guard along a slightly changed path while the other hand holds its guard, and the elbow never locks past
straight.

### HND36. An overarm throw is a short whip at the end of a long chain
stages: plan, blocking, passes

A full throw steps long toward the target, about 0.8 of body height, the lead foot pointing at the target or slightly
closed. The span from the front foot landing to the release is only about 3.5 frames: the hips reach their fastest
turn near frame 1, the chest near frame 2, the layback (forearm laid far back behind the head) near frame 3, and the
ball leaves about half a frame later. The upper arm stays near level with the shoulder line; the arm looks high because
the trunk leans forward (about 30 to 55 deg at release) and toward the glove side. The elbow is near 90 at layback and
still about 25 at release, never locked. The free arm points at the target during the stride, then pulls in to the
chest as the chest rotates; left hanging, it makes the throw look weak. The pelvis visibly leads the chest. If the
chest rotates together with the pelvis, the throw has no power. A casual toss has less layback, less trunk and a
shorter step. An underhand toss swings like a pendulum from the shoulder with a nearly straight elbow, releases in
front of the hip and steps on the opposite foot.

### HND34. A held object is one more link in the chain
stages: blocking, passes

Treat whatever the hand holds as a further link past the hand. At the end of a strike the arm reaches its full length
while the wrist keeps its cocked angle for about one frame; the wrist then snaps, and the held object catches up after
it. Land the wrist on its mark first, and let the hand and the object land after it.

### HND33. Into an accent or an impact, accelerate and cushion afterward
stages: blocking, passes, checks

Any accent, whether a blow, a take, a pointing hand, a stressed word or the effort key of a lift or a pull, sits on
the widest gap of its approach. Grow each gap to roughly twice the one before, so the final step is about twice the
previous one and typically 40 to 55 % of the whole approach, with no slowing just ahead of the accent. For a strike
that ends on a contact, speed climbs all the way in: the frame before contact is either skipped or holds one wide
in-between. Cushioning belongs on the far side: bunch the in-betweens as the hand leaves the hit and as it settles
into the wound-up pose, never as it closes on the contact. A slow-in onto a hit is a fault. When a forearm comes down
onto a target, its elbow starts down while the hand is still travelling up.

### HND37. In repeated effort, the body commits before the arm
stages: plan, blocking, passes

For a cycle of shovelling, planing a board, scrubbing a floor, rowing or splitting logs, set a commitment key in which
the weight has already moved forward while the elbow is still bent, ahead of the arm's extreme. The shoulder swings
forward a moment before the arm straightens, so the arm extends against that momentum. The working stroke is the
shorter part of the loop, about 40 % of the cycle, and the return takes the rest. The return needs only simple
in-betweens, with the tool trailing the body slightly. At the turnaround the chest reverses before the tool head does;
on a downward stroke the hips travel back as the shoulders fall forward, and the largest gap of the cycle is the one
into the impact.

### HND38. A heavy lift by the arms is led by the shoulders
stages: blocking, passes

When the arms lift something heavy, the shoulders, and often the head, move first: the shoulders shrug and pull on
straight arms, and the elbows bend only afterward. The head can also tilt back at the very start. Between one pair of
keys, more than one lead order can work: sketch two small versions and pick one.

### HND3. No key ever ships with default hands
stages: blocking, passes, self_review

The hands are posed on all keys of all deliveries, and that includes blocking. The relaxed default: fingers near their
rest, the little finger curled a touch more than the others, and the thumb posed softly, never set rigid and straight.
Copy that relaxed hand to every key where the hand has no action. A grip closes only as far as the reference shows,
judged from a view that sees it. On a rig without fingers, the hand pose is the wrist set off its zero, in an easy
line with the forearm.

### HND5. Break the hand, never move it as one block
stages: passes

When a hand changes shape, one finger leads before the rest, the thumb moves back against them, and the opening or
folding has a small bump of its own. Never set every finger to the same bend on one frame. Neighbouring fingers share
a motion by a fraction, never by an identical value.

### HND6. A free finger bends most at its middle joint
stages: blocking, passes, checks

With nothing touching the finger, the end joint follows the middle joint and stays below it: typically 0.55 to 0.8
of the middle joint's angle (lower for the index and ring, higher for the middle and little). Never more than 1. When
the tip out-bends the middle joint in free air, the finger looks clawed and tense. When the tip stays straight over a
bent middle joint, the finger looks splinted. The share rises with the size of a held object, from about 0.6 on a thin
rod to about 0.9 on a large jar.

### HND7. A pressed fingertip bends back at the tip
stages: passes, checks

Push on the pad of a finger, as in pinching, pressing a key or resting a fingertip on a desk, and the last joint goes
flat or a few degrees back, typically 0 to -10 deg, while the middle joint stays bent at 20 to 50 deg. This is what
sells pressure. It is correct: do not straighten it, and do not straighten the middle joint to match it.

### HND9. Fingers are coupled to their neighbours
stages: passes, checks

The thumb and the index move almost freely. The ring finger is the least independent. When a ring or little finger
curls alone, its neighbours follow by about a fifth to a third of its angle. Curling the middle finger pulls the ring
along by roughly a quarter; curling the index barely moves the others. Fast finger motion drags the neighbours more
than slow motion. A hard press with one finger shows the neighbours leaning a little toward the surface. A ring finger
kept straight while the middle and little fingers fold fully is effort or a stylised sign; a real counting hand shows
the neighbours half raised. Treat these coupling amounts as rough.

### HND10. A relaxed hand curls when the wrist cocks back
stages: passes, checks

In a relaxed hand, the finger tendons have a fixed length across the wrist. Bending the wrist back curls the fingers;
dropping it forward opens them toward straight and a little spread. As a rough guide, cocking the wrist back by 40 deg
adds about 20 deg at the knuckle, 28 at the middle joint and 12 at the tip to the rest curl, and a wrist dropped 50 deg opens
the fingers to near 0 to 10 deg. A palm turned up curls about a fifth more than neutral; a palm turned down hangs
straighter. A strongly bent wrist combined with tightly curled fingers no longer looks relaxed: it looks like a
deliberate clutch.

### HND11. The palm cups around round things
stages: passes

The arch across the knuckles deepens by about 10 deg when the hand holds a lid or a ball, and the ring and little
finger bones fold in toward the palm. Keep the palm flat for a flat press or an open gesture. Cup it about halfway when
the hand holds a ball or takes a jar lid from above, and nearly fully to scoop water. Held flat against something
round, the palm looks like a paddle.

### HND12. The grip follows the size of the object
stages: blocking, passes, checks

Never use one curl for every object. Size decides the grip more than shape: grip a square bar or the edge of a box as
if it were a round rod as thick as that edge. Typical bends (knuckle, middle, tip joint): a 10 mm rod about 65-75,
95-110, 50-65 deg; a broom handle near 30 mm about 55-70, 80-100, 45-60; a 60 mm bottle about 40, 48, 35; a 120 mm jar
every joint only 12 to 32. On thin handles the middle joint takes the bend; on a large jar the little finger bends
least. In an everyday grasp the index bends less than the two outer fingers, and the thumb bends only about 20 deg at
each joint.

Choose the grip type by the job: a wrap with the thumb opposed for a handle or a bottle, a side pinch for a card or a
key, a three-finger pinch for small round things, a disc grip around a lid, and for carrying a bag, a hook made of
bent fingers with the thumb free and the wrist straight. Most everyday objects weigh under half a kilogram: relaxed
wrist, loose fingers. A strong grip is strongest on a handle about 3 to 5 cm thick; a thick object is held looser.

### HND13. A pinch is made at the thumb tip
stages: passes, checks

In a tip pinch on a small object (2 to 30 mm), the index bends about 41-49 deg at the knuckle, 45-54 at the middle
joint and about 40 at the tip, whatever the size; the thumb tip bends about 47 deg while its knuckle stays near 14. The
relaxed thumb is the reverse, bent at its knuckle and nearly straight at its tip, so the visible change in a pinch is at
the thumb tip. In a side pinch the thumb pad meets the side of the index and the thumb base barely moves. When the
whole hand clamps a handle, the thumb base swings in toward the palm, up to roughly 25 deg, and the thumb folds over.
An object held in the fingertips gets an opposed thumb with a straight tip; one held deep in the palm gets a less
opposed thumb that bends and wraps.

### HND14. Hand shapes by job, and fingers in motion
stages: blocking, passes

These shapes are built from observation and carry low confidence. A punching fist: all knuckles near 90 deg, the
fingers fully folded into the palm, the thumb across the first two fingers, the wrist straight in line with the
forearm, the first two knuckles leading. A gripping fist: fingers round the object, thumb over them, wrist a little
back and turned toward the little finger by about 20 to 40 deg. A softly closed hand adds a third to a half of a full
fist to the resting curl, with the thumb lying along the index. A point: the index straight, the other three curled
together, the thumb over the middle finger or next to it. A hand opened palm up to the viewer shows a visible curl,
most in the little finger, with the fingers fanned a little.

In a walk the hands keep the rest curl with the palm toward the thigh, opening slightly as the wrist trails and curling
as it catches. Fingers lag the wrist by about one frame per joint, knuckle first and tip last. When the wrist flicks
down, the fingers hold open for one or two frames, then catch up and overshoot into a curl beyond rest.

### HND43. Declare every prop attached or free
stages: rig, plan, blocking

Before blocking a shot with a prop, decide how it lives. A prop that stays in one hand and barely moves in it is
parented to that hand's prop joint while it is held and gets no keys of its own. A prop that moves in the hand or
passes between hands is its own animated object on its own control. Write every grip, release, hand swap and stowing
frame into the plan as an event, and match the switch at each hand-over. A held prop must not slide in the grip. Every
pick-up and set-down adds switch frames to match and check, so a prop handled many times is a large share of the
shot's effort: budget it in the plan.

### HND44. A carried object shows its next use
stages: plan, blocking

The way an object is carried says what it will do next. A long object held pointing backward, with the hand simply
hanging at the side, reads odd. If the object is meant to trail, reach the carrying arm back behind the body, held
straight, and keep the far end low, so the character arrives already in the position from which the next use starts.

### HND45. A hand that holds another body follows that body
stages: rig, blocking, checks

For a throw, a hold or a finisher, author both characters on one timeline and play them in sync from the contact. Drive
the gripping hand by IK to a target parented to the part it holds, key that target relative to the part, and switch the
constraint's weight up when the hand grabs and down when it lets go, matching the pose at each switch. During the hold,
the hand must stay on the held part through all of its motion.

### HND47. On a shared body, rest poses leave clearance
stages: rig, plan, blocking, checks

When one animation drives several bodies or outfits, author it on the largest body. Angle the resting arms slightly
out from the torso so they clear it, declare that angle, and keep carried gear and pouches off the paths of the arms:
gear worn on the back is safest. Look at the idle in front and side views, on both the biggest and the smallest
body, for any forearm or hand cutting the hip, a belt or a prop. On a single fixed body, the natural rest pose
applies.
