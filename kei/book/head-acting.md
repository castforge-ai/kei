# Head, eyes, breath and acting

kind: subject

How the head and neck aim and settle, how a body breathes, and how thought, attitude and phrasing turn moving parts
into a performance. Kei's rigs usually have no working eyes, so the head carries every look. Lip sync, dialogue and
facial animation are outside this topic for now.

The rules follow the order a shot needs them: who the characters are, how the shot splits into phrases, which
attitude each phrase holds, the thought behind each act, how the head looks and moves inside the body, the breath,
and finally the passes and checks.

### HED61. Cast characters as contrasts
stages: brief, plan, blocking

Two characters in tension show their traits more clearly than two who agree. When characters share a shot, let each
want something different and carry an opposed energy, for example a nervous one beside a calm one, a fussy one beside
a careless one, or a talkative one beside a silent one. The difference shows in their pace, their stance and how large
they move. In an acting beat for two, give the partner an energy that works against what the main character needs, so
its task gets visibly harder. Relative size and particular bits of business belong to the casting too.

### HED62. The character believes its situation
stages: plan, self_review

Play every beat as true to a character who takes its situation seriously. Remove any clever idea that laughs at the
character from the outside or that makes its world hard to believe. Broad, comic business can sit inside a sad or
tender beat, provided the character is sincere in doing it. When a physical gag hits the character, follow it
with a clear moment showing its opinion of what happened (a hold, a look, a close) instead of piling on one more gag.

### HED63. Every beat has a physical and an inner layer
stages: plan, passes, self_review

The physical layer is effort, gravity and the load. The inner layer is the character being alive: it looks, touches,
prepares and decides, with its mind steering the body. A beat with only the physical layer can be correct and still
dead. Once the weight and timing checks pass, ask about the inner layer as a separate question. The inner force
motivates the physical one, and the character's energy level, strong, tired or sick, changes every pose and every
timing.

### HED41. Personality shows in the reaction
stages: plan, blocking

Character is revealed by how someone responds when things go wrong or shift. If a shot has to establish who the
character is, put an obstacle in its way and give most of the time to the response. Sketch how two or three different
people would respond, then use the response unique to this one. Each event that affects the character earns a
response of its own, long enough to read, and the response matches the character's nature.

### HED48. Write each phrase as an action and a thought
stages: plan

Cut the shot into phrases, each carrying one idea, told one after another. List the beats, then choose which
adjacent beats join into a single phrase; making that choice is what phrasing means. In the plan, write each phrase as its
action plus the thought behind it, in the character's own words. For each group of actions decide whether it is one
thought or several; the answer decides where holds and overlaps go. Do not block a phrase until its thought is written.

### HED51. One pose per attitude, the key attitudes first
stages: plan, blocking

The phrasing sheet gets one numbered pose per attitude, and every one reads on its own; a walk or any cycle counts
as one. The mechanical keys of a move are not phrasing; they belong to blocking. Pose the key attitudes first and add
the sub-actions only once those read. A breakdown exists only to shape the travel between its keys. If it introduces
another idea or feeling, it is really a key, and must earn its place among the attitudes or go.

### HED50. Attitude, not pauses, marks a phrase
stages: plan

In a silent shot, follow the line of emotion through the changes of attitude. A new phrase, with a new main pose,
begins only where the mood changes. A pause with no change of attitude does not start a phrase. An attitude that turns
in the middle of a thought changes the pose right there, without a pause.

### HED53. Every shot contrasts slow and quick
stages: plan, blocking, checks

The brief sets the register: fast, pushed timing plays comic, and a slow pace needs subtler acting. Whatever
the register, each shot needs at least one slow, smooth passage set against a fast, sharp one, and the plan labels each phrase
with its texture. The texture switches on the same frame where the attitude switches. Avoid even timing: a long, slow lead-in
before a big accent lands harder than a quick look followed by a quick reaction, which is the same texture twice.

### HED54. A sustained idea alternates unequal textures
stages: plan, blocking

Over a long continuous idea, such as a search or a slow approach, alternate between two clearly different textures.
Give the phrases different lengths too. One steady pace all the way through is a fault. Make the difference large
enough to read at full speed, and judge it by eye on the stepped playback. Even inside one thought, the timing can
change several times. Let each phrase differ from its neighbour in both speed and size. When the reference or the
soundtrack keeps an even beat, break it on purpose into uneven phrases.

### HED49. Inside one thought actions overlap, between thoughts they pause
stages: plan, blocking, passes

Within one thought, let every action begin while the one before is still finishing: the first step before the character has fully
stood, the reach before the arrival. Keep the first action's readable extreme on screen only as long as it needs,
and launch the second before the first has finished its settle; locate the hook-up frame where one becomes the other,
and act the sequence out to feel its flow. Overlapping shows that the actions share one motive, so two actions with one intention never run as
hold, move, hold, move, with a full stop between them. Between two thoughts, leave a readable beat, a hold or a look,
which tells the audience a new thought has begun.

### HED52. Plan an acting beat from a short menu of gestures
stages: plan, blocking

An acting beat uses few attitudes, each held about 12 to 30 frames, so the character stays one consistent person.
Choose its gestures on paper before blocking. Let one gesture run across several keys instead of starting a new one on
every key. A stock gesture may appear once or twice if this character would truly make it. To find the gestures,
write in one line what the beat says, describe how this character normally behaves, and note how the beat departs
from that. Draw up a menu of six to ten small, everyday gestures that belong to this character, and keep only the few
that the line needs. For each gesture kept, settle how long it lasts, how much it still moves while held, and whether
it begins on screen or is already in progress at the first frame.

### HED38. An attitude must read in one shape
stages: plan, blocking, self_review

Each acting pose must read as one clear shape made by the full figure and the angle of the head. If a beat only works
with a delicate nuance or a tight framing, it needs more work. Test it small and with the face hidden. Sketch the head
at several angles and choose whichever shows the attitude best, even when that means dropping a favourite pose. A
figure with the right attitude needs little animation. Where a nod, a turn or a posture can carry the meaning, animate
it instead of leaning on words, sized for the camera distance, and give each critical beat a moment of its own.

### HED37. Displeasure closes the figure, surprise opens it
stages: blocking, passes

On a displeased beat the head sets the read, so place it first. Bring it low and forward, but stop before it drops or
turns so far that the camera loses the attitude. Lifting the chin on such a pose shifts the read toward threat or
command; do it only when that is the intent. The rest of the figure then follows the head inward for anger,
displeasure and brooding: shoulders up and drawn together, arms near the body. A shock or a take does the opposite
and spreads the figure out. Check each key of such a beat for the direction of the whole shape, inward or outward,
before any detail.

### HED36. An emotion takes one clear posture direction
stages: plan, blocking

Pride goes up and open: head slightly back, chest expanded, arms raised or hands on hips. Sadness bends both the neck
and the upper back, never only the head, and lets the arms hang. Hot anger leans or moves forward. Fear goes back, with
a sideways lean of the trunk. Joy lifts the chest and lowers the shoulders; raised shoulders read as tension or fear.
Tiredness pushes the head forward. Pushing a sad pose bigger does not make it read better, though exaggeration helps
the other emotions.

Build the body set first (shoulders, spine, head, arms, feet), then add one detail that separates the feeling from
its neighbour, since sad and tired share most of a pose. Sad timing stays the same in any style: slow moves,
long holds, loose parts hanging limp. How big the pose is depends on the style.

Let the shapes of the whole body agree with the feeling, placed on two scales. The edge scale runs from soft to
sharp: soft, rounded curves read as kindness, straight lines and angles read as aggression, and spiky points at the
sharp end read as horror. The mass scale runs from closed to open: a compact, gathered body at the closed end reads
as something small or sly, and an open, stretched body at the open end reads as something big.

### HED40. An attitude keeps its strength until the story changes it
stages: blocking, passes, self_review

An angry or excited attitude stays at full strength until the story gives a reason to relax it. A fighting stance
that is held keeps a little bounce. Across the span where an attitude is declared, the main pose values (how deep the
crouch, how far the lean, how high the shoulders) do not slide back toward neutral. Any note on an attitude names
which part moves, which way, how much, and on what frame.

### HED45. Ears, tail, neck, hair and costume act on every key
stages: rig, plan, blocking, passes

Before animating, list every part beyond the bones that can show mood (ears, tail, neck, hair, a coat, a hat, a worn
prop), the range of moods it can show, and how much its shape may be pushed for the sake of movement. On every key,
set each of these parts to an angle that agrees with the body's attitude: the angle comes from the attitude and the
timing comes from overlap. The angles to choose from: for the tail, carried lively or hanging dejected; for the neck,
an arch when the character is angry or alert; for the ears, laid back, upright or pointing forward. A tail is not
left lying still, or resting on the ground, without a reason. A piece of costume may deliberately disagree with
the body, as a tool of personality. A long tube body, such as a snake, a long tail or a tentacle, keeps one straight
side running along its coils while the coils move at different speeds; check its markings for strobing.

### HED46. A body without limbs acts through substitutes
stages: rig, plan, blocking

If the design has no arms, hands or shoulders, give each missing part's job to the nearest part that can do it. Place
and time that part the way the missing limb would move. Sort the jobs by the limb that would have done them: shoulder,
arm, hand or foot. For each job, find the part of the rig that can carry it, and test it in a short pose. Write the
list of substitutes before the shot. Then check that each attitude still reads with the face hidden, from the shape
of the body alone.

### HED39. In strong emotion, one part changes
stages: plan, blocking, passes

In panic, shock or grief, keep the travel of the root and the swing of the limbs small, and let one element take the
biggest change, such as the head or a hand. Count the large moves in the beat: each one needs its reason in the plan,
tied to a story beat, and every move beyond those weakens the moment instead of strengthening it.

### HED44. A delicate beat is decided in the staging
stages: plan, blocking

For a quiet emotional beat, spend the effort in the plan, not in the motion. Decide there the camera angle and
distance on the character, how much open space surrounds it, the moment the viewer is first allowed to see the face,
and who in the shot shows the response; another character's body may show it. The animation itself then stays a
few plain moves around one clear held attitude. Anything extra breaks the mood.

Loneliness and dejection read best from small actions done with no aim. Choose them from the character and its
setting, keep them slow and low, and stage them wide instead of acting them large. A dejected body that sits down
does it slowly and comes to rest in a closed pose, with the limbs and the head drawn in.

### HED25. Hopeless stillness allows one sigh
stages: plan, blocking, passes

A character that has given up stays passive, and the staging carries the story around it. Any move it makes is a
single sigh, built on one breath. Build that breath as in HED22, only larger: the chest rises up and forward, and the
collarbones and the head rise with it. The breath out ends the sigh, and the whole figure settles lower than where it
started. Keep the rest of the body still, so that the one breath reads.

### HED43. A mood that must not break is held, not acted
stages: plan, blocking, passes

Deep grief, or any mood that one wrong move would break, is carried by stillness. Check the shot for every move that
has no need. Remove fidgets, repeated gestures and short, jerky moves. Hold strong poses of the whole body for long
stretches, so that the poses themselves carry the feeling. Keep the breath alive, as in HED22, and make any other
change slow. In a looping idle, the breath crosses the loop seam smoothly.

### HED35. Every key and breakdown carries the attitude
stages: plan, blocking, passes

Begin with a single attitude pose whose feeling is obvious instantly, and carry it into every key and every breakdown,
including the travel poses of a walk. A travel-only key, one that just carries the body from place to place, either
gains a thought or is removed. Something must still say what the character feels or wants: a lean, a look, a hand,
the chin. In 3D this applies to every key and breakdown the animator sets; sample some in-between frames too, but
never key every frame.

### HED26. A conscious action is led by a visible thought
stages: plan, blocking

Before a character does something on purpose, show the thought that drives it: the head turns to the target and stays
there long enough to read, the body pauses or shifts slightly toward it, and an action that needs aim may get more
than one look. Setting up the action in view lets the audience anticipate it. When the character searches, it heads
for the next spot before it has finished with this one. The audience should know what the character will do a moment before it does it.
A gesture and the look that leads it never land on the same frame.

### HED27. Habits and reflexes need no thought beat
stages: plan, blocking

Breathing, blinking, walking steps, reaching for something out of habit and trained reflexes all belong to the larger
thought around them. The visible thought goes on the purpose of the phrase alone. A visible thought before every small
sub-action reads as cliché acting. One master phrase can hold many beats.

### HED28. A decision is a beat of its own
stages: plan, blocking

To show a character making up its mind: halt what it is doing, raise the head, glance off and return, give the head an
uneven tilt, then shift the attitude clearly as the choice is made, and only then begin the action that results. Hold the thinking
pose long enough to read. A changed thought alters the motion on the very frame it changes: the character halts, hunts
about, or restarts larger. In gameplay a player's move has no thinking time; this beat belongs to idles, non-player characters and
cutscenes.

### HED29. Attention decides what moves first
stages: plan, blocking

The force behind a move sits where the character's attention is. Attention chooses which part leads and how much of the
body joins in. When attention stays fixed, the body holds and only one part responds, until the stimulus grows enough
to engage the whole body. When attention wanders, the head goes first and the rest trails after it.

### HED31. The speed of a change follows its thought
stages: plan, blocking, passes

For every change of attitude, write its cause in the plan; the cause sets the timing. A cause that strikes at once,
such as a sudden realisation or a surprise, switches the attitude in 3 to 6 frames. A cause that is worked out, such as
weighing a choice, puzzling over something or a plan taking form, spreads the switch over 12 to 16 frames, and the pose
it arrives at gets a longer hold than a quick switch would. A single beat can carry several switches, each timed by its
own cause. On a faceless rig the head and the chest carry the switch.

### HED30. Keep attitude changes out of the fastest frames
stages: plan, blocking, checks

The viewer cannot see an attitude change that happens while the body is moving fast. Find the window where the root or
the chest travels fastest; the head's attitude at the first frame of that window must equal its attitude at the last.
Key each change on a held or slow pose instead: just ahead of the fast move, so the thought reads first, or once the
move is over, where the viewer can see it. A move that is itself slow may carry the change inside it. A change must
never sit under a busy secondary motion, such as a flapping sleeve or a hand in a large gesture, which would cover it.

### HED32. Aim the body at the target before the action
stages: plan, blocking, passes

Before and through an important action, the head settles on the target before anything else, the trunk tips its way,
and the hand or foot doing the work stays in open space, clear of body and face. Even when the wind-up carries the mass away from the
target, the head stays pointed at it. Each anticipation states what it tells the viewer, where to look and what comes
next, and points at the point of the shot, not at a side action. To move the eye from one place to another, the first
points at the second before the action.

### HED33. A prop is looked at, used, then the reaction moves to the body
stages: plan, blocking

First make it clear that the prop is there: the head turns to it, and a small anticipation precedes its use. Never use
a prop while the head looks elsewhere. While the prop acts, the head and hands point at it. Once that beat has read,
quiet the prop hand and move the main motion into the head and body for the reaction.

### HED1. Every look has a cause, and the head starts it
stages: plan, blocking

The plan names why each look happens: something seen, something heard, or a look the character planned. On a rig
without working eyes, the first frame of the head turn is the first frame of the look. Before a walking character
changes direction, the head turns toward the new path ahead of the body. A strong, sudden look can begin with a small
move the other way before it swings to the target.

### HED4. On an eyeless rig, aim every look at a written target
stages: rig, plan, blocking, checks

When the eyes cannot move, every look is a head aim, in yaw and pitch, at a target named in the plan; the chest joins
once the neck runs out of range. Every feeling goes into the body attitude and the gestures of head and hands. Check
each key for a part that disagrees with the feeling, the shoulders first: a relaxed shoulder in an angry pose cancels
the anger. A design without eyes pays for it with a stronger body and a busier head.

### HED2. The head does only part of the work of a look
stages: plan, blocking, passes

In people, the head covers a fraction of a gaze shift: roughly 0.4 of a look at something seen (anywhere from 0.14 to
0.73 between people) and about 0.6 of a look toward a sound. A look smaller than about 15 deg is nearly all eyes, with
only a slight head drift. The eyes rarely end more than about 22 deg off the head's centre and cannot pass about 45
deg, so beyond 30 to 40 deg the head joins in, and past the neck's range the chest and then the feet follow.

Whether a person turns the head for everything or keeps it still and moves only the eyes is a trait of personality,
legal when written into the plan. A rig without eyes must give the head more of the turn than a human would; that
larger share is a declared choice for the rig, not an accident.

### HED3. A look lands on a slow tail
stages: blocking, passes, checks

The gaze reaches the target first. The head then continues for about 6 frames (4 to 8), slowing all the way, as the
eyes rotate back against it and stay locked on what they found. On an eyeless rig, spend the last 20 to 30 percent of the head turn
on this decelerating finish, so the viewer feels the gaze land before the head does. The finish settles; it does
not pass the target. Passing the target and coming back is a separate acting choice laid on top.
A head that stops dead on the frame of its key looks mechanical.

Typical speeds: a quick 30 deg glance peaks near 80 deg/s and takes about 10 to 12 frames. A forceful voluntary turn
can reach about 780 deg/s, and anything near that reads as a snap or a startle. While walking or running, the
head turns slowly, under about 90 deg/s, and pitches more often than it yaws.

### HED5. A look spreads down the body
stages: blocking, passes

A look begins in the head; the neck, the chest and the spine then follow in that order, each a little later and each
turning a little less. A change of direction, even a small glance, is a pose of the whole body: some head, some neck,
some shoulder, a touch of arm. A single joint wrenched round while everything else stays put looks broken. When the
look is layered on top of other motion, it blends in and out over a few frames, respects rotation limits and keeps
its target named; when several targets compete, one has priority.

### HED6. Fix the head in the world, then solve the neck under it
stages: rig, blocking, passes

When the head must aim, or must stay calm while the body bobs, steps or turns, set the head's position and
orientation in world space first. Then solve the neck and upper spine beneath it, each taking a little less. Turning
the neck first and letting the head ride on top makes the head swing with every body move, like a puppet on a stick.
In a check, the head's world rotation range over the move should be smaller than the chest's.

### HED10. Big head turns roll and dip, everyday acting stays small
stages: blocking, passes, checks

A large head turn is not pure yaw. Near 65 deg of turn, the head also rolls toward the same side by about 15 deg and
dips by about 17 deg, so the roll is about a quarter of the yaw. Ordinary acting lives in a small window, about 20 deg
up and down, 14 deg sideways and 18 deg of turn. A large neck angle needs a reason in the shot: a look behind, a look
down the stairs, reversing a car. Picking something up by stooping turns and tilts the head more than squatting does.

### HED9. Know the neck's range for the character's age
stages: rig, plan, checks

A young adult rotates the head about 70 to 80 deg to each side, bends it sideways about 40 to 50 deg, flexes it
forward about 45 to 60 deg and extends it back about 60 to 68 deg; extension is the larger of the two. Past 65 the
typical ranges drop to about 55 to 69 rotation, 32 to 38 side bend, 35 to 45 flexion and 51 to 64 extension, with the
largest losses in extension and rotation. Age changes the range more than sex does.

Nodding and looking down come mostly from the lower neck. Tucking the chin or looking upward relies far more on the
joint where the skull meets the spine. Because that top joint keeps its rotation in old age, an older character
looking around turns the head and then brings the upper body in sooner. A head held forward of the shoulders (a
teenager, a desk worker) is a declared posture trait.

### HED11. A walker looks down the path, about 1.5 seconds ahead
stages: plan, blocking

The gaze leads by time, not distance: about 1.5 s (36 frames at 24 fps, a conversion), which is about 2 m ahead on flat ground and less on
rough ground. On flat ground the gaze is on the ground a little over half the time; on rough ground almost all the
time, on the footholds two or three steps ahead. A chosen spot to step on is looked at about two steps ahead, 800 to 1000 ms before
the foot lands (19 to 24 frames at 24 fps, a conversion), and the gaze moves on before the foot touches it. Where the surface changes, the eyes check the border before anything
else.

The head's yaw stays on the travel line. On careful ground it pitches down a little and the eyes take most of the
drop; how the drop splits between head and eyes is poorly measured.

### HED60. Around cover, the head leads
stages: plan, blocking

In competitive third-person play, when the character edges past a corner or an obstacle that hides it, the head
travels first, so the character sees round before any other part of the body becomes visible to an opponent. Give
each stance and each carried-item state a silhouette of its own, since opponents identify it by that outline.

### HED8. A listening head keeps answering
stages: passes

A listener spends roughly a fifth of the time in short runs of small, fast nods, each cycle about 4 to 9 frames, and the
opening nod of each run is the biggest. The speaker nods about half as often. The listener's slow head drift echoes the
partner's about 14 frames later. Small tilts appear in almost every exchange; head shakes are rare. Nod sizes are not
well measured, so keep them small and judge them by eye.

### HED7. The head never freezes
stages: passes, checks, self_review

People rarely hold the gaze still. In a hold where the character listens or thinks, the head's aim changes in small,
quick jumps separated by short pauses: not a smooth drift, not a locked stare. A fixed stare is used only when the
plan declares it. No hold or idle goes completely flat for more than about 40 frames.

### HED14. The head drags behind the chest
stages: blocking, passes, checks

The spine is a chain driven by the travel of the hips; the hip rotation, the chest and the head trail it. Shape it from
the root upward: each section trails the one below, and the head arrives and settles last. As a starting value each
higher spine control lags the one below by about a frame and the head by about two; lag a few controls, one at a time,
and look after each, because lagging every joint looks watery. When the hips halt, the rotation carries on upward one
joint at a time and the head is the last part to reverse; it never drops out of the overlap early. At the lowest point
of a step the trunk stands straightest, since the upper body lags the drop, and it is then released into overlap. On a
take, the spine's rotation trails the root's height by 1 or 2 frames, set in the breakdowns.

Measure the head's vertical bob against the chest's: the head's high point comes about a twelfth of a second after the
chest's (2 frames at 24 fps, about 3 at 30), never on the same frame and no later than a sixth of a second (4 frames at
24). A small tilt may ride on that rise. Decide once whether the head's bob rises with the leading foot's step or
against it, and hold that choice for the whole shot. Ears or other loose parts on the head form a third link, a further 2 frames behind.

Because the neck lets a weighty head swing, the head trails the chest and overshoots it, and it also turns against the
body's vertical travel: on a rise it hangs low and tips its nose down, and on a drop it tips up, catching up and
swinging past. Key that counter-rotation already in blocking. Where a head rotation jumps from one key to the next,
make it smaller and hand some of it on to the following pose; then toggle the two neighbours to be sure the face
angle truly differs. On a chest that arrives, keep the chest moving a little past its key, then settle it, then the head. Nothing
in the spine starts or stops on the same frame as its neighbour. Two exceptions, each declared in the plan: a move
triggered by a look may begin with the head, and in an acting beat the upper body may arrive as one so the emotion
reads first (HED17). In a turn into a reaction, the
head turns first and the body swings on past the head once it stops. Each move is keyed from the push that causes it:
toe presses down, heel comes up, and the hips climb under a head kept still for two or three frames. Hips and upper
body come first; the arms get a pass of their own.

### HED17. In an acting beat, the feeling group may land as one
stages: plan, blocking, passes

Normally the mass leads and the head trails. When a beat exists to show an attitude, such as the reading pose of a
take or a held reaction, treat the head, the chest and the shoulders as one feeling group that hits its pose on a
single frame, so the attitude reads at once. The plan declares it. Everything outside the group (arms, hands, soft
parts, cloth) settles afterwards, each on its own frame.

### HED15. A rise unrolls from below, a fold leads with the head
stages: plan, blocking, passes

When a character stands up or pitches forward out of a seat, the lower back moves first, the upper chest trails it by
a few frames, and the head comes last, so it arrives with the energy of the move instead of leading it. Bowing or
folding down reverses the order: the head goes first and the chest follows. In one continuous fold and rise, the rise
carries its momentum without a stop at lift-off, and the character ends a little forward of where it started.

In a performed rise, the acting choice can make the fold phase, from the first bow to lift-off, last about twice as
long as the rise phase that follows it (for instance 16 frames of fold, then 8 of rise). This widens the contrast between the slow
beat and the fast beat beyond what the reference shows. It is a split of phase lengths, not the speed of the trunk:
measured on a natural rise, the trunk dives quickly and straightens slowly over the push up. Start every rise from the
natural timing of ACT31; the long fold is an acting push on top of it, used only when the plan asks for that contrast.

### HED16. After a jolt, the head keeps going
stages: blocking, passes

When an outside force reverses the body, the hips reverse first, then the chest, and the head carries on in its old
direction for at least one more pose, since no force has reached it yet. The spine forms an S while this happens.

### HED18. The head tilt answers the shoulders
stages: blocking, passes, checks

On every key, the tilt of the head relates to the line of the shoulders, and the shoulders relate to the hips, with or
against on purpose. A key where head, shoulders and hips are all level is stiff and worth a second look. When a move
runs entirely in one axis, add a side tilt to the middle and upper back and a larger side-to-side of the head, built
into the chest, then re-pose the arms to the new plane of the shoulders.

### HED12. A character walk gives the head one habit
stages: plan, blocking, passes

In a plain or realistic walk the head stays stabilised: its world yaw stays within a few degrees of the travel line,
alive but never locked. A character walk instead gives the head one behaviour of its own and repeats it at the same
point of every step. The habit can sit at a single point of the step or run through all of it. Through the whole step:
a head that floats along with little bob, or a head that tilts together with the shoulders. At the passing position: a
head that thrusts forward, or a tilt that only arrives there, late. At the end of the step: a head that tips. The plan
names the habit and its reason.

### HED13. In a fast cycle the head stays steady
stages: blocking, passes, checks

In a run, a sprint or a combat idle, keep the head, or any shape that identifies the character, constant in shape and
close to the centre of the body, travelling on a small simple path that is mostly up and down. The legs, arms and
loose parts carry the change. A sideways wobble or a roll that the action does not need is a fault.

### HED19. Give the character the neck it needs, not the reference's
stages: references, blocking

Where the reference actor holds the head stiffly on the shoulders, add compression and curve in the spine and neck,
scaled to how flexible the character is and how it is
built. A large head moves the balance, so check its centre line from
every view. A short neck that cannot reach a pose by rotation alone may stretch a little, written down as a cheat.

### HED22. A quiet breath moves the chest by millimetres
stages: passes, checks

A resting breath lifts the upper chest about 3 to 5 mm up and forward, pushes the belly forward, and repeats about 12
to 18 times a minute (one breath every 80 to 120 frames at 24 fps, a conversion). The back of the ribcage moves forward without the spine
bending, and the head stays still in space. Quiet breathing has no shoulder shrug; shoulders that visibly lift belong
to laboured breathing. Breathing is never one part alone: chest rotated up, collarbones up, a small rise. After
effort, breaths get larger and faster.

### HED24. A lift begins on a breath in
stages: plan, blocking, passes

In the set-up of a lift, the character inhales over the last 12 to 24 frames before the load leaves the ground, filling
the chest up and forward to about 1.4 to 2.5 times a quiet breath; the heavier the load, the deeper. Light and moderate
loads go up on an exhale. Breath holding with a braced chest is reserved for very heavy loads, and it starts late,
around three quarters of the way through the lift, released as an exhale you can see once the load is down. Holding
the breath from the moment the character bends is an animation habit; real bodies do not do it.

### HED23. A run locks the breath to the stride
stages: plan, passes

When breathing shows in a run, start from one breath across two strides, that is four steps. Real runners also lock
into other whole-number ratios such as 3:1 or 3:2. A four-legged animal typically takes one breath per stride.

### HED21. The first pass brings the holds and the head to life
stages: passes

After blocking, loosen each main pose the way the breakdowns loosened the gaps: a slight drift or compression in each
hold (as little as one degree on a held spine), the hands offset, nods and shakes on the head, every head turn on an
arc with an ease into its end, the face turned slightly down into a look. Then re-read the path of action on every
story pose: it should run as a single curve from feet to head. If tilting the head or changing a hand would close that
curve, change the pose.

### HED20. A head wobble is fixed at its source
stages: passes, self_review

When the head bumps or wobbles, look first at its parents: a chest key, a shoulder move, the spine. Correct the curve
that causes it. Keys added on the head to hide the bump are a bandage and are not allowed.

### HED42. The character stays recognisable on its fastest and farthest frames
stages: blocking, passes, checks

On frames where the body moves fast, is smeared, stands far from camera or is changing its form, the head, the line of
the face and the main idea of the silhouette must still read. A smear frame may carry the speed, but the character
has to remain present in it. After a transformation, the new shape keeps the traits that identified the old one. Step
through the fastest frames at thumbnail size and ask who it is and what it feels.

### HED47. A cheat that fits the character reads as acting
stages: blocking, passes

When a constraint breaks (a line of sight, a contact, a match to the camera), repair it first with something the character
would plausibly do, and declare it, before any hidden correction. A motivated fix goes unnoticed; a patch shows.

### HED56. Check the textures after blocking
stages: blocking, checks, self_review

After blocking, list each phrase with its length and speed. If two adjacent phrases have the same texture, alter one
deliberately; otherwise they merge and the viewer can no longer follow the thinking. A rhythm that repeats on purpose, with a new pose each
time, is allowed when the plan declares it.

### HED57. Busy blocking is cured by deleting poses
stages: blocking, self_review

Giving each accent its own pose overloads the shot. When playback feels cluttered, remove poses one by one until the
attitude is clear again; a small look often does more than a large reaction. A critic sees the blocking before it is
refined, not only the animator who made it.

### HED55. Find the phrasing by feel, one change per loop
stages: blocking, self_review

Play the stepped blocking on a loop, adjust a single beat each time round, and stop once the timing feels natural.
Cleanup waits until then. Never
phrase from a frame formula. Phrase from the personality, the mood or a music track when there is one, and avoid
timing that is too contained or literal.
