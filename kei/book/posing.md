# Posing and line of action

kind: principle

A shot is only as good as its key poses. These rules cover how Kei finds, builds and judges a pose: its line of
action, its volumes, its silhouette, its twist and its attitude, from the first thumbnail to the last check.

### POS1. One simple line runs through the main mass
stages: plan, blocking, passes

Each key pose has one gentle curve, a C or an S, that runs from the supporting foot up through the leg, the hips, the
spine and the neck into the head. The arms and the free leg carry that curve on; they do not cut across it. Judge the
line by how simple it is, not by how much it bends: a curl or a hook adds bend and loses clarity. A straight spine
with one arm raised reads as a stick figure, whatever the arm does.

### POS2. Pose first, then time it
stages: plan, blocking, passes

For each key, decide the line of action as a single curve first, then fit every part to it: the tilt and roll of the
pelvis, each spine segment, then neck and head along that one curve. The arm and leg that lead push the curve further;
the rest of the limbs hold it up. Retime only once the poses read. Judge every key with the posing tools (line,
silhouette, asymmetry, weight, balance, appeal) before looking at any motion between keys.

A move whose poses do not read cannot be rescued in the in-betweens. When a smooth curve cannot pass through the keys
without a jerk, the fault is a key pose: reduce the shot to its keys and breakdowns, step through it to locate the bad
frames, change the pose that makes a smooth curve impossible, and only then shape the tangents.

### POS3. Build the line in the core with the main controls
stages: blocking, passes

The curve through the trunk matters more than any other part for believable body mechanics. Make it with the main
controls: a side bend, for example, comes from the hip's sideways shift and roll. Spread any spine bend over every
spine control; a bend on one control alone looks hinged. Use the same control for the same job on every pose, so no
control fights another. Fine controls (spine curvature, extra bend handles, segment stretch) are for the last polish,
in small amounts, and never to rescue a weak pose.

### POS4. Change the line from one key to the next
stages: plan, blocking

Give each key a line whose shape differs from its neighbours: flip the direction of the bend, straighten a curved
line, or curve a straight one. Two neighbours with the same curve waste a key. The strongest contrast puts the most compact pose just before
the most stretched one: a coiled crouch with the spine fully reversed, the head down and the knees up, then a take or
push that opens into one long, nearly straight diagonal. Half-open poses that never fully close or fully open look
bland. A pose that is strongly different from the last is built again from zeroed values, not nudged.

A reversal flips one C into its opposite through the keys of an action: the hips go one way, the spine the other, and
the head carries on. Mark reversals in the thumbnails and spend them only where a moment needs one. In acting, a
reversal can stay small.

### POS5. What a strong crouch and a strong stretch look like
stages: blocking, passes

A strong low pose is deep and compact: the trunk bends down over legs set wide and bent, the pelvis is low, the weight
sits in the legs, and a reaching arm or weapon extends long and low so the whole figure makes one shape. A strong tall
pose is an arched stretch: one reverse C bows the whole body back, starting at the planted foot and ending at the
raised hand, the shoulders open, the weight over the back leg. In a ready stance the knees come forward of the ankles
and one smooth C runs into the extended limb. Faults to look for: a hip that looks broken, a straight spine, a high
pelvis, splayed legs with no weight in them, a weapon resting on the ground like a walking stick.

### POS6. Measure the line as a note, never as a gate
stages: checks, self_review

A number can make a straight spine visible, but it never passes or fails a pose. Fit a smooth curve through the
pelvis, the spine points, the neck and the head on each key, using the skinned trunk rather than the straight bones of
the rig, and report its bend and direction. Follow the supporting foot, the leg, the trunk and the head; a leading arm
joins the line only if it carries the curve on. Judge limbs that hook back on their own, outside the total.

Read unsigned turning, not signed bend: in an S the two halves cancel, so a signed sum can call a lively S straight.
Clarity drops with corners, not with straightness. A joint turning less than about 4 deg is dead (a locked knee, a rod
spine). A joint turning more than the curve by over about 20 deg is a break. Part of a strong line lives in the
silhouette, such as the back contour and a head tipped back, which joint angles cannot see; and a crouch folds its
knees for real, so measure such a knee against a good crouch rather than against zero.

### POS7. Build the body from simple volumes
stages: rig, blocking, checks

Think of each part as a simple solid: a ball for the head, an egg for the chest, a rounded box for the pelvis, tapered
capsules for the limbs with a ball at each shoulder, elbow and hip, flat wedges for hands and feet. The chest and the
pelvis are about the same width; the waist between them is narrower. Rigid props are thin boxes or discs and never
squash. Draw and judge poses on the smooth skinned body, not on the bone sticks: the rig builds its spine from
straight segments, and the curve only appears once the skin is on. Where the trunk compresses, its section widens.

### POS8. Pose masses, not lines
stages: blocking, passes

Pose from the inside out: round masses with weight, placed in space. The outline does the work. A plain rubber
capsule with no limbs and no face can read as eager when it tips toward something, as worn out when it slumps and leans,
and as proud when it rises and tips back. When that outer shape is right, interior detail adds nothing; when it is
wrong, interior detail cannot repair it.

Check every key in three steps, judged by eye on simple capsule rigs. Balance first: the centre of mass sits over its
base, or the pose is clearly on its way somewhere. Then weight: each mass presses down on what holds it up. Then
depth: the pose turns into the space of the scene instead of lying flat on the picture.

Shape each limb and the trunk unevenly as part of that work. The side that pushes or bears load runs straight and the
relaxed side bows out. Stiffness comes from four regular shapes: twin edges running side by side, a joint folded to a
hard point, a mass rounded the same on every side, and left and right limbs that copy each other. Break any of them
that appears.

### POS9. Every in-between keeps the volume and design of its keys
stages: passes, checks

A shape changes its design, size or proportion only on purpose. A part that stretches thins across, and a part that
squashes widens, so its volume holds. A bone's length never changes. An in-between that alters a shape by accident
shimmers on screen; watch one part at a time on ones, or measure length and area per frame.

Turns are in-betweened in space, not halfway across the screen. Think of a mug turning on a table: its middle frame
shows the handle partway round, at the halfway angle seen from above, not a mug squeezed flat. A head works the same
way. A middle frame averaged on the screen loses the depth of the face; the middle frame taken at the halfway angle
keeps part of the far side of the face in view. On a rig, the turn goes the short way round, its middle frame near the
middle angle, with no flip.

### POS10. Chest and pelvis are two masses that work against each other
stages: blocking, passes

The chest and the pelvis tilt and twist against each other. That opposition is the source of contrapposto and of every
weight shift: the hip drops over the free leg, the chest counters it, the spine stays loose between them, the arms
answer the legs, and the whole body rides one subtle curving axis. Hips and shoulders that turn together look wooden
unless that is a chosen trait.

Choose the pivot for each pose. The whole body tilts or turns at the hip joints; the pelvis alone turns under a held
chest at the waist. The trunk pitches forward at the hips while the lower back keeps a nearly constant curve. Each
pelvis key gets a decision on all six of its channels: up and down, travel, sideways shift, tilt, twist, pitch. The
commonest flat pose leaves the pelvis without any sideways shift or tilt.

### POS12. Read the silhouette and the negative space on every key
stages: blocking, passes, checks, self_review

Shrink the shot camera to a small black shape, or render every key as a solid shape with no inner detail and look at
it at thumbnail size: the pose should tell its idea and its attitude alone, and the C or reverse C of its line of
action must be plain. Keep the arms clear of the body where the action allows, so gaps of background show between limbs
and trunk, and make those gaps change readably from one key to the next. At the strongest frames the side view shows
daylight through the gap of the legs and through the gap of each arm against the body. A gesture that crosses in front
of the body with no gap, or a pose held tight inside its own outline, usually reads weaker than an open one.

Neighbouring keys contrast: low against tall, curled against arched, fast against slow. Keep only the poses the story
needs, each with one idea and each held long enough to register.

The holes between an arm and the body and between the legs point, by their tips or long axes, toward the centre of
interest, the way the line of action does; a small hole such as the triangle of arm and waist counts too. A single C
or S passes from the head down the back and out along the arm that presents; a subtle style shrinks it but keeps its
direction. The acting limb keeps air around it and a contrasting value behind it through the whole gesture.

In polish, judge every frame's silhouette as if it were a pose. Contours should flow from part to part, the arm into
the torso and the torso into the leg, rather than a row of separate curves; break the flow only where a joint must
show. Compare two versions of a line and keep the one with tension where it bends and no sag.

### POS13. Strong posing means clear, not extreme
stages: blocking, passes, self_review

A pose gets its strength from a silhouette that reads, weight committed to one side, a lifted chest and head, and
limbs held away from the trunk. It does not come from pushing every value at once. Offer push levels starting from the
real-body pose and show the mildest one that reads. For a game hub idle the push stays small: as a typical value,
weight about 55/45 over one leg, the hip lowered by about 4 deg on the free side, the shoulder line close to flat,
both knees softly and about equally bent. Never max the hip drop, the counter-tilt of the shoulders and the arm spread
together.

A small move still needs clear keys. Build a quiet move from distinct poses, each held long enough to read, and
never let it become one slow, even drift between soft poses.

### POS14. Attitude shows in the pose
stages: plan, blocking

A confident character gets defined, committed poses and firm timing; an insecure one gets soft, undecided poses. A
head tipped down with the eyes looking up reads as determination; a lifted chin reads as carefree. A plain, neutral
movement still pushes its poses a little, so it looks alive without any personality added.

### POS15. Know who the character is before the first pose
stages: brief, understanding, plan

Write who the character is from the inside: type, a short past, habits, how it moves, and one line on how it sees
itself. Every pose, and the size of every reaction, should be explainable from that line: pride keeps a body upright
in the middle of a failure, while carelessness lets it spread out. For an opponent, also decide the kind of threat
(assured, cunning, blunt, cold) and how much composure it keeps. An opponent played straight keeps its composure even
when its plan fails, so it gets no slapstick. An opponent played for laughs may lose all composure and must still stay
dangerous. An assured threat keeps its moves few and is never in a rush.

### POS16. Every pose has a cause and a direction
stages: plan, blocking, self_review

A pose answers something. Each key gets two notes: the thing the character responds to, and its next intention. When
nothing causes a key, that key goes. A reaction never starts before the frame of its cause, except where the plan has the
character anticipate that cause. Seen as a still, each key shows where the body came from and where it is going.

No control moves on its own; it moves because the body carried that part there. Name the body action behind a hand or
foot key, and key the body as well. When only the tip of a limb satisfies the brief, the pose looks mechanical, and a
body move on one root channel alone has no personality.

### POS17. Never the same pose twice
stages: blocking, passes

No two story poses are identical. After copying a pose, change its rotations (shoulders, neck, head) so it differs.
Make a mirrored pose by zeroing and posing again, not by inverting every channel. Let every planted foot sit slightly
angled, never perfectly square. In a cycle, mirror the two halves and then break the mirror a little, with limb
extremes a frame or so off the pelvis extremes; a dragging or limping cycle has two clearly different contacts. A pose
that returns later is built close to the first one, not copied.

A pose held and worked through a phrase is still one pose, and a planted foot that returns to its spot returns
exactly; the rule is about story poses, not about contacts. A final or resting pose never stands with the feet
together or stacked: one foot ahead, one behind, apart, with the body settled into the hips.

### POS18. Cheat the body or the view so the pose reads
stages: plan, blocking

When reading demands it, bend the truth: proportions, joint limits and even physics may give way to the pose. Favour
the views where the character looks its best. If a pose looks awkward through the lens, first check whether another
camera angle tells the shot as well. An angle is never a hiding place, though: a pose that still looks wrong from a flattering view
gets reposed. The chest may turn away while the face cheats toward the lens; a reading of facing taken from the face
alone is too frontal, so let the shoulders and hips set how far the body turns, with the eyes free within about 45
deg.

### POS19. One pose per attitude, worked from inside
stages: plan, blocking

Change the pose only when the attitude changes. Inside a held attitude, animate small accents (a brow, a shrug, a head
tilt, a hand to the face) that keep the whole body's contrast; a move that alters that contrast counts as a fresh
pose, and only a fresh attitude justifies it. Before blocking, try to tell each scene in two or three poses; every
further pose must earn its place with a new attitude. Accents of a line ride inside the held pose, one gesture at a
time.

Too much movement removes emphasis. Where limbs move all the time, replace the flailing in-betweens with a strong pose
held long enough to read and a quick move to the next one. Count the distinct poses per second of acting as a
diagnostic.

### POS20. Storytelling poses come first, without timing
stages: plan, blocking

Look for the smallest set of poses able to tell the scene, each a still that could stand alone and still show one
emotion and one action. A single idea drives each of them through the entire body: a single line of action, hanging limbs pulled
down by gravity, a change of shape where the emotion allows. Choose the pose the audience will settle on, which is not
always the extreme. Fix these attitudes before any frame number, sketch them roughly and throw them away freely.

Before posing, reduce the shot to one sentence naming who acts, what sets them off and how they answer. The thumbnails
must make both the cue and the answer unmistakable. Frame the idea as a contrast of states where one exists, such as
patience against hurry.

### POS21. Thumbnail for information, then choose among options
stages: plan

A thumbnail carries information, not beauty: the pose with its boxed frame number, its line of action, a centre line,
cross marks showing where face and chest point, and brief notes about weight. When several parts move, a note gives
their sequence (for example: the foot plants, the shoulder turns, the hand closes). Small figures stand on one ground line, numbered, with
arrows. A stick figure needs only a hip ball, a shoulder line and tilt lines. Subtle detail is noted in words and
worked in later.

Play the action in the mind until one version repeats, keep it simple, and name the cause of each motion. Thumbnail
the first idea, then at least two others, and give the pose that carries the point four or more candidates, each
flipped together with its set-up poses. Reject options that add actions the point does not need. Favour poses that
open out, lean in the direction of the goal and make use of the prop. If two candidates both deliver, take whichever
suits the character more naturally, note why in a line, and continue. Each pose is a choice: inspect it, swap it out
when it fails, and never leave it open.

### POS22. Build each pose from the root, and the first pose with care
stages: blocking

Inside a pose, set the root first, then the spine, then the limbs, working from the ground up in a free perspective
view rather than the shot camera. Cycle among the hips, the feet, the spine and the arms, again and again, until the
figure works as a whole; no single part is done in isolation. Check it from two other angles, then judge it in the
shot camera. Pose joint by joint, never by dragging the whole rig, and zero a tangled control and pose it again rather
than stacking rotations.

The first pose is the foundation of the others, so spend time on it. Then rough in every other pose broadly before
refining any. Fingers and fine detail wait for the tidy pass.

### POS23. Critique every pose part by part before delivery
stages: self_review

Before reporting any pose or pass, look at the delivery images from the side, the front and a three-quarter view and
question every part in turn, from the head down to each foot, plus any prop contact: would a living body place it so
during this particular action, with this weight to carry, at this instant, or does the part only dodge a fault? Then
reduce the strongest view to a small dark shape. Ask whether that shape alone tells what happens, whether the frame is
a real key rather than a passing frame, and whether its line of action commits. Fix every no, look again, and list
what could not be fixed as uncertain. A pose that passes every check can still be wrong; never report on checks alone.
