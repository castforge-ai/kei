# Contacts and feet

kind: principle

Weight is believed only where something touches the ground. These rules cover how Kei plans, keys and checks every
contact: planted feet, the roll from heel to toe, the swing between plants, slides, and the contacts of hands, seats
and props.

### CNT1. Plan the contacts before the poses
stages: plan, blocking

Before posing, write down where each foot lands, on which frame it touches, which frames it stays down and which
frames it travels. On every key, name what carries the body: one foot, both feet, a foot and a hand, the seat, or a
load still resting on the floor. Balance is judged over that set and nothing else. With two feet down, the plumb line
of the mass falls between them by the share each one carries. Every plant is solid and final in the stepped blocking
before any timing work starts.

### CNT2. A planted contact does not move
stages: blocking, passes, checks

Once a heel, ball or toe tip is down and carrying weight, its key is copied exactly and it stays where it landed. A
contact point that has been on the floor for three frames may drift no more than about 1 cm a frame; the touch-down
frame itself is not judged. A plant that lasts several beats keeps one heading: the foot cannot swivel inside it, even
where a reference shows splayed feet. No heel or toe sinks more than about 1 cm into the floor on any frame. At the
next step the foot that is down keeps its place while the other comes forward.

### CNT3. A planted foot still has a little give
stages: passes

Locked is not frozen. While the body acts above it, a planted foot can rock a little from the ball, change its angle a
few degrees where the weight moves, and let its roll follow when the body pulls up off it. After a stop, small foot
adjustments go where the weight changes and prepare the next shift. A landing that must read solid is the exception:
keep its foot corrections few, because each one weakens the impact.

### CNT5. The ground stops the foot
stages: passes

A foot arriving at the floor is halted by the floor, not by its own muscles, so its height curve meets the ground at
a sharp corner with no ease into it. Default spline tangents on feet ease into the contact, overshoot, dip below the
floor and swim. Start foot curves flat or linear, then shape each segment by hand, and break the tangent at the
contact: a steep incoming side and a flat outgoing side. Check that the foot height never goes below the ground on any
frame.

### CNT6. A walking contact is heel, slap, toes
stages: blocking, passes

In a walk, the contact frame has the heel down with the toes raised, typically 20 to 30 deg off the floor, on a leg
that reads straight. One frame later the sole comes down flat with the toes still lifted; one frame after that the
toes lie flat. A slow roll from heel to sole that lasts until the down throws the weight away. The lowering
of the forefoot is fast and controlled, never a bounce and never a dead drop. A very small negative roll on the
contact key helps the forefoot fall. Read the toe drag at full speed.

Each step must let the contact be felt: a clear heel strike, the weight sinking into the leg just after it, and a foot
that plants without sliding.

### CNT7. The contact knee eases, it never pops
stages: passes, checks

The front leg reads straight on the contact frame and for one or two frames after, then bends on one smooth curve into
the loading. It is never held at one angle and then bent. Around each contact, three frames either side and for each leg, the
knee's change of angular speed stays near 5 deg per frame squared or less in a walk. Runs flex faster and need their
own band; until one is set, judge a run knee by eye for pops and stalls.

At a contact, neither knee sits in its final few degrees before locking, because close to full reach a tiny shift of the hip or
ankle turns into a large jump of the knee angle. Between keys a knee or elbow never reaches the limb's full lock: key the
hips inside the leg's reach. A support knee that zigzags near straight usually means the hip height and the heel
roll were keyed out of step with each other; fix the slopes until a plot of reach per frame is one smooth line. While the support foot is
flat, the hips moving past the ankle stretch the leg, so their allowed height drops between the passing and the
frame the mass arrives over the ball.

### CNT8. Fast landings touch down between frames
stages: blocking, passes

On a landing or a quick step, the true touch happens between two frames. Key the leg long with the heel just above the
floor, then on the following frame put the sole down with the knee bent and bending. A fully straight leg standing on a
fully flat foot never appears; such a key looks posed for the camera. In the air, give the landing leg time to reach and lead with
the heel. The drop to the floor takes one or two frames, the foot can hang on its heel and then come down hard, and
once it is flat the toes may flap a few degrees past flat for a frame or two. Walks keep the heel, slap, toes contact.

### CNT9. The weight arrives after the foot
stages: blocking, passes

The foot plants first and the body follows. In a walk the low point of the hips comes one to three frames after the
contact, never on it, with more delay for a heavier body and about one frame in a run. Getting the body over the support
foot typically takes three or four frames after the heel lands. When the body is about to rise onto a foot, that
foot is planted two or so frames earlier.

The height of the pelvis makes one smooth wave per step. Its fall slows before the heel arrives and the low is a
rounded turn one or two frames later, never a V on the strike frame. The trunk takes each landing as well: a small
forward fold just after the contact that starts low in the spine, climbs one section per frame, and ends with the head
nodding last. A lift key added to rescue a knee is not a fix if it makes the pelvis pop.

### CNT10. A foot lifts only after the weight has left it
stages: blocking, passes, checks

A foot may leave the floor only once the hips have carried the mass over the staying foot: one frame before the lift,
the staying foot should carry at least about 60 % of the weight. The weight foot stays flat and exactly still until
that happens, and it rotates only as it comes up. It therefore rises unloaded, and that unloaded lift is the visible
proof that the shift happened. Move one foot at a time: the other lifts only once this one is flat. Any travel begins
with the hips moving sideways, and a larger shift the other way gives the freed limb more time. An abrupt change of
speed needs a reason. Outside a declared jump some foot touches the floor on every frame (CNT19), and only declared
events may break these rules.

When a walk ends on one leg, the hips first sink slightly and carry the bulk of their sideways travel onto the planted
leg, while the leaving foot still rolls on its ball; only then does it step.

### CNT11. At toe-off the body is already over the front foot
stages: blocking, checks, self_review

When the rear foot lifts, the mass already sits on the front foot or is travelling onto it. By the passing it is
directly above the supporting foot, and later it moves in front of that foot, falling into the next step. A front
foot planted so far ahead that the body is still behind it at the lift would topple backward. This fault is invisible
at the contact; look for it on the first airborne frame of the rear foot and at the up, with one panel per toe-off showing the support
heel and toe, the centre of mass and its direction of travel. On slow frames the mass stays within about 3 cm of the
base of the feet.

A sneak keeps the body back when the front foot touches, yet the rear foot stays down until the mass has
transferred onto the front foot.

### CNT12. The push-off is the power
stages: blocking, passes

The rear foot pushes; it is never dragged. Its heel stays down until the mass reaches the ball, then rises
progressively while the body passes over it, so the rise of the hips comes partly from the heel peeling. The foot
stays on the floor as long as it can, rolls onto the ball, then pivots about a fixed toe tip, and the toe tip leaves
the floor after everything else. The extension reads from the hips out through the thigh and calf to the toe; a more explosive action
snaps it harder. A bare foot rolls from the heel along the outer edge and off the big toe. When two frames of the push
look the same, add a visible in-between of the roll on the ball. If a peel can only be made in one frame, move the
body onto the ball a frame earlier instead. A rear foot that is already flat at the down takes a small roll toward its
lift, around 6 deg; double that already looks too much.

### CNT13. Roll on the ground, rotate in the air
stages: blocking, passes

While a foot is on the floor, it moves by its roll controls: heel, ball and toe pivots. From the frame it leaves, it
moves by rotation. Key both sides of that switch. The toe-off is a deliberate corner, like the slap at the contact;
outside those two moments the foot keys are not on consecutive frames. On the push-off the toes drag behind on their
own control, and the leaving foot lifts mostly straight up, judged from the side. Block the feet with translation,
roll and toe only; leave foot rotations and twists for polish, and measure the stride again whenever a roll key or a
hip rotation changes.

### CNT14. The swing foot follows its heel
stages: passes

In the swing the heel leads and the rest of the foot lags about a frame behind, then flops into place. The heel path
rises steeply as it peels, peaks early, around the down, then skims low and almost flat through the passing and the
up, and hooks down onto the heel at the end. It is never a rainbow arc. The foot's roll is largest at the down and
unwinds toward the strike, with the toes pulled up to clear the floor. A foot held flat in the air looks stiff. Track
the foot's arc at the ankle, never at the toe or the heel.

### CNT15. The swing leaves slowly, passes fast and arrives with speed
stages: blocking, passes

By default the swing foot eases off the floor over a few close frames, crosses the middle in wide spacing and lands
still carrying its forward speed, typically about 0.9 m/s at a walk, with no float down. Approaching a contact, the swing
knee reaches its height one frame or so after the body tops out, and the foot falls in at the very end, quickly; never let the curve average the swing leg
halfway. A foot that has begun to travel keeps one direction in world space until it touches down; breakdowns never
send it backward and forward, even in flight. A reversed foot, snapped up and set down slowly, is a declared trait for a careful, sneaky or
mime-like step, never a way to show weight.

### CNT16. In front view the feet track the centre line
stages: blocking, passes, checks, self_review

Seen from the front, the feet land near the middle line beneath the body, both at the contact and at the passing,
rather than a hip width apart: adult heel centres typically sit about 10 to 12 cm apart, well inside hip width.
Balance sets the width, so a step lands wider on the side the pelvis drifted toward, and a heavy body steps about 30 %
wider. Travelling forward, the swing foot swings a little wide of the standing foot around the passing, then turns
back inward to land. That bow needs at least two breakdowns on the foot's side channel; one gives a jerky, linear
feel. The legs never cross: each ankle and knee stays on its own side, and no foot passes the midline by more than
about 3 cm; a foot that does cross is a catwalk and must be declared. Check feet passing through each other from a low
side angle before polish.

A walk can look right from the side while the front shows these faults, so watch the front view at every gate and read
it by eye. A wide stance, around 28 cm for example, can pass a width measure and still miss the bow.

### CNT17. Keep a walking step low
stages: checks

Measure a lifted foot by whichever of heel, ball or toe is highest, so a raised toe also counts as lift. As a
typical bound, the lift stays under about 4.5 cm when the pelvis is 0.45 m high or lower and about 12 cm for a
standing adult, scaled in between. A character walk may lift higher when the character calls for it. The very small
toe clearance of a real gait is not the target.

### CNT18. Every foot has a stated intent
stages: blocking, checks

On every key, each foot, planted or free, has a named intent: flat, planted, cocked or pointed. Its angle between
shin and foot sits inside the ankle's real range around the rig's rest. The measured angle decides, never a note, a
remembered sign of a channel or a first glance at a picture; probe each rotation channel's sign before trusting it.
Feet and knees point the way the character chooses, consistently, with the knee and toe roughly aligned from two
angles. A foot on the floor stays within 90 deg of the pelvis heading, and typically well inside 80 deg.

### CNT19. Something always holds the body up
stages: checks

Unless the shot contains a jump, every frame has at least one foot on the floor. A foot never touches down for a
single frame and lifts again.

### CNT20. The foot roll depends on gait, footing and body
stages: plan, blocking

At run speed the foot lands on the ball and goes flat almost at once, with no heel strike; it stays flat through the
down, the heel peels on the up and the toe leaves last. A stiff ankle removes the roll. A very heavy creature can come down on a
flat sole or on the front of the foot. Shoes change it: a heel holds the foot pointed and the knee near locked. Climbing a stair, the
whole sole meets the step at once and the rear foot lingers; descending, the landing absorbs more than on a floor.
A backward step lands toe first, lowers the heel, rocks and settles flat; a twisting foot lands on its edge and rolls
flat within a frame. A trait of one limb, such as a stiff ankle, stays on that limb in all its poses, also on frames
where the other leg carries the action.

### CNT21. Slides, drags and pivots are declared contacts
stages: plan, passes, checks

A foot that moves while touching the floor is a different contact type and is written on the plan, so the lock checks
read it as intended. A dragged foot stays on or near the floor, turned out, with the knee and ankle trailing. A turn
twists the first planted foot as a pivot. When a walk slows to a halt, its last short step may arrive on the toe and slide in. A sliding
foot lands heel first and cushions in on broken tangents; a slide that halts and starts again should drop the key in its
middle, and a long slow foot move reads floaty, so shorten it. When braking hard, the rear sole stays flat and the
front heel digs and ploughs forward. On ice or any slippery floor the loaded feet slide the way they push, the legs splay, and the body sinks as
the base widens.

### CNT22. The feet catch the mass
stages: blocking, passes

The support foot is the fixed end of the chain and the body moves over it, pivoting on the ball of that foot. Build
each pose upward: first the foot and where its weight sits, then knee, pelvis, spine and arms. Set the motion of
the centre of gravity first and let the feet break while you do; then move each foot to where the weight needs
catching, in front of the mass. Never bend the body's motion to reach a foot pose. Whenever a foot changes, look again at
the whole chain above it, and after any hip or spine change step through every leg pose again from the side.

### CNT23. Fix the foot's parent channels first and retime a step as a block
stages: passes

Work a foot in order: translation, then roll, ball and toe, then twists. A change of even one frame to the translation
undoes the work layered on top, so refine a layer only as far as the one beneath it is settled. To retime a step, key
every channel of the foot on the step's first and last frames, remove stray keys, and slide or scale that block to
whole frames. Both keys of a planted foot hold the same value; set the step length by moving the pair together until the
foot lands where the weight needs it, then play it back and adjust again.

### CNT24. Hand over control of a contact over several frames
stages: passes, checks

When two controls share one job, such as the foot roll and the foot IK both setting the foot's angle and toes, or a
pivot and its parent, the job passes from one to the other over several frames, never on one. Let the roll carry into
the early swing and fade to zero before the passing, while the IK only carries the ankle along its path. A toe pinned to the floor
while the body runs away from it should peel over about a frame. Across any hand-over frame, measure four things: the
toe joint, how the foot meets the floor in angle, the ankle speed, and any sideways drift of the ankle.

A switch between IK and FK is different: make it on a single frame using the matching tool of the rig, so positions agree within
about 1 mm and angles within about 0.5 deg. Use IK for planted, weight-bearing feet and FK from the frame a foot leaves
when contacts are brief. When a pose offset is layered over captured motion, alter the offset on a planted foot only during
frames when that foot steps or pivots, and hold it until its last move.

### CNT25. Hands, seats and trapped parts are contacts too
stages: blocking, passes

A hand on a surface or object works in the space of what it touches, and switches to that space exactly when the
touch starts or stops. On a chair, a rising body tips forward over the leading edge of the seat and never slides back; check it
like a planted foot in a side view. A hand or foot that is caught, held or gripped stays pinned for the whole reaction
while the body moves around it, drifting no more than about 2 mm a frame.

When two characters touch, or one reacts to the other, block both in the same pass on a single timeline. Plan in
frames the delay from each action to its reaction, so the answer neither lands on the same frame by chance nor drifts
late. Set heights, reaches and hand positions from both bodies' real proportions, so neither one strains toward the
contact with a locked elbow or a raised shoulder.

### CNT26. Leave reach to spare at a contact
stages: passes, deliver

Use FK for limbs that swing freely and IK for limbs that hold a contact. At each contact the limb stays a little bent
and the touching point stays unobstructed, so that a later adjustment to height, slope or character size can still reach. If the limb would have to straighten fully to
get there, move the root instead.

### CNT27. Decide travelling or in place, and match the speed to the stride
stages: brief, plan, blocking, checks

Before blocking starts, write on the plan whether a cycle travels with root motion or stays in place; in a game the
root is where the engine believes the character to be. A loop is in place by default, and root motion is made only
when the author asks for it: the engine moves the character, and the cycle is judged where it stands. Either way the travel per frame equals the step length divided
by the frames per step, and every root or camera move is solved around that so no planted foot slips. A travelling
root moves on a straight, even line under the feet, keyed only at the ends of the cycle or of each pair of steps, while
the body carries the surges and the bob around it. A following camera rides the root, so any jerk there shakes the
whole view. The root's position and heading agree at every join, and the root is never switched off. In place, every
planted foot slides backward by exactly that amount on every frame, which in a game equals the speed the engine gives
the capsule, and the shot declares that speed so the checks add the travel back and still catch a skating foot. When the required speed changes, retime or rebuild the cycle; no camera or root move ever
excuses a skating foot.

### CNT28. Seams and joins keep the foot phase
stages: plan, deliver

No foot lands on the opening or closing frames of a loop, since a landing there jolts at the seam; shift the steps and
note it on the plan. For every transition, write down which foot is where on the outgoing clip's final frame and on the
incoming clip's opening frame, and never let a foot slide backward against the body through the blend. A game loop
that can start from a standing idle or a start clip begins with the legs scissoring past each other, and the leg in
the air is the one that stood further back in the idle, so it travels forward as the blend runs. Make a clip feel
responsive with an early interrupt frame rather than a shorter blend; if a join pops, lengthen the blend. Clips with
planted feet keep high compression precision on the legs.

### CNT29. A prop's contact is a corner
stages: blocking, passes

A bouncing prop meets the surface on a sharp corner: fast in and fast out at the surface value, a broad rounded turn
away from it, and the steepest slope on the frame just before the touch. Never key a contact flat or on a default
spline. Every touch removes part of the forward speed as the bounce height shrinks, so travel and height fall together.
A light, soft object such as a dropped paper card settles gently instead: it lands on one edge, tips back and forth
once or twice with each tip smaller, and comes to rest flat, with no bounce and no squash.

### CNT30. Read every contact frame by frame on a shadowed floor
stages: checks, self_review

Check each step in side and front views, frame by frame, with a per-frame read-out of knee angles, ankle paths and
pelvis path. Render with a ground plane and a contact shadow: the shadow should join the foot at every plant and
separate from it at every lift. Without that visible meeting the character floats, whatever weight the motion carries.

After any change inside three frames of a contact, read the knee bend, the pelvis low and the foot angle again, on
both legs, before the pass is shown. These three move as one system near a contact.
