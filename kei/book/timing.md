# Timing and holds

kind: principle

Timing decides when each pose lands and how long each part of a move lasts. These rules cover frame counts, ones and
twos, beats and rhythm, retiming, and holds that stay alive.

### TIM1. Timing is when, spacing is how far
stages: plan, blocking

Timing is the frame on which each pose arrives and the number of frames an action takes. Spacing is the distance a part
covers from one frame to the next. Fix the timing first, starting from how long the action really lasts. Put every
contact and every extreme on a whole frame, then solve the spacing between those frames. A contact is a single moment:
its frame closes one arc and opens the next, and the frames on either side of it sit at roughly the same height, like
mirror images.

### TIM7. Count in the delivery rate and know the frame by feel
stages: plan, blocking, self_review

Game animation usually plays at 30 fps. Measured film timings are usually counted at 24 fps. Convert by time: frames
at 30 equal frames at 24 times 1.25, rounded, and keep the 24 fps figure next to the converted one. Before judging
timing, calibrate the eye at the delivery rate on spans of 1, 3, 8, 12, 16 and 24 or 30 frames, so each written count
has a felt length. Broad film counts converted this way are a guide for a broad style only, never a target for realistic
motion.

### TIM8. The viewer, the medium and the style set the pace
stages: brief, plan, self_review

The brief names who watches and where: film, television, gameplay, a cinematic or the web. The plan then states the
timing style, broad or naturalistic, and every move is judged against it; one move in a different style is a fault.
Within the style, the forces at work (weight, push, momentum) set the frame counts. The amount of push is specific to the
scene: a wild cartoon may take about ten times the exaggeration of photoreal work, and timing that must match live
action or captured humans is changed least of all.

### TIM16. Reference timing must match the shot's length
stages: references, study

A reference acted over twice the frames of the shot plays at half speed and is of little use for timing. A long take can
still teach the mechanics. Record or choose reference at the pace the shot needs before taking frame counts from it.

### TIM17. Plan in beats, then poses
stages: plan

Split the action into beats first: anticipation, action, and reaction or recovery, plus the start and the final. A beat
is not a pose; it may need several. Start with one pose per beat and add more only when the idea does not read. On the plan
sheet, give every beat a kind (hold, action, anticipation, cycle) and a length in frames; together the lengths fill the
clip exactly. Mark every frame an event must hit, such as a contact, an impact or a gameplay frame. A beat can only get
longer if a neighbour gives up the same number of frames.

### TIM18. Place the main accents first
stages: plan, blocking

Pick out the handful of moments the shot hangs on, usually three to five, and pin each one to an exact frame before
anything else. The smaller business then fills the gaps, and every piece of it gets a precise frame count. Where the
shot needs contrast, plan it as a section of its own: for example, a long, quiet glance held after a frantic scramble.
Write all of this timing down before animating, since moving a number on paper costs minutes. A continuous action such
as a tremble goes on the plan as a span, and its frame counts change later only where a beat fails to read.

### TIM9. One timing personality per character and emotion
stages: plan, blocking, self_review

Give each character in each emotional state one timing style, such as snappy, or slow when sad, and keep it for the
whole shot. A sudden change of pace is allowed only as a motivated surprise or a gag. The mood sets two dials. The
first is tempo: low for heavy-hearted moods such as mourning or defeat, high for buoyant ones such as excitement or
relief, and measured for guarded ones such as doubt or caution. The second is the share of time held: large in the
heavy-hearted moods, with frequent long pauses and sinking breaths; small in the buoyant moods, where every landing
rebounds; and in the guarded moods, enough held time for the face to be read. Loose parts follow the first dial:
slack when the tempo is low and heavy-hearted, springy when it is high and buoyant.

### TIM14. Exaggerate timing as deliberately as poses
stages: plan, blocking, passes

Move into poses faster than life and hold them longer than life. Squeeze certain beats and lengthen the rest. A duration
taken from reference or capture is only a starting value: locate where it is quick and where it is slow, then make that
difference larger, for example by giving a slow fold twice the frames of the quick rise it leads into. Timing
that could have been filmed is live action. Each push is an acting choice, because looking quickly and looking slowly
express different thoughts; note the thought speed (snap or consider) next to each timed beat. Beside a realistic world
such as filmed footage or motion capture, keep the timing real and put the push into the poses.

### TIM12. Push each section off real speed, in both directions
stages: plan, blocking, passes

Set every section of the shot slightly quicker or slightly slower than its real duration, and swap between the two as
the shot goes on. Even pacing everywhere kills a shot: flag any shot in which every move lasts within about 10 % of its
real duration. When every move has the same speed and size, remove ideas until the ones left each own a distinct pose,
then speed some moves up. In realistic game work the push stays small and is declared on the plan.

### TIM13. A repeated rhythm is a declared device
stages: plan, blocking

A beat repeated at an even gap gives the viewer something to hold onto: for example, a few knocks on a door, six
frames apart. A beat that short is keyed on ones. Each repeat keeps the same spacing pattern with a new pose, the
largest variation comes last, and the rhythm breaks at the story point. Most action needs no rhythm at all; a
repeated beat is chosen and written on the plan.

### TIM40. An emotion beat takes the smallest movement that reads, then time to land
stages: plan, blocking, self_review

Name the feeling, then find the size of movement that carries it with a test loop. Start from the smallest size, a
held pose. While the thought does not read, step up one size by bringing in one more part of the body: a small shift,
then a single gesture, then the whole body, then full action. Stop at the first size that reads. Budget the beat's
frames in two spans: one in which the feeling arrives and the viewer reads it, and a short margin in which the viewer
savours it. Cut away before the beat starts to drag. When contrasting feelings follow each other, each gets a brief
but sufficient share of both spans. Gameplay often drops the margin, so there the feeling must arrive and read
quickly.

### TIM46. Build an escalation in stages
stages: plan, blocking

Keep the biggest extreme for the peak, and make every step before it larger and quicker than the last. A physical or
comic escalation opens small and almost playful, then grows until the character can no longer govern it, which shows
in nearby objects that get caught up and pulled along. After the peak comes a breakdown, then a rest reached slowly
over many frames. Give each stage its frames on the plan. In a chase or a panic no beat is slower or plainer than the
one before, and bodies may stretch weight and gravity as long as they stay believable.

### TIM39. A chain of surprises lands a planned hold each time
stages: plan, blocking

When surprises or setbacks follow one another, stop after each one on a pose that says a single attitude, for example confused,
cross, wary or stubborn. Each of these stops lasts at least the read time. Order the setbacks so each peak is bigger
than the previous one. Repeating the same gag in the same way does not escalate.

### TIM36. Limited animation is a declared style
stages: brief, plan

Long holds, few poses per second and reused drawings save work, and they suit only a simple, flat, graphic design. They are never
a default. They belong only to a shot whose style study has chosen a limited, graphic look. A realistic character moves
fully, and its holds stay alive.

### TIM3. Free flight and swings take the time physics gives them
stages: plan, blocking, checks

Air time for a jump or a bounce of height h is twice sqrt(2h/g). Counted at 24 fps, going up 1 m and coming back takes
about 22 frames, with 11 of them on the way down; a 0.25 m hop needs about 11 and a 2.5 m leap about 34. From rest, a
falling body drops roughly 0.08 m after 3 frames, 0.3 m after 6, 1.2 m after 12 and 4.9 m after 24, so twice the time
means four times the distance. A pendulum swings in 2 pi sqrt(L/g): a 1 m
pendulum takes about 24 frames per swing and a 0.25 m one about 12. Wide swings run a little slower, so integrate the
motion instead of using the small-angle formula.

### TIM4. Each rebound shrinks by the restitution
stages: plan, blocking, checks

With restitution e, every bounce reaches e squared of the previous height and lasts e times as long. A rubber ball (e
about 0.8) dropped from 1 m gives bounces of roughly 17, 14, 11, 9 and 7 frames. A bowling ball (e about 0.25) makes one
small hop of about 5 frames. With the same timing, the heights alone change the weight: rebounds that barely leave the
floor read heavy, and rebounds that keep most of the drop read light. Spacing from the apex grows steadily toward the
ground and shrinks again on the way up; equal steps look dead.

### TIM5. A lively ball touches the ground for two frames
stages: blocking, passes, checks

A bouncy ball spends two frames on the ground: first the touch, then a single squashed frame. A third ground frame makes
the ball look sluggish, as if it paused to push off. Each frame beyond the base count of the style reads as an extra hop.

### TIM6. Heavy things are slow to start and slow to stop
stages: plan, blocking, passes

For every moving body or prop, count two spans: how long it takes to reach top speed from a standstill, and how long it takes to come back
to a standstill. A heavy one has long spans at both ends. Under a steady, moderate push it gathers speed for a little over a
second (about 28 frames at 24, 35 at 30), until each frame's step is about five times as wide as the first one, then
keeps that speed and needs a long distance to lose it. A light one has short spans, because the air lets it go and
brakes it just as quickly: it is at full speed within about a sixth of a second (3 to 4 frames at 24, 4 to 5 at 30)
and at rest again the same time later. A small force that lasts only an instant leaves a heavy body where it was,
while a modest force kept up long enough sets it going: a quick shove does not roll a parked car, a steady lean does.
Whenever a heavy thing changes its motion, the screen shows a force large enough, or lasting long enough, to explain
the change.

For the same action, a heavy character also sits lower, prepares more and takes about 1.5 times the frames of a light
one.

### TIM43. A stagger replays one smooth series out of order
stages: blocking, passes

For a skid, a shudder, a shiver or a shake, start with an ordinary, evenly eased run of in-betweens between two
extremes. Then play those frames in an order that keeps doubling back yet gains ground overall, on ones or twos: the plainest
moves on by two and back by one, again and again. Larger, irregular jumps through the run make it more
violent, and brief freezes of 1 to 3 frames add variety. When some progress is wanted, as in a strain or the end of a
strong take, the run eases into its last frame while the shudder goes on. Write the cause (cold, fear, laughter, rage,
strain) and the span on the plan. Sorted by value, the frames of the shaking channel all fall on one smooth path, with
no pose outside the two extremes.

### TIM42. A fast vibration is extremes only, on ones
stages: blocking, passes, checks

Key a fast vibration, such as a plucked steel ruler on a desk edge, as extremes only: one side, then the other, on
consecutive frames, each swing smaller as it closes on rest. The same pattern held for two frames per extreme reads as a
lazy wobble, not a buzz. A longer or heavier vibrating thing, such as a tall lamp post just after a van clips it,
spends more frames on each swing from one side to the other: roughly 4 at 24, 5 at 30. Every reversal leaves its
extreme more slowly than it arrived. A tremble on a held pose is one secondary key that pushes the far extreme of the
pose further, with the motion spread between the two across the hold, never a single shake on one frame.

Repeat the two extremes for as long as the cause lasts, then close in on rest by one step each time. Give each part its
own amount, for example a chill in which the hands rattle hard while the head barely moves, and vary the passing shapes
so the shake does not turn mechanical.

### TIM47. A tired or heavy step drops instead of rolling
stages: blocking, passes

For a step that drags with fatigue or weight, the middle breakdown is an up that lags: the swing foot is left behind,
the body reaches forward and up with effort, the back reverses, and the figure seems close to falling. A breakdown
before it favours the key and dips the head before it rises. From that up, work straight ahead into the down: the
body dips below where the down key will finally sit, the foot flips up, and loose parts such as ears, a scarf or a
soft belly lag and settle one after another. Leave out the heel-to-toe roll and put a big gap into the down, so the
step lands as a flop rather than a counted roll. The anticipation is slow, the drop is fast with no in-betweens, and a
short, contained cushion follows. The missing roll is a declared choice.

### TIM45. A reaction starts as its cause peaks
stages: plan, blocking, checks

When one thing triggers another, such as a door slam that makes a dozing figure jolt, the responding part leaves its
pose as the trigger reaches its extreme, at most a frame or two later. Empty frames between them read as a pause nobody
chose, so remove them. The only exception is a pause the plan names as acting: the hold inside a take, a moment of
thought, or the wait while news sinks in. As a starting value, more than about 2 unexplained frames at 24 fps (3 at 30)
is a fault. Starting the response early is a fault too.

### TIM38. Time a broad take from one span
stages: plan, blocking, passes

However strong its poses, give a broad take one span, from its accent to a readable settle, of 0.6 to 0.8 of a second
(14 to 19 frames at 24, 18 to 24 at 30), and derive its parts from that span. The drop takes about 0.3 of it and the
settle about 0.4. The rise between them runs from a single popped frame up to 6 eased frames, never more than 6; when
the rise is a single pop, the drop and the settle share the rest of the span between them. Before the take, give a
beat of noticing of about an eighth of a second (3 frames at 24, 4 at 30), in which the head or a loose part lifts
slightly before the drop. A snappier take sits near 16 frames at 24 (20 at 30), with an anticipation a quarter of
that length. Any extreme distortion is gone again within 3 frames at 30, or 2 at 24; after it the shape is back at
normal size, keeps the attitude of the take, and its loose parts point along the take's direction. A double take uses
the same phases; on the way up, let the head, still compressed, turn quickly left and right.

### TIM37. A reaction waits for its message
stages: plan, blocking

The news needs time to sink in before anything answers it. Count from the frame the stimulus lands to the first
frame of the squash: that gap is still, and it lasts at least a fifth of a second, which is 6 frames at 30 or 5 at 24. Its
length belongs to the character, closer to that floor the quicker its mind and the smaller its body, longer as either
grows. After the gap, the body squashes, travels past its end pose, and comes back to settle. The settled pose carries
the whole reaction and holds for one settle unit, half a second (12 frames at 24, 15 at 30). A player character in
gameplay skips the still gap; enemies, other characters and cinematics keep it.

### TIM29. Strikes are fast and their hits are held
stages: plan, blocking, passes, checks

In a broad style a strike takes about 3 to 4 frames at 24 (4 to 5 at 30) from wind-up to hit. Fewer frames than that
reads too snappy. The extreme is felt for a single frame. What the viewer then reads is the pose after
the recoil, kept on screen for roughly half a second (10 to 12 frames when counted at 24 fps, up to 15 at 30), sinking and drifting a little the whole time.
When anything thrown or swung fast comes to a stop, its end pose holds for the same half second, 15 frames at 30 or
12 at 24.
After a soft arrival the hold instead drifts on toward a stronger version of the pose. A hit-stop belongs to the game
engine, which freezes the accent frame at runtime; it is not keyed into the clip as a hold. If a hit-stop must be baked
in, the plan declares it and a second version without it ships for a miss.

### TIM15. Momentum changes the next interval
stages: plan, blocking, passes, checks

After a strong push, the body reaches its next pose sooner than the even grid suggests. Arrive at the final pose a
little early and hold there. The recovery into the final pose carries a small compression, never a glide. A body that has just come up to its
feet quickly holds there for two thirds of the settle unit, a third of a second, which is 10 frames at 30 or 8 at
24, before it moves on.

### TIM19. Give every idea time to read
stages: plan, blocking, checks

Work read times out in seconds, then convert. A pose that must read, such as a point, a settle, a hit pose or the read
pose of a take, holds for at least a quarter of a second: 6 frames at 24, 8 at 30. Inside very fast action only, that
floor drops to a fifth of a second: 5 at 24, 6 at 30. Anything shorter than a sixth of a second (4 frames at 24, 5 at
30) is not seen at all, so spend the frames on the main beats instead. A crowded, busy beat stays on screen longer than
a simple one. Words on screen get two thirds of a second each (16 frames at 24, 20 at 30).

### TIM20. End on a readable picture
stages: plan, blocking, deliver, checks

An action ends after its extension, its recoil and its rest, then one to three more frames. Never end a clip, cut or
blend out on the extension frame or halfway through a move. Inside one fixed camera, each move completes, out to full
extension and back to rest, before the last frame. Before a cut, leave a still, readable picture for 8 frames or more at
24 fps, or 10 at 30. When a character looks out of frame and the next shot shows what it sees, hold the look
for the settle unit of half a second (12 frames at 24, 15 at 30) before the cut. Keep the shot no longer than its point needs; any extra length is agreed against the frame limit of
the brief.

### TIM33. A hold must be able to hold and must have a reason
stages: plan, blocking, checks

Every hold on the plan carries its reason and a length. Run two tests on it. The first asks whether the body can stay:
a pose kept for more than a few frames has its centre of mass over the supporting foot or feet, and a body caught off
balance at an extreme passes on quickly and is never parked there, unless the imbalance is the joke. The second asks
how long the drama wants it. Measure the answer in the settle unit, half a second (12 frames at 24, 15 at 30), and set
the length by eye from there: a stop that only marks the end of a move sits near one unit, a pose meant to be looked
at for its own sake runs longer, and a pose that passes on sooner runs shorter. The rules for each kind of hold give
its starting length (TIM29, TIM15, TIM20, TIM34, TIM19). Paused on any held frame, the pose reads as a picture by
itself.

### TIM30. A hold stays alive
stages: blocking, passes, checks

No hold is a flat, lifeless curve. Give it one or two tiny keys, hundredths of a unit or under a degree on the body's
centre, too small to see but enough to feel. Each hold drifts slightly further in the direction the pose settled,
without seeming to head anywhere new. When a pose is pushed, set keys on all of its channels at the start of the hold,
so the drift continues instead of sliding back to rest. Bend the tangent of the final key as well, so no channel begins
or ends abruptly. The exception is any channel fixed by a contact: a planted foot keeps its height and position with no
drift at all.

### TIM32. Constant slow motion through a hold is floaty
stages: passes, self_review

Do not avoid holds. Keeping the whole body slowly moving through a hold reads swimmy. Where an idea has to land, hold for
real, and give the hold life through a slight drift in one part, such as a hand or the head, on top of the invisible keys on the
root. In a hold almost any motion is noise, so keep holds clean and smooth while the strikes stay sharp. Gather all
the visible motion of a hold in one small area, such as the hands on a prop or the face, and make that area the
centre of interest of the beat while the rest stays still. A character whose reaction is the subject of the shot keeps
one attitude, varied only by small accents.

### TIM34. Long still holds have a ceiling
stages: plan, blocking, passes, checks

With every channel flat, a pose starts to look dead after about 1.7 seconds, which is 50 frames at 30 or 40 at 24.
Take that as the upper limit for a declared still, never a goal. A still comic pose that is there to be looked at
gets two settle units, a full second (24 frames at 24, 30 at 30), well under the limit. A pause stretched far past life on purpose, or an expected action held
back until the viewer longs for it, is a valid exaggeration only when declared, and its fully still span stays under
the limit. In a mostly static shot, pick one or two moving elements, such as the breath, a slow drift of the head or a
hand, each with its own motive. Arrive in every hold on an ease, so the stop does not jolt.

### TIM35. A follower holds back
stages: plan, blocking, passes

Only one character or part leads at any moment. Anything outside the focus, a second character or a minor
part, keeps a moving hold and never starts a new gesture that pulls the eye. A listener keeps its head on the speaker in a moving hold until the roles swap. If even
a moving hold distracts, a declared still is better than a distracting motion. The lead never freezes.

### TIM23. Lay the poses out first, then retime the whole pass
stages: blocking

One valid order is to block the storytelling poses first with no timing, one pose per frame, judged only on whether the
idea reads. The poses are then spaced on an even placeholder grid, every four frames for example, in time order, each
breakdown added once both neighbours exist and the hold placeholders last. Retiming then moves whole keys of every
control together, which only works if every control is keyed on every blocking pose. Building the timing in from the
first key is equally valid. A delivered blocking is always timed.

### TIM44. Later passes keep the accents of the pose test
stages: blocking, passes, self_review

Play the timed story poses stepped at the real rate as a pose test, and judge them for rhythm and read; much of the
animating is decided here. Hold each key on it for anything from a sixth of a second to a full second (4 to 24 frames
at 24, 5 to 30 at 30). Show each end pose held from the frame its cushion or settle begins, while the final pose keeps
its true arrival frame. Keep that playblast and run every later pass next to it: each key must still land on the same
frame, just as sharply. When a pass reads softer, look for the fault in the spacing between the keys, such as an even
ease, a late arrival or a lost hold, and fix that before touching a pose. Reject a replacement pose that blunts the
accent, however good it looks alone.

### TIM24. Retime by trading frames
stages: blocking, passes

The first timing is a rough estimate. When a story pose flashes by too quickly, give it more frames and take exactly
that many from the transition next to it, so the shot keeps its length. For example, a 4-frame story pose can grow to
9 while its 8-frame transition drops to 3, twelve frames before and after. Lengthen just the part that has to feel
slower. Make the large change first, then pull it back. Poses come first, timing second. Retiming is done when the
gaps are wide enough to place the breakdowns.

### TIM21. Too much action in too few frames
stages: blocking, passes

When a move reads rushed, suspect its length before its size: the most common timing fault is a move given too little
time. Give it twice the frames first, then ease each end with tighter spacing. Shrink the pose only if it still looks
too big after that. A loop gets no eased ends; every start, stop and single action does.

### TIM2. A slow move needs new timing, an even move needs new spacing
stages: blocking, passes, self_review

When a part feels sluggish, slide its keys to other frames. When it feels flat or mechanical, keep the frames and change
the values or the tangent handles. For example, three keys spaced five frames apart can become four keys spaced 3, 3
and 4 frames: a quick swing followed by a longer settle. With the hits held fixed, try at least two spacings and pick one
by eye before touching the timing. When a shot feels awkward without being slow, move the key beat one or two frames
earlier or later and compare.

### TIM27. Ones in the final, twos only for review
stages: blocking, passes, checks

Computer animation plays on ones in the final; drawing on twos was mostly a way to save labour. Stepped blocking may be
reviewed on twos. Where the gap between frames is wide, as in a run, a strike or a dodge, block more densely and key each
frame the curve cannot carry. A gap wider than the moving part itself makes it strobe. On twos, the frames next to an
extreme can show twice and outweigh it; hold the extremes longer instead. Never accept even, mechanical in-betweens:
vary the favour of each breakdown and cushion so no section is evenly spaced.

### TIM26. Lock the timing before the curves are smoothed
stages: blocking, passes

The timing is settled before the keys are converted to smooth curves; after that, only small retimes are made. The last
change before conversion is often an opening hold before the first move, roughly 12 frames at 24 fps, so the eye can
settle. Create it by sliding all keys of the first move and everything after it later in time, never by re-posing, and
keep the shot length.

### TIM11. Avoid the metronome
stages: blocking, passes, checks

A metronome repeats the same gap and the same size of swing, and timing built that way reads dull and predictable. Keys every fifth
or tenth frame give a heavy, mechanical beat. After blocking, list the beat lengths and the beat sizes, and change some
of each on purpose; in a two-strike loop the two intervals differ by at least two to one. Neighbouring holds and
transitions get different lengths, since two frames already make a visible difference. A cycle is the declared
exception and still varies inside its range. A bouncing series takes the same treatment once its clean decay works:
change a few of the bounce lengths and heights, but no bounce climbs above the one before it without a cause.

### TIM10. Review timing for rhythm, texture and readability
stages: passes, self_review

Rhythm is a tempo the viewer can latch onto, then vary or break with a bold action or a hold. Texture means several
timings inside one shot instead of one even, floaty pace. Readability means a first-time viewer understands each idea,
because each one stays on screen as long as it needs. Timing works like punctuation: build a rhythm, break it for
emphasis and keep the most extreme beat for the climax. Get the root timing right before the limbs.

On the plan, mark every section as either continual or rapid; a shot at a single speed throughout has no texture.
Design three things at once: repetition, like the even steps of a run; variety, like the uneven beats near a story
pose; and texture, the contrast of the continual sections with the rapid ones.

### TIM48. A revision note names a symptom; fix its cause
stages: passes, self_review

Before changing anything for a note, look: the curve of every channel with its speed per frame, the path traced on
screen, and each frame of the named part. Write down the cause; one sentence can hide several faults. Never add motion the object has
no cause for: a note asking to work a settle means letting the existing motion die out well. Notes that share a cause
are one problem. Keep the reviewed version untouched and write the new one beside it. List what was liked, hold it
fixed, and afterwards compare it with the old version by numbers; declare any change to it as a trade-off. Re-run
every check on each pass that changed, apply the same fix to other shots with the same fault, and test each fix
against the one-sentence idea of the shot. To tune a timing value, first try one that is clearly overdone, then
split the difference toward whichever side looked better; change one thing per version.
