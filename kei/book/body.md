# The body: proportions, ranges and posture

kind: subject

How a real human body stands, rests, bends, twists and gets down to the floor, with measured values from published
research. Kei uses these values to set rest poses, to keep joints inside believable limits and to judge a pose by eye.

### BIO1. A body at rest leans slightly forward from the ankles
stages: blocking, checks

In quiet standing the line of gravity passes through the ear, slightly forward of the hip axis, slightly behind the
knee and roughly 5 cm ahead of the ankle joint. Put the centre of mass about 3 to 7 cm forward of the ankles, never
straight above the heels. The knees are close to straight (within a few degrees) and the shins tip forward about 4 deg.
The trunk stays upright over the pelvis; a trunk pitched well forward reads as an animal or as a sprint start.

The spine keeps its curves: the low back hollow by about 50 deg, the upper back rounded by about 40 deg. The pelvis
sits tipped forward and down. The head is level, with the ear sitting just forward of the shoulder (an angle of about 50 deg
between the neck line and the horizontal at the base of the neck).

### BIO2. Rest feet sit about hip width with the toes turned a little out
stages: blocking, checks

At rest the heel centres are typically about 17 cm apart (10 to 25 cm), and the long axes of the two feet open by
about 14 deg. Feet pressed together, or wide apart with parallel toes, look posed rather than relaxed. Use parallel
feet only when the plan asks for a held, deliberate stance.

### BIO3. A relaxed arm hangs nearly straight with the hand beside the thigh
stages: blocking, checks

The upper arm hangs close to the trunk, within about 5 deg of vertical. The elbow stays soft at about 15 deg of bend
(roughly 8 to 25), never locked straight and never at 30 or more. The forearm angles outward by about 10 deg so the
hand clears the hip, a little more in women. The palm faces the side of the thigh, with the forearm near mid-rotation.

The shoulder blade rests rotated forward, so the point of the shoulder sits a little ahead of the chest's side plane.
The hand still lands on the outer seam of the thigh: on its mid-line in side view, outside it in front view, never on
its front. On a real adult the hanging fingertips reach about 37 % of body height. On a rig with short arms the hand
ends where the arm reaches, but every angle above still applies.

### BIO4. A relaxed hand curls; it is never flat and never a fist
stages: rig, blocking, checks

At rest the wrist is bent back about 16 deg (5 to 25). The fingers curl, with the middle knuckle bending most. Typical
bends, given as base / middle / end joint in deg: index 28 / 26 / 13; middle finger 33 / 30 / 13; ring finger
25 / 35 / 12; little finger 17 / 32 / 16. The middle-knuckle curl grows from the index to the little finger, a cascade. The thumb lies relaxed along
the side of the index, slightly bent. With the palm turned up, the fingers curl about a fifth more. On a rig with
fist hands, apply the wrist value only.

### BIO5. A joint held away from rest is doing a job
stages: plan, blocking, self_review

A relaxed joint settles where gravity and soft tissue leave it, at the values in BIO1 to BIO4. Any other position costs
muscle and so reads as intent. Examples: the arm lifted in front, the elbow flexed between 30 and 90 deg, the hands
kept ahead of the legs, the chin raised. Ask whether the character could keep the pose for ten minutes without
effort. When the answer is no, the plan states what the pose is for: waiting, guarding, carrying or presenting. When
the story says relaxed, every joint takes its rest value. A declared ready or combat idle counts as a held pose, so
the rest values do not judge it.

### BIO6. Nobody stands perfectly still
stages: plan, blocking, passes

Standing sway is mostly front to back, a slow rock at the ankles with the body nearly rigid above. Over a 10 s idle the
pelvis drifts only a few millimetres: about 3 to 25 mm front to back and 2 to 12 mm side to side. The main sway cycle
lasts about 3 s (about 72 frames). Any visible oscillation shorter than 16 frames reads as jitter.

A resting breath takes 3 to 5 s (72 to 120 frames); after effort it quickens to 1.5 to 3 s (36 to 72 frames). The chest
rises and falls; the pelvis and legs make up for it, and the head keeps its place in space. Never freeze a standing character dead,
never rock it visibly, and never lift the whole body with each breath.

### BIO7. A long idle moves between square standing and a hip-hang, at uneven intervals
stages: plan, blocking

Relaxed people stand either with the weight shared about evenly or with most of it (around 87 %) on one foot, and spend
little time in between. Women hang on one leg more often. A small fidget (a brief shift that returns, a toe adjustment,
a hand moving) comes about once a minute; a real change of weight every 3 to 5 minutes. Space them unevenly, and more
often as time and discomfort grow.

People hang on one hip for comfort or out of tiredness, and it actually costs more energy, so a fresh or alert character stands
square. A short game idle loop (5 to 10 s) is too brief for a real shift; put shifts in separate idle-break clips. In a
hip-hang the centre of mass sits over the loaded foot.

### BIO8. The hips move the weight from side to side; the ankles handle front to back
stages: blocking, passes

To put the weight on one leg, slide the pelvis over the stance foot, let the free hip drop a little and bend its knee,
and let the chest counter the move. The ankles do not tip the body sideways. Front to back balance in a normal stance
belongs to the ankles.

Seen from the front, the loaded leg drives upward into its hip joint, and everything else is suspended from that
point: the unloaded side drops, while the shoulders tilt against it. The flank over the loaded leg shortens and the
opposite flank lengthens, with the unloaded leg tucked in a little. Running slowly makes this alternation bigger, flat
then squeezed on every step; a walk or a jog that lacks it reads robotic.

### BIO9. Relaxed sitting rolls the pelvis back
stages: blocking, checks

Sitting is not the standing spine folded at the hips. When a person sits down, the pelvis tips backward by about 19
deg. The low-back hollow shrinks with the seat: about 48 deg standing, about 36 in a chair with lumbar support, about 18
in a plain chair, close to flat on a stool, and a little rounded when sitting on the floor. The chest drops slightly and the
head moves forward about 2 deg.

Upright sitting takes effort; the slump is the resting state, and a long sit shows small changes and a slow sink. Rest
the forearms or hands on the thighs, with the feet flat and the shins near vertical or slightly forward.

### BIO10. After hard effort the body rests braced on its knees
stages: plan, blocking

A common recovery pose bends the trunk forward roughly 30 to 60 deg, with the hands pushing on the lower thighs or
knees and the elbows straight or almost straight. These arms carry real weight. The head hangs and the shoulders lift
with each deep breath, every 36 to 72 frames, slowing over about a minute. The other common choice is standing upright
with the hands on the head or on the hips. Balance stays unsteady, with more sway and a wider stance, for roughly 5 to
10 minutes.

### BIO11. Age and mood reshape the rest posture as a whole
stages: understanding, plan, blocking

An old body rounds the upper back, rolls the pelvis back, bends the knees a little (about 1 deg more per decade) and
tips the shins forward. Its head comes forward while the gaze stays level. It shifts less often and less far. A sad
character drops the head forward, rolls the shoulders forward and down, sinks the chest and moves little. A confident
one stands taller with the shoulders back, the head up and more small movement. Change the whole chain together, not
one joint.

### BIO12. Weigh the body by real segment shares, whatever the rig looks like
stages: rig, weight, checks

In living young adults the share of body mass is typically: head about 7 %, trunk about 43 %, upper arm 2.6 to 2.7 %,
forearm 1.4 to 1.6 %, hand 0.6 %, thigh 14 to 15 %, shank 4.3 to 4.8 %, foot 1.3 to 1.4 %. One leg is about a fifth of
the body. An older table built from a few elderly cadavers gives a heavier head and trunk (8 % and 50 %) and a lighter
thigh (10 %); the tables disagree most at the thigh, because of where the cut between pelvis and thigh falls.

A stylised rig with a large head can measure a head near a quarter of its volume. Know which centre of mass each check
reads. When judging weight, count the head at its real share, not at its drawn volume. A heavy human stores its extra
mass mainly in the trunk, upper arms and thighs: roughly double what goes to the forearms and calves. A real obese
trunk is about 50 to 55 % of body mass (unchecked).

### BIO13. Angles carry across proportions; distances scale with the rig
stages: rig, plan, checks

A real adult has an upper arm about 0.19 of body height, a forearm about 0.15, a hand about 0.11, the hip joint at
about 0.53, and a head about 0.13 (some 7.5 heads tall). Stylised rigs often have shorter arms and a head of a fifth of
the height or more. Proportions are a design choice. Apply the joint angles in this rule book as stated. Scale every
distance and height (fingertip height, step width, dip depth) to the segment lengths of the rig in use, and judge
reach on the rig itself.

### BIO14. Keep joints inside real ranges unless a deep posture or a style says otherwise
stages: blocking, passes, checks

Typical passive ranges in adults aged 20 to 44: hip flexion about 130 to 134 deg, hip extension only about 17 to 18,
knee flexion 138 to 142, ankle dorsiflexion 13 to 14 on a straight knee (near 40 when the knee bends and the foot bears
weight), ankle plantarflexion 55 to 62, shoulder flexion about 170, elbow flexion 145 to 150, pronation 77 to 82,
supination 85 to 91. Women's elbows straighten about 5 deg past straight, men's about 1. Every range shrinks with age,
most at hip extension and in the trunk.

Deep postures go beyond the clinical limits. In a squat on the toes the knee folds to about 157 deg, and in a kneel to
about 143, because the thigh squeezes against the calf. Show that contact; never let the two pass through each other.
Breaking a joint past straight, cartoon fashion, must be declared as a style. A realistic counter-bend keeps within
the ranges above.

### BIO15. Everyday work uses only part of each range
stages: blocking, passes, self_review

Ordinary tasks use roughly half to two thirds of the range: the elbow between 30 and 130 deg, the forearm from 50 deg
of pronation to 50 of supination, the wrist up to 40 deg either way and turned toward the little finger by 20 to 40
deg. A working wrist held perfectly straight looks stiff. The upper arm lifts to about 110 deg before the trunk starts to help,
leaning up to about 23 deg and turning up to about 32. A pose near the end of a joint's range needs a cause in the
shot.

### BIO16. Heavy bodies lose range where tissue gets in the way
stages: rig, blocking, checks

On a heavy body, soft tissue restricts several moves: the arm across the chest, the arm behind the back, bending
backward, bending to the side and folding the knee. The loss is roughly 11 to 39 % (a low-confidence estimate). Reaching up and forward is not limited. Range is
not confidence: a heavy character still moves with intent. Heavy bodies, and some old ones, use a hand to get down to a
kneel.

### BIO17. The thigh turns about 45 deg each way in a young adult, less later
stages: blocking, checks

With the hip straight, a young adult turns the thigh in by about 44 to 54 deg and out by about 47 to 53; with the hip
bent to 90 deg, about 40 to 45 each way. Ordinary adults between 20 and 60 manage about 30 to 40 each way, usually turning
out more easily than in. Women tend to rotate further inward (knees falling in), men further outward (knees falling
out); use this as a cue for rest poses, not a rule. Age and weight reduce the range, so an old or heavy character changes
foot direction with a step instead of pivoting at the hip. Children have the most inward rotation, which fades through the
teens.

### BIO18. The pelvis and spine supply most of a long leg line
stages: blocking, passes, checks

Measured against the pelvis alone, the thigh flexes only about 95 to 107 deg and extends behind only about 5 to 15. The
rest of a knee-to-chest comes from the pelvis rolling back. The line of a long stride, a lunge, a split
or an arabesque therefore comes mostly from a tipped pelvis and an arched low and mid back, never from the hip
joint alone. A thigh more than about
20 deg behind the pelvis means the pelvis and spine are doing the work, or the style is declared.

When a trained dancer opens to 180 deg, the pelvis pitches over the front leg and the back arches to lift the
rear leg. An untrained character might open perhaps 90 to 120 deg between the thighs (an estimate, low confidence).

### BIO19. The foot rolls sideways only a little
stages: blocking, passes

The heel joint alone turns about 10 deg inward and 5 outward. The whole foot against the shin reaches about 35 inward
and 15 outward: the roll onto the outer edge is about twice the roll onto the inner edge. On flat ground a walking foot
rolls in about 5 to 10 deg after the heel lands. A large roll, on rough ground or in a sprain, takes the heel, the
midfoot and the ankle together. Teenagers and young women have the loosest ankles.

### BIO20. A forward bend rounds the back first and returns pelvis first
stages: blocking, passes, checks

Touching the toes with straight legs combines about 40 to 60 deg of low-back bend with about 53 to 70 deg of the pelvis
tilting forward over the thighs; the chest adds only about 10. The two blend, but the share changes: early in the bend the back does
about twice what the pelvis does, mid-bend they are even, and late in the bend the pelvis does more. Going down, the low
back leads the pelvis by about 2 to 7 frames.

On the way up the order reverses. The pelvis tucks back beneath the trunk before anything else, leading by about 3 to 7 frames, and the
back stays round until the last quarter of the rise, then unrolls. With a load in the hands the low back does more of
the straightening and the two move more together. An old body bends less in the back (about 8 deg) and more at the
hips (about 7 deg). A flat-backed bend all at the hips is a careful or trained bend; a rise that straightens the back
first while the pelvis stays tipped reads as pain.

### BIO21. A trunk twist happens in the middle of the chest
stages: blocking, passes, checks

At a full twist the upper chest rotates about 25 deg relative to the lower chest, most of it in the mid and lower chest
segments. The whole low back gives only about 7 to 9 deg each way; the spine as a whole about 34. To turn the shoulders
45 to 55 deg while standing, the pelvis rotates on the hips and the shoulder blades slide as well. Past about 35 deg
the pelvis swivels above the planted feet, or one heel comes up.

A strong twist is not a pure turn about the vertical. The low back side-bends a little to the opposite side (about 1.6 to
3.4 deg per segment) and flexes slightly forward; a pure turn looks like a turntable. On a rig, put most of the twist in
the upper spine and chest controls, give the lowest spine joints only a few degrees, and add a small opposing side-bend
low in the spine on a big twist.

### BIO22. Kneeling: lean, land the knees, rise over them, sit back
stages: plan, blocking, passes

A young person kneeling to both knees leans the trunk forward (hip bent about 60 deg) while the knees bend, sets the
knees down, brings the trunk up over them (hip about 15 to 30) and lets the pelvis sink back toward the heels while the knee
folds to roughly 140 to 146. Standing up repeats a deeper lean, about 75 deg. The hip and ankle peak before the knee
does; the ankle bends up about 40 deg, and thigh and calf twist about 20 deg against each other, since the knee does
more than hinge. Young people need no hands. Old and heavy people bend far forward, the hip near 110 deg, and push
with one hand on their knee, on a chair or on the floor.

A deep kneel or squat rests its weight on the calves and heels, which carry about half the load, so the pose can relax.
A one-knee kneel is a strained pose that does not last: the weight soon moves to a hand or the front thigh.

### BIO24. A trained dancer is turned out from the hip, not looser everywhere
stages: understanding, blocking, checks

Dancers gain outward hip rotation and lose inward rotation and the ability to bring the thigh across. Standing, walking
or resting, they keep the thighs rotated outward at the hip and the knees over the toes. An untrained character asked to turn out
twists the feet at the ankle and knee instead, so the feet point further out than the knees: that is the visible tell.
Dancers keep pointe and turnout with age but have less ankle dorsiflexion, so their heels-down squat is no deeper.

En pointe, the ankle points about 58 deg and the foot adds about 25 more, about 82 in all, so the foot continues the line
of the shin or goes slightly beyond it. A small knee bend (demi-plie) flexes the ankle up about 25 deg, about 35 with the
foot. An untrained rise onto the toes stays near 50 to 60 deg.

### BIO25. Skill shows in which joints correct the balance
stages: understanding, blocking, passes

A trained body balances with the small joints far from the trunk, such as the wrists and ankles, and moves its joints
in sequence. An untrained body corrects with the big joints near the trunk, mostly the hips, and moves several joints
together, so it looks stiffer. Confidence in these values is medium.

Upside down on the hands, the body stays straight and stiff from wrist to toe, and the balance is steered through the
fingertips pressing against the base of the palm. Each fix follows the drift some 4 to 6 frames late, so a believable
inverted hold wanders and gets pulled back. Skilled performers adjust with the wrists alone, plus a touch of shoulder;
beginners fold at the hips, hollow the back and shuffle their hands. When a dancer spins, the face stays fixed on one
target while the trunk rotates under it, and then the head snaps ahead of the trunk to reach that target again before
it; a beginner rotates face and chest as one. A raised back leg gets its height from a pelvis that tilts ahead and
opens, plus a back that arches, chiefly at the chest; an ordinary adult hip only opens backward about 17 deg, so a
beginner's version keeps the leg low and pitches the trunk far forward. Standing still on one foot, dancers sway about
as much as anyone; their advantage is a clean stop after a spin.
